TWELFTH REPORT,l,OF,THE &lt;span class=&quot;hitHighlite&quot;&gt;WORKING&lt;/span&gt; &lt;span class=&quot;hitHighlite&quot;&gt;MEN&rsquo;S&lt;/span&gt; &lt;span class=&quot;hitHighlite&quot;&gt;COLLEGE&lt;/span&gt;.,A S some time lias elapsed since the issue (1876) of their last Report, the Council think it well to give those who take an interest in their work some account of the present condition and prospects of the College.,In October, 1879, the College completed the twenty-fifth year of its existence. Looking back over that period, the Council are able to assure their friends that, though their work has had varying success, there has been no change in the principles that govern it. They have tried to keep constantly in view the original purposes of the founders, to put within reach of the working men of London the means of ob&not; taining a liberal education, and to unite in this work men of different classes.,The past twenty-five years have seen the growth of many institutions of kindred aims, with the work of which the Council fully sympathise, but they feel that they may justly claim that the College is the most conspicuous example of the advantages to be gained by making places of education for working men the centres of a common life, gathering round the work of learning and teaching.,The changes of a quarter of a century have not lessened the needs which brought the College into existence. On the contrary, the spread of elementary education con&not; sequent upon the Act of 1870 has increased and is increasing the field of its work, and the experience of its past years shows, the Council think, enough of valuable result to be an encouragement to them to continue their efforts. Many thousands of students have passed through the College, and of them not a few have remained attached to it for many years. From among them have come a body of earnest co-workers, whose plea&not; sure it has been to devote their labour to making the College useful to their successors. The work of the Executive Committee, with whom rests the general management and the arrangements of classes and lectures, is in great part carried on by former students, as also is the important and onerous work of the Finance Committee. The list of classes shows how much help in the actual teaching is given by those who first entered the
College as learners. With all this the fraternal and corporate spirit which knit to&not; gether the first generation of teachers and students has remained a characteristic of the College : the traditions of its early years have heen maintained ; it has heen to a large number of men a common point of attraction, a centre of social life amidst the isolating vastness of London, drawing to itself a body of students who find within its walls the sympathy and help that spring from common interests and united work.,But while trying to give the College this homelike character, the Council do not forget that the success of their work must primarily depend upon the efficiency of the actual teaching, and they are glad to be able to report that they can still find able men, members of the Universities and others, to undertake the various classes. Few of the earliest teachers or managers of the College are still among the acting members of the Council, but it has been recruited from time to time by the accession of younger volunteers, through whom the College may be said to keep up, in a newer generation, its old connection with the Universities. It still finds among Oxford and Cambridge men coming to London for professional work, some who are sufficiently interested in its objects to devote their evenings to regular class teaching.,To turn to the detail of its work, and particularly to that of the past few years, the College seems to have felt, as have so many other institutions, the depression of the times. The total number of class entries in the autumn terms of 187G, 1877, and 1878, were respectively 290, 380, and 343. The entries for the present year show a marked improvement. In October, 1879, the number was 520, being a larger number than at any time since the foundation of the College.,The occupations of the students may be seen from the annexed table {p. 10) from which it appears that some 36 per cent, are men working at manual trades; about an equal number clerks, and the remainder of various occupations, assistants in shops, lithographers, decorators, teachers, &amp;amp;c. It will thus be seen that the expression &ldquo;workingmen&rdquo; is understood in the largest sense, in accordance with the intentions of the founders. The Science classes have grown largely of late years, and the Council cannot but mention, as the chief cause of this success, the zealous devotion of their colleague, Mr. Dunman, to the work.

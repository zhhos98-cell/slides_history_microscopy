# External technical genealogy 01 — microscopic relocation, 1853–c.1913

Date: 2026-09-08  
Branch: `research/finder-slide-localization-tech`  
Status: **BOUNDED EXTERNAL TECHNICAL PASS COMPLETE**

## Result in one sentence

The nineteenth-century history of the microscope finder is not a linear story from Maltwood outward. By 1853–1856 microscopists were already testing several competing ways of making an exact microscopic locality recoverable; the decisive difference among them was **where the reference frame lived** and therefore what happened to the address when the slide, stage, microscope, magnification, observer, or collection changed.

A useful operational chain for the whole project is:

`field/object -> stabilised reference frame -> recorded address or material mark -> displacement -> reconstruction of locality -> same field/object`

The historical question is therefore not simply who invented a finder. It is how a fleeting microscopic view was turned into a persistent address.

---

## 1. Before Maltwood: a crowded problem space, 1853–1856

### Tyrrel, Amyot, Wright and Okeden

By the early 1850s the *Quarterly Journal of Microscopical Science* was already reporting multiple finder/indicator schemes. Bailey's 1855 survey retrospectively grouped Tyrrel, Amyot/Aymot, Wright and Okeden as attempts to solve a common problem: once an interesting microscopic object had been found, how could its locality be recovered later?

These schemes did not all solve the same version of the problem. Some were effectively tied to the geometry of an individual microscope or stage; others tried to externalise the reference. This distinction matters more than the device names.

Okeden is especially useful because later nineteenth-century descriptions make the coordinate architecture clear: two graduated scales controlled determinate vertical and horizontal stage positions. Carpenter subsequently connected this kind of system to a standardisation problem. If makers agreed on common graduation, distant observers could in principle exchange numerical references rather than merely exchange slides. The historical analogy to standard slide dimensions and universal fittings is explicit enough to treat cross-instrument addressability as a contemporary engineering ambition, not a retrospective analytical imposition.

**Mechanism family:** instrument/stage-bound coordinate frame.  
**Historical issue exposed:** can a numerical address remain valid after the instrument changes?

### Jacob Whitman Bailey, 1855: portability becomes explicit

Bailey's *On a Universal Indicator for Microscopes* is the strongest early source in this pass. His intervention was not simply another convenience for moving a stage back to a previous setting. He criticised arrangements that remained useful only on the instrument for which they had been made and designed an indicator whose spatial record could be detached from the original microscope and observer.

That makes the Universal Indicator a direct source for three project concepts:

1. **correspondence portability** — a recorded locality should be usable by a distant microscopist;
2. **observer independence** — the address should remain intelligible after the original observer is absent;
3. **collection persistence** — a collection becomes more scientifically reusable when exact individual objects can be found again rather than merely when slides survive.

Harvard's surviving Bailey collection closes this materially. The Farlow Herbarium identifies slides by standard reference lines, orientation arrows/numbers and other features characteristic of Bailey's Universal Indicator. The historical catalogue records individual contents together with marks and measurements for finding specimens. Bailey's failing health and his attempt to organise the collection for future researchers make the persistence problem unusually visible.

**Mechanism family:** transferable external indicator/carrier.  
**Project status:** central S-level historical case.

### Bridgman, 1855: do not calculate the address — mark the target

A second solution was already present at the same date. W. K. Bridgman's method used a diamond to scratch a circle around the desired locality on the cover glass; later microscopy manuals also describe varnish/black-ring variants.

This is a different epistemic architecture. A coordinate system stores a relation between target and external frame. Direct marking instead changes the preparation so that the reference travels with it.

**Mechanism family:** direct material target marking.  
**Historical issue exposed:** portability can be purchased by modifying the material object rather than by standardising coordinates.

The coexistence of Bailey and Bridgman in the mid-1850s is important. Coordinate/indicator systems and material marking were alternatives from near the beginning, not stages in a simple progression from primitive marking to sophisticated numerical location.

## 2. Maltwood, 1858: the substitution reference slide

Thomas Maltwood's Finder supplied a particularly durable solution. The user centered the desired object, removed the preparation without changing the stage position, substituted the finder slide, and read the numbered grid now lying under the optical axis. To return, the procedure was reversed.

Its technical importance is the relocation of the reference frame onto a standardised *surrogate slide*. The target slide itself does not need a permanently engraved grid. Instead, reproducible contact with a stop/edge/orientation relation lets an external grid stand in for the preparation.

This explains both the strength and the fragility of the system:

- the **address can travel** as a compact grid reference;
- it does not require the original mechanical-stage readings;
- but it depends on repeatable slide orientation, stable edge geometry, consistent placement against a stop, and an agreed grid convention.

Nineteenth-century manuals eventually described Maltwood's Finder as useful for interchange of notes between microscopists at a distance and for ensuring identity of objects examined at different times. This is the same portability problem Bailey had already stated, solved through a different reference architecture.

**Mechanism family:** substitution reference slide/grid.  
**Project status:** central S-level device family.

### Date guard

The device is conventionally associated with Maltwood's 1858 description, while some bibliographic records assign 1859 to the Transactions publication. The project should distinguish description/presentation/publication dates when exact priority matters rather than forcing one date globally.

## 3. A rare transition object: Bailey Indicator -> Maltwood Finder

The strongest new evidence in the external pass is not another gadget. It is an **object-level re-addressing chain** in the Harvard Jacob Whitman Bailey Collection.

The online catalogue contains slides whose records explicitly combine Bailey-system and later Maltwood references. Examples include entries described as:

- recorded by J. W. Bailey on an indicator/card and later recorded by Loring W. Bailey with Maltwood's Finder;
- `by indicator card A, by Maltwood's Finder, L.W.B.`;
- `by indicator, by Maltwood's Finder`.

This matters because it shows a historical collection passing between address regimes. The same microscopic material did not merely survive from the 1850s into a later collection. Its **spatial key was translated**.

That gives the project a much stronger unit of analysis than device succession:

`microscopic object -> Bailey indicator address -> later Maltwood re-address -> catalogue -> surviving slide collection`

The history of finders can therefore be written as a history of **re-addressing and reference-frame migration**.

This chain should be treated cautiously: the catalogue demonstrates explicit dual-address entries, not that every Bailey slide was converted to Maltwood.

## 4. The 1880s: differentiation rather than convergence

By the 1880s the term `finder` covered increasingly different operations. This is evidence against treating finder history as the diffusion of one standard device.

### William Webb, 1880

Webb's improved finder refined the substitution-grid family by increasing spatial subdivision/resolution. Later technical descriptions report a much finer ruled system than Maltwood. Before using exact subdivision numbers in publication prose, the original 1880 paper should be checked directly.

**Family:** substitution grid.  
**Design pressure:** finer address resolution.

### Royston-Pigott, 1881

The General/Transfer Finder placed the reference in the optical/eyepiece system. Its important operation was transfer of a locality through changes of optical power/objective rather than necessarily transport of an address to a completely different microscope.

**Family:** optical/eyepiece transfer reference.  
**Design pressure:** preserve locality across magnification/optical reconfiguration.

### Smith, 1881

Smith's Object Plate and Finder used a movable carrier/stage geometry read through rotational and linear settings. It belongs with coordinate systems in which the address remains closely coupled to apparatus geometry.

**Family:** instrument/carrier-bound coordinate reference.  
**Design pressure:** reproducible mechanical setting.

### Francotte, 1884–1885

Francotte's finder/marker returned to the material-marking solution: a centered object was surrounded by a small ring applied/pressed onto the cover glass by a specialised marking point.

**Family:** direct material marking.  
**Design pressure:** make the target visually recoverable without translating a numerical address.

### Klönne & Müller, 1886: bacteria finder

The Royal Microscopical Society apparatus record confirms a specialised Bacteria Finder. A follow-up description says the frame allowed the slide to move directly on the stage surface so that the Abbe condenser could approach closely; it also discusses reading the scale of a circular slot and suggests adding a vernier.

This is enough to classify the device provisionally as a **frame/instrument-bound coordinate apparatus**, but not enough to reconstruct the full geometry. The original p.127 description/figure remains a bounded technical residual.

The bacteriological specialisation is itself significant: exact relocation became important in disciplines where sparse or difficult microscopic targets had to be revisited under high optical demands.

## 5. Klein and Winkel, 1888: selection, marking and the individual form

The repository's earlier Klein/Winkel lead can now be upgraded.

An 1889 *Hedwigia* review of Ludwig Klein's 1888 second paper on permanent freshwater-algae preparations explicitly says the paper recommends **Winkel's Markirapparat** and gives instructions for selecting individual forms from mixtures. This closes the historical association at source-review level.

A later Zeiss/Winkel catalogue closes the mechanism of the apparatus family: the `Objektmarkierapparat (W)` used an adjustable diamond point to scratch a circle on the cover glass around a desired preparation locality specifically so that the place could later be found again. The same catalogue separately sells a Maltwood Finder and explicitly describes its function as the recovery of particular preparation localities.

This juxtaposition is analytically valuable. In one catalogue, two different relocation architectures coexist:

- **Maltwood:** translate the locality into an external grid address;
- **Winkel:** inscribe the locality materially onto the preparation.

For Klein, the connection to `Aussuchen einzelner Formen aus Gemischen` adds a further step:

`mixed material -> selection of individual form -> marking -> future return`

The individual microscopic form becomes a durable research object partly because its location has been stabilised.

### Evidence guard

The later Zeiss/Winkel catalogue proves the working principle of the Winkel marking-apparatus family. It does **not** prove that every mechanical detail of the apparatus Klein recommended in 1888 was identical to the later catalogue model. Klein's exact 1888 primary wording remains a legitimate exact-source residual, not a reason for further broad discovery.

## 6. Grunow revisited: coordinates are part of a cross-media work system

The external finder genealogy changes the interpretation of the repository's Grunow evidence.

Grunow is not merely an isolated example of somebody writing stage numbers. His surviving addressing architecture belongs to the same nineteenth-century problem space:

`sample no. -> preparation variant -> slide/reference alignment -> paired coordinate -> exact valve -> drawing -> work-status notation`

Sample 1363b links `12.9 / 32.9` to an exact diatom valve and drawing. Sample 3073b preserves several valve coordinates and uses circling to show specimens already drawn. Older preparations could carry pasted scales; later localisation instructions aligned the upper-left slide corner to a millimetre scale whose zero corresponded to the microscope focus.

The important difference from Maltwood is that Grunow's address is embedded in a larger documentary and drawing system. It does not merely return the observer to a field. It connects that field to a specimen identity and to the production history of an image.

This makes Grunow the strongest case for **cross-media relocation** in the existing repository.

## 7. What finder history is *not*

### Not collection-level retrieval

A cabinet number, maker label, series number or accession number tells the user which slide to retrieve. It does not necessarily tell the user where on that slide a microscopic object lies.

### Not simply a within-slide index

Elcock's Type Slides give named specimens spatial positions on one mounted preparation. This is a close comparator but remains a controlled arrangement of specimens rather than an arbitrary exact-field relocation system.

### Not the same as a test object

Nobert, Grayson, Möller, Norman and related test preparations make an optical challenge or comparison repeatable. Their problem is `repeat the test`, not primarily `return to this arbitrary microscopic locality`.

Meegan Kennedy's current `Test Objects in Victorian Microscopy` work makes this boundary especially important historiographically. The finder project should use test objects as comparators only.

## 8. The historical taxonomy after the external pass

### Family 1 — instrument/stage-bound coordinates

Examples: Wright; Okeden; Smith; some later mechanical stages; provisionally Klönne & Müller; Grunow in part.

Reference frame lives in the apparatus geometry.

Core question: **does the address survive recalibration or a change of microscope?**

### Family 2 — transferable external indicator/carrier

Examples: Tyrrel/Amyot routes; Bailey Universal Indicator.

Reference frame is externalised so it can accompany the record/slide beyond the originating microscope.

Core question: **can a correspondent reconstruct the same locality?**

### Family 3 — substitution reference slide/grid

Examples: Maltwood; Webb; later England Finder.

A standardized reference slide is substituted for the preparation at the same stage position.

Core question: **can stable slide geometry convert a field into a portable address?**

### Family 4 — direct material marking

Examples: Bridgman; Francotte; Winkel; Gage; perhaps Fuess, pending exact identification.

Reference is inscribed directly on/around the preparation locality.

Core question: **can the preparation carry its own spatial key?**

### Family 5 — optical/eyepiece transfer

Example: Royston-Pigott.

Reference lives in the optical system.

Core question: **can the same locality survive a change in optical power/configuration?**

These are not necessarily evolutionary stages. They coexist because they optimise different combinations of precision, portability, convenience, permanence and risk to the specimen.

## 9. Modern afterlife clarifies the engineering problem

Modern palynology and micropalaeontology continue to use finder references, especially the England Finder, because ordinary mechanical-stage coordinates are normally microscope-specific. Modern technical literature explicitly frames the goal as relocating a point of interest on another microscope.

The technical chain now extends into digital collections:

`Maltwood-type physical grid -> England Finder address -> software coordinate conversion -> whole-slide-image x/y -> digital specimen annotation/identifier`

This continuity should not be used to impose present-day digital ontology on Victorian actors. It is useful because it makes the persistent engineering variables visible: orientation, slide geometry, calibration, reference-frame independence and coordinate translation.

## 11. External-pass conclusion

The external pass closes the principal technical question strongly enough for synthesis. The project no longer needs a generic hunt for more named finders.

The central claim to carry forward is:

> Nineteenth-century microscopic relocation developed as a family of competing reference architectures. Their difference lay in where spatial reference was stabilised — in the instrument, in an external indicator, in a substitutable grid slide, in the optical system, or materially on the preparation itself. These architectures determined whether an observed microscopic object could remain the *same locatable object* after the slide, instrument, observer, medium, or collection changed.

The strongest historical evidence is now Bailey 1855 + surviving Bailey collection + later Maltwood re-addressing, with Grunow as the strongest repository-native cross-media comparator and Klein/Winkel as the strongest selection/marking route.

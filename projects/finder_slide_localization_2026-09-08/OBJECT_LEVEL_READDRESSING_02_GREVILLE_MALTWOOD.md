# Object-level re-addressing 02 — Greville, Maltwood, calibration, and modern type recovery

## Why this case matters

The Bailey collection demonstrates migration between two nineteenth-century reference systems: Bailey indicator addresses and later Maltwood references. The Greville/Gregory material at the Natural History Museum and its modern re-study supplies a second, different kind of evidence: a historical finder address can survive for more than a century, but only through **calibration, interpretation and re-addressing**.

This case therefore corrects an over-simple formulation of finder portability.

A finder address is not simply `portable` or `universal` by virtue of being numerical. Its portability is an **achievement maintained by geometry, edge conventions, manufactured equivalence, calibration and documentary context**.

The stronger project term after this pass is:

**calibrated portability**.

## 1. Danielidis & Mann 2002 — historical Maltwood addresses require empirical translation

### Source

D. B. Danielidis and D. G. Mann, “The systematics of *Seminavis* (Bacillariophyta): the lost identities of *Amphora angusta*, *A. ventricosa* and *A. macilenta*,” *European Journal of Phycology* 37 (2002): 429–448. DOI 10.1017/S0967026202003724.

### Historical material

The authors studied Gregory type material preserved in the Greville collection at the Natural History Museum, London.

Greville’s slide labels sometimes record the **Maltwood Finder coordinate of the specific specimen he drew**. A dot placed close to the written finder reference was also significant: once the slide had been moved to the relevant finder square, the dot indicated the position of the intended individual within that area.

On other preparations, finder references correspond to spots marked directly on the coverslip.

Thus the historical address could combine:

`Maltwood square -> local dot/spot -> exact individual -> published drawing`

### Calibration problem

Danielidis and Mann report that no Maltwood Finder was exactly identical to another in the material practice they encountered. They therefore translated the original historical references into the coordinate frame of the Maltwood Finder available in Edinburgh by an **empirical correction**:

`first/numerator value − 2`  
`second/denominator value + 10`

That transformation proved consistent and accurate across the relevant slides and individuals.

They also note that another investigator using the same Edinburgh finder obtained a consistent but not identical deviation because of a different locating convention and differences in the **physical dimensions of the slides, especially width**.

### Analytical result

This is perhaps the project’s clearest demonstration that `universal address` should not be treated as an all-or-nothing property.

The actual chain is:

`historical finder frame -> known systematic deviation -> empirical transformation -> modern finder frame -> exact historical individual`

The address survives because its reference frame can be **translated**.

### Evidence grade

**S — modern peer-reviewed re-use of historical object-level finder references with explicit calibration procedure.**

## 2. The coordinate is cross-media, not merely spatial

The 2002 study also reconstructs a relation among:

- historical slide;
- Maltwood coordinate;
- local dot/spot;
- exact diatom individual;
- Greville’s drawing;
- modern light micrograph;
- modern type designation and taxonomic argument.

A finder reference therefore acts as a bridge between material specimen and image/document history.

This closely parallels the repository-native Grunow chain:

`sample -> preparation -> coordinate -> valve -> drawing`

but with a much longer temporal span and a modern re-identification episode.

## 3. Williams et al. 2021 — finder addresses remain active in type nomenclature

### Source

David M. Williams and collaborators, “Notes on the diatom collection of the Natural History Museum, London (BM) II–IV. Some type specimens in the genus *Aulacodiscus* Ehrenberg and nomenclatural notes on *Thumia* Cleve ex F.B. Taylor,” *Phytotaxa* 480.2 (2021).

### Result

The paper uses Maltwood Finder coordinates preserved with Greville material to distinguish and recover particular historical individuals. Examples include:

- BM 2745, Maltwood Finder 22/12;
- BM 2818, Maltwood Finder 12/17.

The authors relate slide labels, Greville determinations, Rattray’s later labels, unpublished drawings and exact specimens to decide questions of holotype/epitype interpretation.

### Analytical result

The historical finder coordinate is not antiquarian metadata. It still participates in **current nomenclatural acts**.

The address transports not merely a visual field through time but an identity claim that can affect the legal/technical application of a taxonomic name.

### Evidence grade

**S/A — peer-reviewed modern taxonomic re-use of nineteenth-century finder addresses.**

## 4. Riaux-Gobin, Compère & Ector 2014 — direct marking without precise coordinates creates a different afterlife

### Source

Catherine Riaux-Gobin, Pierre Compère and Luc Ector, “Some *Cocconeis* species (Bacillariophyceae) originally described by William Gregory and Robert Kaye Greville from the Firth of Clyde and Loch Fyne (Scotland),” *Nova Hedwigia* 99.1–2 (2014): 171–192. DOI 10.1127/0029-5035/2014/0190.

### Historical material

Five Gregory/Greville `Arran57` slides preserve taxa marked by **diamond circles on the coverslip** and by labels. Unlike the better-addressed Greville slides, these preparations do not carry precise Maltwood coordinates; they offer only rough spatial positions through numbered circles.

The modern researchers report that the ringed specimens were not always easy to find. To support new x/y coordinates, they added a modern benchmark/crux as an origin on each slide.

### Analytical result

This is a controlled comparison between two relocation architectures:

`direct material circle -> rough locality -> difficult modern recovery`

versus

`finder coordinate + local mark -> calibrated numerical translation -> exact individual recovery`.

Direct marking travels with the preparation and is robust against instrument change, but its spatial resolution and future interpretability may be poorer unless it is paired with a coordinate convention or a well-defined origin.

### Evidence grade

**S — peer-reviewed modern examination of historically marked slides and explicit modern re-addressing.**

## 5. England Finder practice makes the geometry explicit

The Royal Botanic Garden Edinburgh’s current finder instructions explain why modern finder references are still accompanied by careful orientation conventions. England Finder accuracy is guaranteed relative to specified edges, and a finder coordinate can be transferred among microscopes only when those edge relations are reproduced.

This makes explicit what the Greville case demonstrates historically:

**the grid is not the whole reference system.**

The operative system is:

`grid + slide dimensions + chosen edges + orientation + stage relation + reading convention`.

A coordinate stripped from those relations can become ambiguous or systematically displaced.

## 6. Williams, Sims & Ashworth 2026 — exact-slide/exact-specimen links remain research infrastructure

### Source

David M. Williams, Pat A. Sims and Matt P. Ashworth, “Notes on the diatom collection of the Natural History Museum, London (BM) X: Roper’s species of *Biddulphia* (Bacillariophyta),” *Phytotaxa* 755.2 (8 May 2026): 121–153. DOI 10.11646/phytotaxa.755.2.1.

### Result

The paper reports the discovery and use of Freeman C. S. Roper’s original drawings. In most cases the drawings identify the **exact slide**, and sometimes the **exact specimen**, used in his nineteenth-century study. Those relations allow the authors to re-examine original material and revise taxonomic interpretations.

The case is not itself proof that every exact object was found by Maltwood. Its importance here is as a current continuation of the same problem:

`historical image/document -> exact slide/specimen address -> modern re-observation -> taxonomic revision`.

### Evidence grade

**S for present-day use of exact-slide/exact-specimen documentary links; comparator rather than a Maltwood-only case.**

## 7. Revised portability model

The earlier project language can now be sharpened.

### Too simple

`instrument-bound coordinate -> portable finder coordinate`

### Better

`instrument-bound coordinate -> nominally transferable finder frame -> calibrated cross-finder translation -> modern standardized finder/coordinate regime`

The historical variable is not portability alone but **how portability is produced and maintained**.

Relevant conditions include:

- standard slide dimensions;
- stable orientation;
- reproducible reference edges;
- stage stops/carriers;
- finder manufacture and scale placement;
- local dots/circles within a finder square;
- correction between non-identical finder instances;
- explicit coordinate conventions;
- later re-benchmarking when the original frame is incomplete.

This suggests the project’s governing variable should be described as:

**reference-frame portability under calibration**

or, more compactly,

**calibrated portability**.

## 8. Object-level temporal transport

The Greville case permits a stronger statement about `transport through time`.

The material slide survives from the nineteenth century, but the scientifically relevant unit is not merely the glass. What survives is a recoverable relation:

`historical observer -> exact microscopic individual -> spatial address -> drawing -> collection record -> modern observer`.

When the original finder is no longer available, that relation can still be reconstructed by calibrating one reference frame against another.

Thus re-addressing is not a secondary museum task. It is one of the operations by which a historical microscopic object **continues to be the same scientific object** for later research.

## 9. Relation to Bailey re-addressing

The branch now has two complementary forms of reference-frame migration:

### Bailey collection

`Bailey Universal Indicator -> later Maltwood Finder address`

A collection is re-described using a later reference architecture.

### Greville/Gregory collection

`historical Maltwood frame -> empirical correction -> later Maltwood / England-style coordinate practice`

A historical address is mathematically/empirically translated so that the exact individual can be recovered under a different physical reference instance.

Together they justify treating **re-addressing** as a historical process in its own right.

## 10. Writing payoff

The strongest article-level contrast is no longer simply:

`stage coordinate vs finder slide`.

It is:

> Where does the reference frame reside, what displacement is it designed to survive, and what work is required to keep that address meaningful when the original apparatus, observer or reference object disappears?

This formulation can connect the 1850s portability debate to surviving collections without turning the project into a complete history of every finder apparatus.

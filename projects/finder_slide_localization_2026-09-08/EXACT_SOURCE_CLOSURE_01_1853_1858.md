# Exact-source closure 01 — early finder portability, 1853–1858

Date: 2026-09-08  
Branch: `research/finder-slide-localization-tech`  
Status: **BOUNDED EXACT-SOURCE UPGRADE — BROAD DISCOVERY REMAINS CLOSED**

## Purpose

This note closes the most important early-Victorian source chain behind the project’s concept of `reference-frame portability`. It does not add another generic finder list. It tests whether nineteenth-century actors themselves formulated the problem as one of moving a microscopic address across microscopes, observers and correspondence networks.

The answer is now securely **yes**.

---

## 1. Jacob Whitman Bailey, 1855 — portability stated as the problem

### Source

Jacob Whitman Bailey, “On a Universal Indicator for Microscopes,” *American Journal of Science and Arts*, ser. 2, 20(58) (1855): 58–65, pl. 1. A British reprint appears in *Quarterly Journal of Microscopical Science* 4 (1856), beginning p. 55.

### Primary-source result

Bailey begins by reviewing earlier devices associated with Tyrrell, Amyot and Okeden. He praises their convenience but identifies a common defect: they remain tied to the particular instrument on which the position was registered. He calls this class of apparatus, in a deliberately provocative phrase, **“selfish contrivances.”**

Bailey’s stated objective is much broader. He wants a `Universal Indicator` that allows a slide to be sent to a **distant correspondent**, who can recover the same object without possessing the original microscope or finder. Once registered, the record and object are to become independent of the **original instrument and observer** and applicable to another microscope.

He explicitly connects this to:

- interchange of specimens among naturalists;
- correspondence at a distance;
- the future scientific utility of a registered collection;
- the ability to preserve access to individual microscopic objects after the original observer or apparatus is absent.

Bailey describes his engraved card as what may be considered a **transferable stage**.

### Analytical consequence

`Reference-frame portability` is not merely a retrospective analytical category. Bailey himself separates:

`instrument-bound location -> transferable recorded location`

and links that transition directly to correspondence and collection durability.

### Evidence grade

**S — direct primary statement of purpose and mechanism.**

### Guard

The American 1855 paper is the priority publication. The 1856 QJMS appearance and separate-print catalogue records should be treated as reprint/republication evidence, not as proof that the invention dates only from 1856.

## 2. Thomas Edward Amyot, 1856 — physical transport and address transport in one device

### Sources

Thomas Edward Amyot, “The Finder,” *Quarterly Journal of Microscopical Science*, old series 1: 302–303.  
Thomas Edward Amyot, “Finders and Indicators,” *Quarterly Journal of Microscopical Science*, old series 4: 151–153.

The later paper is signed `Thomas Edward Amyot` and responds directly to Bailey’s criticism of non-transferable finders.

### Primary-source result

Amyot distinguishes Wright’s arrangement, which he regards as satisfactory only for an individual microscope, from his own and Tyrrell’s devices. He adopts Bailey’s language of a finder being non-transferable or “selfish,” but argues that Tyrrell’s and his own instruments were not simply fixed apparatus.

His strongest statement is unexpectedly logistical: the devices also formed **packing cases for slides**. Wrapped in paper and enclosed in a **twopenny stamped envelope**, they could carry slides safely to a correspondent. Amyot therefore places in the same paragraph:

- a physical carrier for the microscope slide;
- postal circulation;
- cross-microscope use;
- the problem of retaining a location reference after transfer.

Amyot says his own device was suited to any microscope and was in that sense a `Universal Indicator`, though he acknowledges practical shortcomings. His subsequent experiments with crossed scales on card failed because he could not ensure a uniform position on every movable stage; he identifies Bailey’s removable central piece as the missing geometrical solution.

### Analytical consequence

Amyot provides the cleanest early bridge between the larger `Transporting Slides` question and the finder satellite:

`transporting the glass` and `transporting the microscopic locality` were already technically entangled.

The historical problem is therefore not merely metaphorically about mobility. A finder could literally be part of the packing/correspondence infrastructure through which the slide moved.

### Evidence grade

**S — direct primary technical and postal statement.**

### Guard

Amyot’s claim that his apparatus was universal is an actor claim. It should be assessed against the actual geometrical and stage constraints he himself discusses rather than repeated as a modern verdict.

## 3. Microscopical Society Committee on Finders, 1856 — relocation becomes a collective design problem

### Sources

Proceedings of the Microscopical Society, meeting of **25 June 1856**, records: `A Report from the Committee on Finders was read.`

Arthur M. Edwards, “On Finders for Microscopes,” *Transactions of the Microscopical Society of London*, old/new-series volume context 1857, pp. 200–205, reproduces the committee’s design criteria and develops two indicator designs.

### Committee criteria

According to Edwards, an efficient finder should:

1. work with **any microscope**, whether or not it has stage movements, without preventing use of those movements;
2. require no new label, mark or index to be added to the specimen slide;
3. avoid repeated removal of slide/finder for each act of registering or finding;
4. have divisions that are easy to read;
5. remain usable with the microscope inclined;
6. be cheap and mechanically simple enough for construction without exceptional skill.

### Standard slide geometry

Edwards then makes a crucial design choice: because the slides most commonly used followed the Microscopical Society’s recommended **3 × 1 inch** dimensions, he made his indicator to that size.

This establishes a source-level relation between:

`standard slide geometry -> finder geometry -> potential interoperability`

rather than leaving that relation as a later inference.

### Operational friction

Edwards also treats registration as a practical workflow problem. A microscopist may encounter an unusual diatom valve, crystal or cell unexpectedly. A system that forces the observer to lose the object, remove the slide, insert a finder, adjust scales and then recover the object imposes enough friction that useful registrations may simply not be made.

Thus the committee was optimizing not only theoretical accuracy but:

- universality;
- non-destructive registration;
- low interruption of observation;
- legibility;
- posture/instrument orientation;
- cost and manufacturability.

### Analytical consequence

By June 1856 microscopic relocation was already a **society-level design problem**. The relevant historical unit is therefore not a sequence of isolated inventions but a collective attempt to define the requirements of a portable and usable address system.

### Evidence grade

**S for the committee’s existence and Edwards’s reproduced requirements; A/S for the standardized slide-size design relation.**

## 4. Okeden through Carpenter — common graduation as cross-instrument infrastructure

### Source

William B. Carpenter, *The Microscope and Its Revelations*, later 1860s editions, discussion of object-finders and the Okeden plan.

### Mechanism

The Okeden arrangement uses two graduated stage scales so that a slide and stage can be returned to recorded numerical positions. Carpenter regards the scheme as straightforward enough that microscopes with stage movement could routinely carry such scales.

### Standardisation proposal

Carpenter then makes the decisive infrastructural move. If different makers adopted a **common system of graduation**, analogous in spirit to the already-standard 3 × 1 inch object slide and compatible standards elsewhere in microscopy, observers at a distance could exchange numerical references and recover one another’s objects on stages graduated by the same method.

### Analytical consequence

This is a nineteenth-century formulation of what the project calls:

`cross-instrument addressability through standards`

It also shows a route alternative to Bailey and Maltwood. Instead of making a transferable indicator or substitute grid slide, one could make the **instrumental coordinate systems themselves interoperable**.

### Evidence grade

**S for Carpenter’s published proposal; A for its use as evidence of the wider meaning of Okeden’s architecture.**

### Attribution guard

Do not attribute Carpenter’s proposal for makers to agree on a common graduation directly to Okeden unless Okeden’s own paper is separately shown to make that claim. Current exact-source evidence securely attributes the standardisation synthesis to **Carpenter**.

## 5. Thomas Maltwood, 1858 — exact publication anchor

### Source

Thomas Maltwood, “On a Finder for Registering the Position of Microscopic Objects,” *Transactions of the Microscopical Society of London* 6 (1858): 59–62.

### Chronology result

The original Transactions volume and contemporary bibliographic trail support **1858** as the publication year of the named Maltwood finder paper. Some later taxonomic literature cites 1859; that should be treated as a downstream bibliographic discrepancy unless a specific reissue is being discussed.

### Architectural significance

Maltwood’s key shift is to use a **substitute reference slide** bearing a photographic grid. The specimen slide is placed reproducibly, the desired object centered, and the specimen slide replaced by the finder without altering the optical/stage relation. The grid value at the optical axis becomes the recorded address.

The architecture therefore relocates the reference frame away from a permanently graduated microscope stage and into a standardized substitute object whose relation to slide edges/stage stop can be reproduced.

### Evidence grade

**S for paper identity, year and core substitute-grid architecture.**

### Guard

Do not describe Maltwood as the first solution to microscopic relocation. By 1858 the problem already had several named British and American solutions and had been considered by a Microscopical Society committee.

## 6. Revised early chronology

The early history should now be written as a debate over portability rather than as a single-inventor sequence:

- **1853–1855:** Tyrrell, Amyot, Wright, Okeden and related designs explore carriers, stage references and registering devices.
- **1855:** Bailey makes instrument/observer independence and distant correspondence an explicit design objective; Bridgman represents the competing direct-marking route.
- **1856:** Amyot answers Bailey through a combined finder/packing/correspondence logic; Microscopical Society Committee on Finders formalises design requirements.
- **1857:** Edwards publishes the committee criteria and ties his indicator geometry to the Society’s 3 × 1 inch slide standard.
- **1858:** Maltwood publishes the substitute-grid finder architecture.

This is a short but dense formation episode.

## 7. Stronger historical formulation after exact-source closure

The project can now say, with source-level support:

> Mid-Victorian microscopists did not merely invent devices for refinding objects. They debated where a spatial reference should reside so that a microscopic locality could survive the removal, packing, posting and reinsertion of a slide, a change of microscope, and a change of observer.

The central distinction was already articulated by participants:

`particular-instrument finder` vs `transferable / universal indicator`.

The committee stage then converted that distinction into engineering requirements.

---

## 8. Implication for the larger Slides project

The early finder material makes one larger distinction unusually concrete:

**A slide can be physically portable while its microscopic locality is not.**

Standard dimensions, carriers and postal packaging can move the preparation. A finder/indicator/coordinate convention must additionally preserve the relation that allows another observer to recover a particular microscopic object.

This is the strongest bridge between the finder satellite and any future `Transporting Slides` argument.

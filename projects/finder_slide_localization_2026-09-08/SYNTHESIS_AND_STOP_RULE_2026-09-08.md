# Finder / microscopic relocation — synthesis and stop rule

Date: 2026-09-08  
Branch: `research/finder-slide-localization-tech`  
Status: **SYNTHESIS READY — BROAD DISCOVERY CLOSED**

## 1. What this satellite project now establishes

The nineteenth-century `finder` is best treated not as one accessory with a single line of descent but as a family of technologies for making **microscopic locality persistent**.

The shared operational problem was:

> an observer has centered or identified a microscopic field/object; what must be stabilised so that the same locality can be reconstructed after something changes?

The possible changes include:

- time;
- slide removal/reinsertion;
- stage position;
- microscope;
- objective/magnification;
- observer;
- correspondence partner;
- drawing/documentary medium;
- collection/custody regime.

The crucial historical variable is therefore **reference-frame portability**.

A location is not automatically portable because a slide is portable. The slide may travel while the spatial relation that made one object identifiable disappears.

---

## 2. Five nineteenth-century solutions

### I. Put the address in the instrument/stage

Examples: Wright, Okeden, Smith, mechanical-stage verniers; partly Grunow; provisionally Klönne & Müller.

The microscope or carrier supplies a calibrated coordinate frame.

**Advantage:** precise numerical return on the same controlled apparatus.  
**Weakness:** change the stage, zero, orientation or instrument and the address may cease to mean the same place.

This is the sharpest case of an **instrument-bound address**.

### II. Externalise the stage as a transferable indicator

Central example: Jacob Whitman Bailey's Universal Indicator, 1855.

Bailey made portability itself explicit. The record should not be useful only to the owner of one microscope; it should support correspondence, interchange and future use of a collection.

The address is therefore detached from the original instrument and made reconstructible through a transferable reference convention.

This is the strongest early case for a **correspondence-bound / inter-observer address**.

### III. Substitute a standard reference slide

Central example: Maltwood's Finder, 1858.

The object slide is positioned reproducibly, removed, and replaced with a numbered grid slide. The reference read at the same optical axis becomes the address.

Here portability depends not on common stage graduations but on **reproducible slide geometry and orientation**.

This is the ancestor of the England Finder family and the cleanest case for a **slide-mediated portable address**.

### IV. Put the address directly on the preparation

Examples: Bridgman 1855; Francotte; Winkel; Gage; related marking systems.

Rather than encode a coordinate, the microscopist scratches or otherwise marks a small ring/locality on or around the preparation.

The spatial key travels with the slide itself.

This is a **materially embedded address**.

### V. Put the address in the optical system

Example: Royston-Pigott's General/Transfer Finder.

The reference is used to transfer locality across optical changes such as objectives/magnification.

This is an **optically bound address**, solving a different displacement problem from long-distance correspondence.

## 3. The central historical case is now Bailey, not Maltwood alone

Maltwood remains the most durable named finder architecture, but Bailey gives the project the strongest historical problem statement.

Three levels of evidence now converge:

1. **1855 publication:** Bailey formulates a Universal Indicator against finders that remain tied to particular instruments and links location to correspondence/interchange.
2. **surviving objects:** Harvard identifies Universal Indicator reference lines, orientation arrows/numbers on Bailey slides.
3. **collection documentation:** the catalogue preserves marks/measurements used to find individual specimens and later includes explicit Maltwood references.

Most important, some Harvard catalogue entries combine the two systems:

`Bailey indicator address -> later Maltwood Finder address`

This is a documented case of **reference-frame migration**.

The historiographical payoff is large: finder history can be studied through a collection being re-addressed rather than through a list of successive inventions.

---

## 4. Grunow is the strongest bridge back into the core Slides repository

Grunow's system demonstrates a different extension of relocation:

`sample no. -> preparation variant -> coordinate -> exact valve -> drawing -> work-status record`

The address is not merely a route back to a field. It binds an exact microscopic locality to image production and documentary history.

That makes the Grunow case especially important for the repository's larger concept of `addressability`:

- collection addresses find slides;
- slide/series addresses identify preparations;
- finder/coordinate systems recover localities;
- cross-media references bind those localities to drawings, publication, catalogues or later records.

The finder satellite therefore supplies the finest-grained spatial mechanism in the larger slide-address hierarchy.

---

## 5. Klein/Winkel closes the selection-marking route

The earlier internal lead can now be stated more securely.

A contemporary 1889 review says Klein's 1888 paper recommends Winkel's marking apparatus and gives instructions for selecting individual forms from mixtures. Later Winkel/Zeiss catalogue evidence establishes the apparatus-family operation: a diamond scratches a circle on the coverslip around a desired preparation locality so that it can later be found again.

This produces an analytically useful sequence:

`mixed microscopic material -> selected individual form -> material mark -> later relocation`

The history of relocation is therefore also a history of **individualisation**. An object can become scientifically persistent because its place among many similar microscopic forms has been made recoverable.

Guard: later catalogue mechanism should not be used to assert that every mechanical detail of Klein's 1888 apparatus was identical.

## 6. The project-level argument

### Compact formulation

> The microscope slide made microscopic material transportable; finder and marking systems made microscopic **locality** transportable.

### Stronger formulation

> Nineteenth-century microscopists developed multiple reference architectures for converting a transient microscopic field into a persistent address. These systems placed spatial reference in different locations — the instrument, an external indicator, a substitute grid slide, the optical train, or the preparation itself — and therefore differed in what could survive displacement across instruments, observers, magnifications, media and collections.

### Relation to the repository's `double fixing`

The slide's first fixing stabilises material on glass.

Relocation adds another operation: it stabilises a **recoverable relation** between that material and a particular locality.

This is more specific than labels/catalogue numbers and more operational than a generic claim about documentation. A microscopic object becomes durable as an object of inquiry when another act of observation can reconstruct its place.

## 7. What this can support in future writing

The finder material can function at several scales without becoming a separate giant project.

### As an article/project

Possible working formulation:

**Portable Microscopic Addresses: Finders, Marking, and the Problem of Relocation in Nineteenth-Century Microscopy**

Core cases:

- Bailey 1855 + Harvard collection;
- Maltwood 1858;
- Bailey-to-Maltwood re-addressing;
- one direct-marking line (Bridgman -> Winkel);
- Grunow as cross-media relocation;
- modern England Finder as brief afterlife, not core chronology.

### As a section of a larger `Transporting Slides` project

Finder history answers a specific question that physical circulation alone cannot:

`Can I transport your slide?` is not the same as `Can I recover what you saw?`

Standard slide dimensions solve part of physical/mechanical interoperability. Finder systems solve the finer problem of **observational interoperability**.

This is the strongest route by which the finder satellite can plug into an early-Victorian transportation/portability project without forcing all later material into the early period.

---

## 8. Historiographical ceiling

The project should not claim that microscope slides, Maltwood, Bailey, Victorian microscopy or finder devices are unstudied.

Existing scholarship occupies:

- slide materiality/epistemology;
- circulation and postal microscopy;
- standards/interoperability;
- embodied observation;
- early American medical microscopy;
- Bailey/protistology;
- test objects;
- museum/collector histories of instruments and slides.

The bounded gap is narrower:

**No dedicated contemporary HPS synthesis was recovered in this pass that organizes the history around exact microscopic relocation, competing reference frames, portability and re-addressing across multiple device families.**

This is a search result and framing hypothesis, not a universal negative.

## 9. Residuals that remain genuinely open

These residuals are now small enough that they should not keep broad discovery active.

### R1 — Klönne & Müller 1886, original p.127

Need only if exact mechanism becomes important. Current evidence confirms the Bacteria Finder and a movable frame/circular-slot scale architecture but not enough to reconstruct every coordinate relation.

### R2 — Fuess Object Finder 1895, p.587

Indexed identity is secure; exact mechanism unresolved. A surviving Fuess object marker with diamond tip is not enough to equate the two.

### R3 — Klein 1888 II full primary text

Would refine exactly how Klein integrated selection and Winkel marking. The historical link is already strong enough through the 1889 review; this is an exact-source upgrade, not a discovery gap.

### R4 — exact Maltwood 1858/1859 publication chronology

Resolve only if priority/publication dating becomes important in writing.

### R5 — an unlocated modern HPS treatment centered specifically on relocation

If one appears, revise the historiographical positioning rather than broaden device discovery.

---

## 10. Hard stop rule

**Broad finder discovery is closed.**

Do not continue general searches for more nineteenth-century finder names, apparatus catalogues, museum examples or modern practitioner pages merely to enlarge the list.

Reopen this satellite only when a bounded source can change one of the following:

1. **mechanism classification** — e.g. original Klönne & Müller/Fuess text proves a different reference frame;
2. **chronology** — an exact source materially changes the pre-Maltwood sequence or Maltwood dating;
3. **object-level re-addressing** — another surviving collection shows translation between coordinate/indicator/finder regimes;
4. **Klein/Winkel exact mechanism** — primary 1888 text changes the selection/marking interpretation;
5. **historiographical overlap** — a modern academic study directly treats exact relocation/addressability/reference-frame portability as its core problem;
6. **writing need** — a draft chapter/article requires exact quotation, figure, patent/manufacturer detail or source image for one already bounded case.

Otherwise treat the project as **closed for discovery and open for synthesis/writing**.

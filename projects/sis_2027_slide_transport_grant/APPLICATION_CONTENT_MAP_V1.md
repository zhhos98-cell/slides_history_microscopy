## 1. Working title

**Keeping Preparations in Use: Microscope Slides, Cabinets, and Scientific Societies in Nineteenth-Century Britain**

Why this title works for SIS:
- says immediately what historical objects are involved;
- names cabinets/societies as the institutional mechanism rather than pretending the project is only about one microscope;
- foregrounds continued use rather than generic circulation;
- avoids theoretical vocabulary (`affordance`, `addressability`, `object identity`) before the empirical problem is clear.

Alternative if a shorter form field is required:
**Keeping Microscope Preparations in Use, c.1860–1900**

## 2. One-sentence project question

**How did nineteenth-century microscopical societies keep prepared microscope slides usable and retrievable as they passed between private collections, society cabinets and temporary borrowers?**

Do not expand this into a national survey.

## 3. Project statement architecture

Official SIS guidance asks the project statement to cover:
1. relevance to history of scientific instruments;
2. relation to prior research;
3. how/when research will be conducted;
4. curator/archive contact and specific historic instruments/objects to inspect.

Build the statement in that order unless the application form imposes another structure.

## A. Opening problem / historical scene

### Best empirical opening
Bolton Microscopical Society, summer 1881.

Controlled sequence:
- John Thomas Redmayne died in 1880;
- Mrs Redmayne placed his whole private slide collection in the hands of the Society President and Secretary so they could select the most suitable preparations;
- six dozen = 72 slides were chosen;
- another 48 diatom slides were added separately;
- the Society's collection was being re-arranged;
- contemporary reporting explicitly says Society slides were lent to members;
- the surviving archive contains a `Register of Slides Lent Out, May 1882–Jul 1887`.

Opening payoff:
The historical problem is not simply that slides moved. A private preparation had to become society property, acquire a place in a reorganised cabinet, leave under temporary custody, return, and become retrievable for another user.

### Avoid opening with
- Pattern Post;
- broken slides;
- abstract instrument identity;
- a literature review;
- grant logistics.

## B. Why this is history of scientific instruments

### Core factual basis
Thomas Davies (1873) states that an object viewed under the microscope normally had to be supported on a glass slide, almost always 3 × 1 inches; glass quality and thickness affected successful viewing, especially with higher powers.

Surviving nineteenth-century object ensembles reinforce that operating relation:
- Science Museum Group `1981-1827`: Powell & Lealand microscope outfit with microscope, objectives, attachments, accessories, **slides**, and correspondence;
- `Y2001.221`: J. B. Dancer compound microscope whose parts include **slides belonging with the microscope** and a drawer of eight glass slides;
- `1876-1172`: A. C. Cole & Son case of 24 prepared slides catalogued within `Microscopes`;
- Pritchard slide sets survive within scientific-instrument collections.

### Application sentence
**Prepared microscope slides were working components of the nineteenth-century microscope system: their dimensions, glass thickness, mounting and labels determined whether specimens could be observed, compared and reused with instruments by successive users.**

### Project-specific instrument question
The project asks how societies maintained those working components when:
- original carriers were incompatible and preparations were remounted;
- private preparations were selected into collective cabinets;
- individual slides passed into temporary member custody;
- returned preparations had to re-enter an institutional order.

### Guard
Do not assert `the cabinet itself was an instrument` or `every slide was a complete scientific instrument`.
Use `working instrument component`, `microscope outfit/system`, `prepared object used with the microscope`.

## C. Relation to previous research

### Literature to acknowledge, not compete with

**Lea Beiermann**
Postal microscopy as knowledge infrastructure; safe transmission; packing; breakage/mending; durability; liability; standardisation/interoperability.

**Simon Schaffer / Jutta Schickore / Alexi Baker**
Breakdown, maintenance, repair and instrument mobility as central to instrument history.

**Howard Lynk**
Richardson/Thiersch transparent injections; awkward German formats; trimming/remounting; transition toward standard 3 × 1 inch slides.

**Collection mobility / temporary-loan literature**
Temporary custody, outward loans and return are not themselves novel historical phenomena.

### Distinct empirical problem
**What happens when a repeatedly loaned collection object is also a working instrument component?**

The project shifts attention from outward circulation/transmission to:

`selection → cabinet organisation → temporary custody → return → re-entry into collective use`.

### Novelty ceiling
Do not claim:
- first history of moving slides;
- first evidence slides broke in transit;
- first history of slide repair;
- first argument that scientific instruments change in movement;
- first history of temporary collections.

Claim instead:
The research reconstructs ordinary scientific societies' transaction-level management of prepared microscope slides as collectively reusable working objects.

## F. Method

### Bolton method
Full-register capture first, interpretation second.

For each transaction where recorded:
- date out;
- borrower;
- slide number/address;
- subject/maker;
- quantity/unit of issue;
- return date/duration;
- repeated borrowing;
- annotations/non-return/damage;
- officer/custodian marks.

Then crosswalk diagnostic items to 1898 catalogue and relevant minutes/accounts.

### Oxford method
Event-led archival reading.

Start with public date hooks rather than reading boxes sequentially.
For each selected event ask:
- temporary or permanent custody?;
- catalogue/register address?;
- expenditure or cabinet work?;
- later historical catalogue entry?;
- surviving/current museum address?

### Comparative method
Compare small lendable Bolton cabinet with large RMS institutional collection and mature Quekett reference/lending cabinet.
Do not measure success by number of societies covered.

## J. Application risks and answers

### Risk 1 — `This is collection history, not instrument history.`
Answer with Davies + surviving microscope outfits + carrier/remounting/use relation.

### Risk 2 — `The research already seems done.`
Distinguish preliminary identification from new transaction-level/archive/object crosswalks. The lending register and internal RMS records remain unexamined.

### Risk 3 — `Why travel if much is digitised?`
Bolton's lending register and 1898 catalogue are not publicly digitised; Oxford internal archive/object crosswalk cannot be completed from public databases.

### Risk 4 — `Too broad for £750 / Bulletin.`
Funded empirical core is two archive clusters: Oxford + Bolton; Manchester is bounded and London self-funded. Bulletin output is deliberately narrower than the larger article.

### Risk 5 — `Where are the specific instruments?`
Close Oxford cabinet identifier + 2–5 slide IDs before submission. This is the only significant remaining application-readiness task.

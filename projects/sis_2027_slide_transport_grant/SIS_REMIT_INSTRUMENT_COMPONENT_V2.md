# SIS remit — instrument ecology and mobile preparations v2

Updated: 2026-09-13
Status: GRANT-FRAMING AUTHORITY / ARMSTRONG UPGRADE

## 1. Core framing unchanged

Do not argue abstractly that `a microscope slide is a scientific instrument`.

Use the historically narrower claim:

**Prepared microscope slides were detachable working components in nineteenth-century microscopy: they were made, selected, mounted and catalogued for repeated use with microscopes, and could pass between privately owned instruments and collectively managed society cabinets.**

The SIS relevance lies in these relations of use, compatibility, custody and maintenance.

## 2. Why the project is not merely collection history

The article/grant asks how a preparation moved between:

`glass/mount format`
↔ `microscope stage and optical use`
↔ `cabinet/tray position`
↔ `register/catalogue address`
↔ `temporary borrower`
↔ `return to collective use`.

A society cabinet was therefore not only a repository. Where lending is directly documented, it supplied prepared working objects to members who used microscopy outside the cabinet itself.

Bolton is the clearest archive for testing this transactionally because `FZ/14/3/1` is a dedicated **Register of Slides Lent Out, May 1882–Jul 1887**.

## 3. Bolton: private microscopes and society preparations in the same ecology

Contemporary reporting of the Bolton Microscopical Society's 19 Nov 1880 Conversazione places microscope stands/objectives and prepared material together.

Named instrument makers included Ross, Smith & Beck / R. & J. Beck, Swift, Crouch, Baker, Dancer, Parkes and local Banks Bros. The report remarks that these makers appeared to have found purchasers among Society members.

The same event displayed Redmayne's microscopes, photomicrographs, pathological preparations and diatoms shortly after his death.

The following summer the Society's slide collection was reported as being re-arranged after additions; Society slides were lent to members.

Safe analytical sequence:

`members possess/acquire microscopes`
→ `Society assembles and re-arranges prepared slides`
→ `preparations enter member custody`
→ `the same preparation can be examined on privately controlled instruments`
→ `register/cabinet procedures make later collective use possible`.

The archive must establish actual borrower/object/return cycles; do not infer individual microscope ownership from a borrower's name alone.

## 4. Manchester: instrument-maker participation is now a strong historical relation

The Manchester pre-PMS case no longer rests on a vague `possible maker link`.

Controlled sequence:
- 1866: a circulating-cabinet notice directs Manchester-area microscopists to `Mr. T. Armstrong, Deansgate, Manchester`;
- 1867–68: independent parish records place **Thomas Armstrong** on Deansgate;
- Science Museum Group identifies Thomas Armstrong as the actor managing the Deansgate family business and expanding it into spectacles and optical instruments before 1851;
- 1869: `T. Armstrong` exhibits prepared slides in the Manchester Circulating Microscopic Cabinet Society;
- Royal Microscopical Society fellows lists record **Thomas Armstrong**, Manchester/Urmston, as elected in **1871**;
- later contemporary lists style him `F.R.M.S.`;
- a surviving 1876 Redmayne microphotograph album is inscribed to Thomas Armstrong and independently places him in a microscopy exchange/material network.

Evidence grade:
`A++ / near-direct identity closure`.

No 1866/69 society source yet expands `T.` in full, so write:

**`the evidence makes Thomas Armstrong, the Deansgate optical-instrument maker and 1871 RMS fellow, the overwhelmingly likely identity of the Manchester cabinet contact and slide exhibitor`**

rather than `proved to be`.

## 5. Why Armstrong matters to SIS remit

This is more useful than finding a later Armstrong microscope advertisement.

The historical point is that circulating preparations were not a separate hobbyist world disconnected from instrument manufacture. In Manchester, the circulation/preparation milieu very strongly intersects with a named optical-instrument actor who also entered the Royal Microscopical Society.

The project can therefore state without ontological overreach:

**The practices under study linked instrument makers, microscope users, prepared objects and cabinet systems.**

That is a direct history-of-scientific-instruments problem even when a particular archival page is about a slide register rather than a microscope stand.

## 6. Richardson 1862: compatibility is an instrument-use problem

Richardson's remounting of imported German preparations matters because the original carrier could be incompatible with local cabinet/slide geometry.

The operation is not just specimen conservation:
- a prepared object is materially reformatted;
- the original glass can be discarded;
- the preparation is made compatible with another standard of handling/storage/use.

Prior scholarship already knows the episode; use it to establish historically that preparation identity could persist across carrier substitution.

## 7. Instrument exhibitions remain useful background, not the sole remit argument

Existing v1 anchors remain valid:
- 1862 International Exhibition routes place microscopes, object glasses, microscopic objects and cabinets within the same Smith, Beck & Beck display ecology;
- 1876 Special Loan Collection of Scientific Apparatus lists microscopes and collections of microscopic preparations in the same apparatus/teaching environment;
- Möller's 1868 Typenplatte illustrates a prepared object whose scientific function depends on coupled slide arrangement, written key and microscope use.

These examples should support, not carry, the application.

The stronger grant-specific evidence now comes from the project's own cases:
- Bolton transaction-level lending archive;
- Oxford RMS registers/cabinet/current collection;
- Quekett freely circulating reference cabinet;
- Manchester optical-instrument actor inside pre-PMS cabinet culture.

## 8. Preferred application language

### Full version

`Prepared microscope slides occupied an unusual position between scientific specimens, collection objects and working components of microscopy. Microscopical societies assembled slides into cabinets but also lent them for repeated examination with members' instruments; instrument makers themselves could participate in the same exchange and preparation cultures. This project examines how preparations were made compatible, selected, catalogued, lent, returned and re-entered into collective use as their material settings and custodians changed.`

### Compact version

`The project examines prepared slides as detachable working components of nineteenth-century microscopy, asking how scientific societies kept them usable and retrievable as they moved between microscopes, cabinets and custodians.`

## 9. Language guard

Avoid:
- `slides were universally classified as instruments`;
- `Thomas Armstrong is proved to be the 1866 T. Armstrong`;
- `every slide borrower owned a microscope`;
- `society lending was simply an instrument-loan programme`.

Prefer:
- `working component of microscopy`;
- `instrument ecology` as analytical shorthand;
- `member custody` / `collective cabinet`;
- `overwhelmingly likely identity` for Armstrong until source-explicit closure.

## 10. Grant payoff

The SIS grant would fund the archive work needed to reconstruct how a working component of microscopy moved between collective and individual settings:

- **Oxford**: receipt, custody, registration, cabinet address and survival in a major microscopy/instrument collection;
- **Bolton**: actual lending transactions and return/re-entry into a society cabinet;
- **Manchester**: pre-PMS circulation and the intersection with a scientific/optical instrument actor;
- **Quekett (self-funded London)**: mature freely circulating reference cabinet and contemporary recognition of the limits of the finished slide alone.

The proposal should therefore present travel as a means to answer an instrument-use problem, not as a tour of slide collections.

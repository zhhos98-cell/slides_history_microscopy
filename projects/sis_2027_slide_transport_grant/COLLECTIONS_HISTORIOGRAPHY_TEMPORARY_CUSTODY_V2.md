# Collections historiography — temporary custody, loans, and re-entry v2

Updated: 2026-09-13
Status: HISTORIOGRAPHIC BOUNDARY / DOMESTIC-USE CLAIM TIGHTENED

## 1. Core boundary

Histories of museums and collections already establish that nineteenth-century collections were mobile, distributed, loaned, withdrawn and returned. The SIS project therefore does not claim novelty for temporary custody, collection circulation or return in general.

Its distinctive empirical scale remains:

**individual prepared microscope slides leaving a scientific society's working cabinet for named members, then becoming available for subsequent collective use through lending, return and collection-address practices.**

## 2. Adjacent literature already occupies broad mobility claims

### Mobile Museums
Felix Driver, Mark Nesbitt and Caroline Cornish (eds), *Mobile Museums: Collections in Circulation* (UCL Press, 2021) makes circulation fundamental to collection history rather than an exceptional afterlife of static museums.

Do not claim novelty for:
- distribution;
- exchange;
- duplicates;
- objects moving between institutions;
- later re-mobilisation.

### Swinney / Edinburgh
Geoffrey Nigel Swinney identifies the Edinburgh Museum of Science and Art's `Register of Specimens on Loan`, begun in 1865, as evidence for `temporary collecting` predicated on ephemerality rather than permanence.

Do not claim that a loan register first reveals temporary custody or destabilises permanent-property models.

### South Kensington
South Kensington developed outward circulation and tailored regional loans. The 1876 Special Loan Collection of Scientific Apparatus also depended heavily on temporary loans from individuals, societies, makers and institutions, followed by return.

Do not claim novelty for temporary scientific-instrument loans, transport, exhibition registration or institutional return.

## 3. Bolton at a different transaction scale

Bolton differs in scale and use, not because it invented lending.

Controlled facts:
- a local microscopical society maintained a slide cabinet;
- in 1881 the collection was being re-arranged after recent additions;
- Mrs Redmayne placed the whole private collection in temporary officer custody for selection;
- seventy-two Redmayne slides were selected;
- forty-eight diatom slides were also added;
- the published notice explicitly says Society slides were **lent to members**;
- the archive contains `FZ/14/3/1`, **Register of Slides Lent Out, May 1882–Jul 1887**;
- a later 1898 catalogue survives as `FZ/14/3/2`.

This supports transaction-level study of a small working collection rather than only institution-to-institution mobility.

## 4. Important wording correction: do not pre-assign the place of use

The 1881 notice says that members to whom slides were lent would have ample occupation for leisure hours during winter evenings.

It does **not** explicitly say:
- `at home`;
- on a particular privately owned microscope;
- that every loan physically left Bolton Society premises;
- or where each borrower examined the preparation.

The domestic reading is plausible, especially given `winter evenings`, but it remains an inference until the loan register, rules, minutes or another actor source gives the place/practice of use.

Therefore prefer:

`member temporary custody / lending for individual examination`

rather than:

`home lending / home examination`

unless field evidence closes the location.

## 5. Distinctive operation: repeated exit and re-entry

The strongest sequence to test is:

`institutional slide address`
→ `named member / temporary custody`
→ `individual use or examination`
→ `return if recorded`
→ `re-entry into same or translated collection address`
→ `availability to later users`.

The contribution is not simply that an object moves. It is how a small prepared scientific object can repeatedly leave collective order and become retrievable again.

## 6. Why retrievability matters

A microscope preparation can remain physically intact yet become institutionally impaired if:
- its number is lost;
- its maker/subject relation becomes unclear;
- its cabinet position is unknown;
- it can no longer be related to its series, catalogue or reference literature.

Conversely, material intervention may preserve use if a preparation is remounted/relabelled/re-addressed without losing the relation by which users identify and retrieve it.

This is where the project meets the main repository's addressability argument but remains causally distinct: the SIS satellite asks how temporary custody and changing material settings generated the need for such restoration of order.

## 7. Comparison with existing loan histories

### Edinburgh temporary collecting
`external owner → museum temporary custody/display → withdrawal/return`.

### South Kensington circulation
`museum/loan collection → regional institution/exhibition → return`.

### Bolton
`society-held individual preparation → named member temporary custody → return/re-entry into society collection`.

This distinction is analytical and scale-specific. Do not imply Edinburgh only received objects or South Kensington only sent them outward.

## 8. Instrument-history conjunction

Schaffer/Baker establish mobility, impairment and maintenance of instruments. Collection historians establish loans and circulating collections.

Bolton asks what happens when the mobile thing is simultaneously:
- a collection holding;
- a prepared scientific object;
- and a detachable working component used with a microscope.

That conjunction, rather than `mobility` itself, is the empirical niche to defend.

## 9. Evidence thresholds

### Minimum useful result
`FZ/14/3/1` records named member–slide transactions sufficiently clearly to reconstruct real temporary custody.

### Stronger result
Return marks/dates allow repeated loan cycles to be reconstructed, including the same slide passing through multiple users.

### Stronger still
Identifiers from the 1882–87 loan register persist or translate into the 1898 catalogue.

### Highest-value upgrade
A missing, damaged, remounted, replaced or renumbered preparation is shown returning to later use.

The article does not require the highest-value outcome to remain viable.

## 10. Claim ceiling

Safe before fieldwork:

`Histories of museums have already shown that nineteenth-century collections were mobile and that loans were constitutive institutional practices. Bolton makes it possible to investigate temporary custody at the transaction scale of individual preparations in a scientific society's working slide cabinet.`

If register returns are explicit:

`The Bolton register reveals repeated custody cycles in which individual preparations left and re-entered collective collection order.`

Do not yet claim:
- domestic/home use as direct fact;
- Bolton invented slide lending;
- every lent slide was used on a privately owned microscope;
- this was unique among scientific societies.

## 11. Research stop rule

Do not broaden into museum-loan historiography further unless a source directly treats:
- member-level lending of scientific preparations;
- working scientific-society cabinets;
- microscope-slide loan registers;
- or repeated object-level return/re-entry.

Otherwise the adjacent historiographic boundary is sufficiently controlled.

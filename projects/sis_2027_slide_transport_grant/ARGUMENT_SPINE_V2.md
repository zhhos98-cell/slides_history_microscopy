## 1. Before the Postal Microscopical Society: mobility already determined viability

### 1869 — Manchester Circulating Microscopic Cabinet Society

Controlled evidence:
- a Manchester Circulating Microscopic Cabinet Society existed by 1869, with Mr Horne as president;
- contemporary meeting reports are cited at *The Monthly Microscopical Journal* (1869), pp. 62 and 174, and *Quarterly Journal of Microscopical Science* 9 (1869), p. 216;
- one report describes slides of santonine belonging to Horne and aniline salt belonging to T. Armstrong;
- at the 27 July meeting J. C. Hope read on fern structure and members exhibited slides they had prepared since the previous meeting on the announced subject;
- Hope later advertised microscope-slide exchanges in *Hardwicke's Science-Gossip*.

First-order inference:
Manchester had an organised circulating-cabinet / collective slide-making culture before the Postal Microscopical Society was founded in 1873. The currently controlled passages show coordinated preparation and collective examination; they do **not yet show the physical route of the cabinet, postal carriage, loan duration, liability or repair**.

Guard:
Do not infer from the society's name alone that every meeting slide circulated through the post or that the cabinet itself travelled.

### 14 March 1871 — Pattern Post restriction makes one microscopical circulation impossible

Direct primary source:
House of Commons debate on small parcels / Pattern Post.

Controlled event:
An MP read a communication from an unnamed microscopical society saying that recent Pattern Post restrictions had dealt the society its `death blow` because it could no longer send its prepared subjects by post.

First-order inference:
A microscopical society had already made the postal eligibility of prepared objects part of its operating conditions. A change in national postal classification could therefore stop a scientific practice without changing the microscope, slide or society's intellectual programme.

Guard:
The society is **unidentified**. Chronological proximity to the Manchester Circulating Microscopic Cabinet Society is not evidence of identity. Do not connect the two unless a direct letter, newspaper report, society minute or named campaign survives.

Historical payoff if identity remains unknown:
The anonymity is not fatal. The event establishes a pre-1873 infrastructural problem: prepared microscopical subjects were already being moved in ways dependent on cheap small-parcel post.

## 2. 1873–1889 Postal Microscopical Society: control case, not novelty claim

### 1873 — movement designed into the institution

Alfred Atkinson's `Microscopic Postal Cabinets` proposes a light box compatible with safe postal transmission, a manager, a circuit of correspondents, physical slides and accompanying notes.

Inference:
Enclosure and movement were constitutive of the scheme at foundation. This is historiographic baseline already treated by Beiermann.

### 1880/81 — failure separates the components of a slide

The Seventh Annual Meeting report gives a society with roughly 690 slides in circulation and a formal register. Packing/addressing rules were fixed to box lids. The same source family discusses travelling slides, brittle cement, dry mounts failing in the post, and cases in which glass slip or cover survived while the object was lost.

Inference:
`breakage` was not one material state. Failure could divide slip, cover, cement/mount and specimen into different survival outcomes.

Use for this project:
This is the empirical basis for asking ordinary society archives what actors actually preserved, repaired or replaced.

Not a novelty claim:
Beiermann already shows that postal circulation influenced material selection, packaging and durability testing.

### 1882 — failure becomes administrable

The PMS published a 17-page *Rules and Names and Addresses of Members* supplement. Beiermann routes p. 5 to a Bath subcommittee handling detention of boxes, damage/non-circulation of slides and other irregularities, and treats the rules as allocating handling, liability and optional insurance.

Status:
The bibliographic item is directly controlled; the exact p. 5 language still requires inspection.

Inference ceiling:
Do not quote rules wording or claim exact legal/financial arrangements until primary pages are recovered.

### 1883–1887 — successful infrastructure makes breakage less visible

J. W. Measures, `Presidential Address`, *Journal of the Postal Microscopical Society* 6 (1887): 1–7, recalls frequent breakage in the early Society and says that after the 1883 parcel post and sturdy boxes it had practically disappeared. He still treats detention of boxes as an administrative problem.

Inference:
A low later frequency of recorded breakage need not mean mobility ceased to matter. Mature infrastructure can suppress the visible symptom while leaving custody, timing and forwarding work in place.

This matters for the archive method:
Do not search only for dramatic `broken slide` anecdotes. Search accounts, box/cabinet purchases, postage/carriage, custodians, registers, delays, replacement and loan/return procedures.

## 3. Ordinary scientific societies: the unresolved comparative problem

The article becomes distinct from Beiermann only if it can move beyond explicitly postal organisations.

### Quekett Microscopical Club — controlled circulation, maintenance open

Controlled:
- founding programme included exchange and a comprehensive cabinet;
- transatlantic slide exchange by 1868;
- more than 2,000 slides by 1879;
- slides freely circulated;
- the president explicitly said circulation of a finished slide did not transmit all the maker's knowledge of origin and crude state.

Open:
- historical circulation boxes;
- loan registers;
- loss/damage records;
- repair/remounting;
- replacement of individual preparations;
- whether returned slides resumed reference/teaching use.

Use:
Quekett proves that an ordinary London club redistributed slides materially; archival/object work must establish whether it also maintained them as circulating instruments.

### Royal Microscopical Society — strongest archival test

Controlled archive architecture at Oxford:
- ordinary/annual minutes 1840–1900, especially Box 31 (1868–83);
- council minutes, especially Box 38 (1863–80) and Box 28 (1880–93);
- ledger 1841–96;
- cash books 1860–90;
- Register of slides 1897;
- catalogue of microscopes/apparatus and donations;
- surviving large RMS slide collection / cabinet ecology at HSM.

Open mechanism:
No finding aid currently proves transport, repair or replacement content inside these records.

Archive test:
Can a particular preparation or class of preparations be followed through:
`acquisition / donation → storage or loan → movement → failure/loss → expenditure or decision → repair/replacement/re-registration → continued scientific use`?

Minimum success condition:
One actor/date/object chain in which the archive distinguishes the component lost from the component retained or replaced.

Stronger success condition:
A chain in which the society preserves an object's working or register identity through physical replacement/remounting.

### Manchester — potentially central, not merely provincial

Controlled nineteenth-century prehistory:
- circulating-cabinet society by 1869;
- coordinated topic-specific slide preparation and collective examination;
- later Manchester Microscopical Society founded 1879/80;
- 2023-deposited GB133-MIC archive now has item-level catalogue access.

Why it matters:
Manchester gives a pre-PMS circulation culture and later institutional archive in the same city. It can test whether mobility/custody practices persisted, changed organisational form, or left no administrative trace.

Do not claim:
- continuity from the 1869 circulating cabinet into the 1880 Society without primary evidence;
- identity with the anonymous 1871 Pattern Post complainant;
- postal movement merely from the word `circulating`.

Potential upgrade:
If GB133-MIC contains retrospective histories, accessioned predecessor papers, loan/cabinet records or correspondence naming the earlier society, Manchester may become a full case rather than a comparator.

### Bolton — collection endpoint as reverse index

Controlled:
FZ/14/3/2 = 1898 *Catalogue of Slides, Journals, and Books*.

Archive test:
Read backwards from a formal society slide collection into minutes/accounts/correspondence for acquisition, exchange, cabinet/storage, lending, loss, repair or replacement.

Payoff:
A catalogue can preserve the endpoint of a maintenance history even when the movement itself appears only in administrative records.

### Bristol — early chronological control

Controlled:
Bristol Microscopical Society archive 44409 spans 1843–1883; detailed catalogue exists.

Why use it:
Bristol can test whether maintenance/custody developed in a society whose history substantially predates the PMS.

Do not use Bristol merely to claim geographical breadth.

## 4. Material publication: Cole changes the boundary of the object

Controlled repository case:
Arthur C. Cole's *Studies in Microscopical Science* distributed letterpress, image and prepared slide as a subscriber unit. Some parcels were crushed/folded during delivery.

First-order inference:
If the slide was damaged or detached, the printed text could survive while the publication as designed became incomplete. Here the object maintained through movement is not `glass` alone but a coupled preparation–image–text issue.

Article function:
Cole can show early that the boundary of a microscope slide as a working object depends on what it is doing. In a material publication the relevant unit differs from that in a society cabinet or a postal loan box.

## 5. Maintenance, not conservation

Central distinction:
The paper is not about later museum conservation unless a modern intervention illuminates a nineteenth-century object's earlier repair history.

Historical maintenance must change whether an object can continue to work:
- returned to circulation;
- returned to a cabinet/reference series;
- used again for teaching or comparison;
- remounted while retaining identification;
- replaced while a numbered/catalogued position remains operative.

A repair with no demonstrated historical return to use is insufficient for the strongest instrument-maintenance claim.

## 6. The proposed argument in first-order form

### Proposition A — movement exposes object boundaries

When slides moved, actors encountered failures in different components: slip, cover, cement/mount, specimen, enclosure and documentation did not fail together.

Evidence already sufficient to motivate the question: PMS 1880/81 + Ward comparator + Cole.

### Proposition B — institutions assign the consequences of failure

Liability, custody, insurance, box detention, replacement and registers convert material failure into institutional work.

Evidence already strong in PMS; **ordinary-society evidence remains open**.

### Proposition C — repair is historically important only if use resumes

What matters for instrument history is not that damaged glass can be conserved, but that a preparation can return to comparison, teaching, reference, publication or circulation.

Evidence remains to be earned from archives / tightly controlled remounting cases.

### Proposition D — material continuity is only one possible continuity

A slide/preparation may remain scientifically legible after the glass, mount or institutional address changes; conversely intact glass can cease to function as the same knowledge object if the preparation or documentation is lost.

Repo already has Pacini/remounting and address-system comparators supporting the broader possibility. The SIS project must show whether mobility/maintenance actors themselves enacted this distinction in British microscopy.

## 7. Provisional article structure

### Opening scene
1871 Pattern Post `death blow`: a postal rule stops prepared subjects moving.

Why this opening works:
It begins with an infrastructure failure that affects scientific practice without any microscope itself breaking.

Guard:
Keep society anonymous.

### Section 1 — what broke
PMS 1880/81 component-level failures + Ward as explicitly American comparator.

Question generated:
What is `the slide` when slip, cover, mount and object have different survival histories?

### Section 2 — who became responsible
PMS rules / Measures: box rules, detention, liability/insurance (after direct p.5 control), parcel post, administrative maturation.

Question generated:
Did ordinary societies institutionalise the same responsibilities?

### Section 3 — societies keeping objects in use
Oxford RMS + Quekett; Manchester if archive permits; Bolton/Bristol as bounded comparisons.

This section must be archive-driven, not written in advance.

### Section 4 — different units of maintenance
Cole material publication + one repair/remounting case if secure.

Payoff:
The maintained unit can be slide, preparation, issue, cabinet address or circulation system depending on scientific practice.

### Ending
Return to 1871: postal policy could kill a circulation system because movement was already part of how prepared microscopical objects worked socially. By the later nineteenth century, societies had made some of that vulnerability routine through boxes, registers, rules and custody. Whether they also preserved slide identity through repair/replacement is the central archival test, not a premise.

## 8. Grant-versus-paper distinction

The grant proposal can promise a clear research question without pretending the strongest conclusion is known.

Grant-safe formulation:
`The research will examine how nineteenth-century microscopical societies managed the material consequences of moving slides: custody, breakage, repair, replacement and return to use.`

Paper-level hypothesis:
`Mobility made the working identity of a microscope preparation depend on more than material continuity in a single glass slip.`

Do not turn the paper-level hypothesis into an application fact before archival testing.

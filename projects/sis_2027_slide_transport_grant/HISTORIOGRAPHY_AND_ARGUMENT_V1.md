# SIS 2027 — historiography and argument boundary v1

Updated: 2026-09-13
Status: ARGUMENT DEVELOPMENT / HISTORIOGRAPHIC BOUNDARY SET

## 1. Immediate correction to the initial grant framing

The first working question — `How did the requirements of circulation change the material conditions under which nineteenth-century microscope slides could function as scientific instruments?` — is too close to existing scholarship to carry the project by itself.

Lea Beiermann's 2021 article on the American Postal Microscopical Club is already in the repository bibliography. Her 2023 Maastricht dissertation substantially extends that work to the British Postal Microscopical Society. The dissertation treats British and American postal microscopy as knowledge infrastructures and directly documents:

- the British Postal Microscopical Society's slide-box circuits;
- rules for handling, packaging and forwarding slides;
- responsibility and optional insurance for breakage;
- the effect of the 1883 parcel post and sturdy boxes on breakage rates;
- choice of mounting methods and materials for postal durability;
- long-distance circulation as an empirical test of the comparative durability of preparations;
- the relation between standard slide/box dimensions and interoperability.

Therefore the following propositions are **not available as the project's main novelty claim**:

1. postal circulation shaped slide materials or mounting practice;
2. breakage encouraged sturdier boxes or altered packing;
3. transport itself became a test of preparation durability;
4. standard slide dimensions and standard boxes facilitated circulation;
5. British postal microscopy can be distinguished from Beiermann simply by switching from the American club to the British society.

Beiermann should be treated as the immediate historiographic predecessor, not as a gap to be filled by repeating the British side in greater detail.

Repository baseline:
- `bibliography/bibliography-05.csv#lit_beiermann_2021`;
- `docs/UK_US_RETURN_TO_CORE_2026-08-10.md`, which already states that Postal Microscopical Society circuits joined slides to notes, drawings, packing rules, postage, damage liability and valuation.

External continuation to control before drafting:
- Lea Beiermann, `“A Co-operation of Observers”: Crafting knowledge infrastructures for microscopy` (PhD dissertation, Maastricht University, 2023), especially the chapters on British and American postal microscopy.

## 2. A different historiographic entrance: instruments in failure and repair

The stronger project does not ask primarily how craft knowledge travelled. It asks what recurrent movement, breakage, repair, replacement and custody did to the historical identity and working boundaries of a microscope preparation as an instrument component.

Two instrument-history interventions give this project a route not exhausted by postal-microscopy scholarship:

### Simon Schaffer, 2011

`Easily Cracked: Scientific Instruments in States of Disrepair`, *Isis* 102.4 (2011), 706–717.

Schaffer's useful proposition is methodological: breakage, defect, repair and recuperation make visible the mutable character of instruments and the systems of instruction, support and use that normally disappear when an instrument is represented as complete and self-contained.

### Alexi Baker, 2012

`“Precision,” “Perfection,” and the Reality of British Scientific Instruments on the Move During the 18th Century`, *Material Culture Review* 74–75 (2012), 14–29.

Baker makes mobility part of instrument history rather than an external context. Movement and changing environmental conditions impaired use; mending and adaptation consequently formed part of technological practice.

The slide project can extend this repair-and-mobility problem into nineteenth-century microscopy, where the working object was unusually small, reproducible, lendable and replaceable, and where a preparation often moved without the microscope itself.

## 3. Proposed conceptual shift

Do **not** define the project by the generic phrase `transport shaped affordances`.

Instead begin from a concrete historical sequence:

`preparation made → mounted on glass → assigned to a box/cabinet/publication/society → moved or lent → damaged / altered / detached / lost → responsibility assigned → slide repaired, remounted, replaced or written off → object returned to use or re-entered into a collection/register`.

The analytical question is what remains the same working object across that sequence.

A microscope slide could fail at several different material levels:

- the glass slip could break while the preparation survived;
- the cover glass or mountant could fail while the slip remained intact;
- the specimen could disappear while `nothing but the glass slip` remained;
- a slide could survive physically but lose its documentary relation to maker, specimen, series or cabinet address;
- a preparation could be remounted onto new glass;
- an institutional slide could be replaced while the cabinet/register position persisted;
- a complete preparation could become unusable because its enclosing box, packing or circulation arrangement failed.

These are not equivalent failures. The project should ask which part actors attempted to preserve and which part they regarded as replaceable.

## 4. Stronger working questions

Current preferred question:

**How did breakage, custody and repair shape the microscope slide as a mobile instrument component in nineteenth-century Britain?**

Alternative formulations to test against sources:

- **What changed when microscope slides had to be lent, posted, returned, repaired and used again?**
- **How did repeated movement redistribute the work of keeping microscope preparations usable across glass, mounts, boxes, cabinets and scientific societies?**
- **When a microscope slide failed in transit, what exactly was repaired, replaced or preserved?**

The last formulation is the sharpest mechanism test. It makes the historical boundary of the object an empirical question rather than assuming in advance that `the slide` means the glass slip.

## 5. Relation to `Using Up Old Slides`

The submitted Orléans 2027 proposal, `“Using Up Old Slides”: The Changing Affordances of Glass in British Microscopy, 1862–1883`, should remain a **related but distinct paper** rather than being silently converted into the SIS project.

That proposal begins from reuse and remounting:

- old slips cleaned and scraped for reuse;
- scratched glass reassigned to opaque objects;
- revarnishing and remounting;
- a valuable ruled cover glass salvaged from a spoiled Nobert plate;
- postal damage changing what a slide could withstand.

Its conceptual object is the changing usefulness of glass. The SIS project's stronger object is the maintenance of a mobile scientific preparation through institutional systems of custody, repair, replacement and re-entry into use.

The two can overlap in individual cases without making identical claims.

### Geography guard

Richard H. Ward's `inertia of the slide itself` example belongs to the American postal-microscopy context, although it circulated in British print. It cannot be treated as a British institutional case merely because the passage was reported to British readers. It is best used, if retained, as a tightly labelled transatlantic technical comparator.

## 6. Existing controlled evidence that already supports the new direction

### A. Cole — failure of a material publication in transit

Authority: `data/analysis/slide_as_publication_cole_v1.json`.

Cole's *Studies in Microscopical Science* joined descriptive letterpress, image and a prepared microscope slide in a subscriber issue. Complaints recorded issues arriving crushed after being folded into letter boxes, and the editor asked subscribers to have postmen deliver parcels at the door.

Historical use for this project:
- the preparation was not merely freight inside a publication; it helped constitute the publication unit;
- postal failure could therefore make a publication materially incomplete even if the printed pages survived;
- the object needing maintenance is larger than glass alone.

Do not reduce this case to `postal damage`; its value is the coupling of slide, image, text and subscriber delivery.

### B. Quekett Microscopical Club — custody after circulation

Authority: `data/analysis/quekett_club_cabinet_circulation_authority_upgrade_v1.json`.

By 1879 the Club said its cabinet contained more than 2,000 slides and that preparations were freely circulated. Its president also distinguished the preparer's knowledge of origin and crude state from what a first-time borrower could learn from the finished preparation.

Historical use for this project:
- circulation moves an object into temporary custody;
- a society cabinet connects lending to return, classification and reference use;
- mobility creates documentary as well as physical maintenance problems.

Open edge:
- surviving circulation-box, loan-register, damage, replacement or repair documentation has not yet been identified.

### C. *Science-Gossip* / Butterworth — maintenance designed into circulation procedure

Authority: `data/analysis/wmc_science_gossip_periodical_operations_2026-09-10.md`.

The 1865 `Circulating Cabinet of Objects` specifies trays, six objects per tray, elastic bands, raised beads, a purpose-made box, one-week custody, replacement of an object on return, a travelling memorandum book, a stamp-case and carriage finance.

Historical use:
- circulation is inseparable from a designed custody/return procedure;
- replacement is built into the scheme rather than treated as an exceptional afterlife.

Evidence ceiling:
- Butterworth says `objects`; do not silently rewrite every circulated object as a microscope slide.

### D. Oxford RMS — institutional maintenance archive, not yet transport evidence

Authority: `data/analysis/oxford_hsm_rms_cabinet_database_upgrade_v1.json` plus the July 2026 internal archive list supplied by HSM.

Oxford preserves an unusually strong conjunction:
- a major surviving RMS slide cabinet / microscopical collection;
- historical and modern cataloguing/address systems;
- ordinary and council minutes;
- long-run ledgers and cash books;
- a 1897 register of slides;
- catalogue of microscopes/apparatus and donations.

This does **not** yet prove that the archive contains packing, repair or breakage records. Its value is that it can test whether movement and maintenance entered ordinary institutional administration: purchases, carriage/postage, cases/cabinets, repairs, replacements, custody and registration.

## 7. What the archive trip must establish

The archival programme is now a set of mechanism tests, not a search for more anecdotes of broken glass.

### Test 1 — failure ownership

When a slide or preparation was damaged, who bore the loss?

Possible documentary traces:
- liability rules;
- insurance;
- council decisions;
- correspondence with lenders/donors/preparers;
- replacement payments;
- write-offs or changed register entries.

### Test 2 — object boundary

What did an institution replace after failure?

Possible states:
- cover glass only;
- mountant / cell;
- specimen remounted on another slip;
- whole slide replaced;
- cabinet position retained but physical slide changed;
- documentary record corrected while object stayed physically unchanged.

Finding these distinctions would move the project beyond generic fragility.

### Test 3 — return to use

Did repair merely conserve a damaged object, or return it to circulation, teaching, comparison or reference use?

This is crucial for instrument history. Conservation alone is not the claim; maintenance matters because it changes whether an object can continue to work in a microscopical practice.

### Test 4 — institutionalization

Did recurrent movement produce durable administrative structures?

Test for:
- slide registers;
- cabinet/box numbering;
- loan rules;
- custody periods;
- named custodians;
- accounts for postage/carriage/repairs;
- inventories recording loss/replacement.

A positive result would let the project move from isolated technical advice to scientific-society maintenance infrastructure.

### Test 5 — ordinary society versus postal society

Beiermann establishes strong postal-society evidence. The new project should test whether the same maintenance problems also structured ordinary local/professional societies whose primary identity was **not** postal exchange.

This is the reason for RMS + Manchester + Bolton + Bristol comparisons.

If no comparable evidence appears outside explicitly postal organizations, that negative result is historically meaningful: it would delimit mobility/maintenance as a specialized postal regime rather than a general condition of society slide collections.

## 8. Provincial comparison logic

### Manchester

Use GB133-MIC not simply to find `transport` vocabulary but to test society custody, acquisition/exchange, cabinets, lending and payments. Manchester is especially useful because nineteenth-century local microscopy also generated circulating-cabinet practices; exact archival subreferences still need to be pinned.

### Bolton

Use FZ/14/3/2, the 1898 `Catalogue of Slides, Journals, and Books`, as a controlled endpoint. Work backward from a formal slide collection into minutes/accounts/administration where possible.

### Bristol

Use 44409 as an earlier society comparison. The point is not geographical coverage. It is chronological/institutional control: can a society whose archive begins before the postal-society moment show a different route from acquisition and display to custody and maintenance?

### London — self-funded

Quekett is analytically more important than its £0 budget line suggests. It can supply the strongest society-circulation comparator if current or historical documentation of boxes, loans, breakage, repair or replacement can be identified.

## 9. Claim ladder

### REJECT AS NOVELTY

`Postal circulation changed slide materials, packaging and durability practices.`

Reason: securely occupied by Beiermann.

### TOO GENERIC

`Transport changed the affordances of microscope slides.`

Reason: true enough, but difficult to distinguish from existing postal/craft scholarship and from the Orléans proposal.

### VIABLE

`Mobility made maintenance part of slide use: scientific societies had to assign custody, manage breakage and return preparations to working order.`

Needs: direct institutional records.

### STRONGER

`Breakage and repair exposed that a microscope slide was not a self-contained glass object. Its continued instrumentality could reside in a preparation, mount, box/cabinet position, label/register address or institutional custody arrangement, and different failures required different forms of replacement.`

Needs: at least two source-controlled cases in which actors distinguish what is preserved from what is replaced.

### STRONGEST POSSIBLE, NOT YET EARNED

`The nineteenth-century slide became a maintainable instrument component because societies learned to preserve its working identity across material replacement.`

Do not use this as a grant conclusion unless Oxford/provincial archives produce direct evidence linking repair/replacement to continued use or registration.

## 10. Falsification / stop rules

The project should narrow or change direction if archival work shows any of the following:

1. RMS/local society records contain acquisition/display but no evidence of loan, return, maintenance, damage or replacement;
2. all strong maintenance evidence remains confined to the British/American postal societies already treated by Beiermann;
3. records do not distinguish damaged components or continued object identity after replacement;
4. `repair` evidence proves to be modern museum conservation rather than nineteenth-century working practice.

If so, the project can still produce a bounded SIS Bulletin article around Cole + Quekett + postal evidence, but it should not claim a general British society maintenance regime.

## 11. Provisional titles

Preferred working titles, in order of current usefulness:

1. **Keeping Slides in Use: Breakage, Repair, and Mobility in Nineteenth-Century Microscopy**
2. **Supporting Glass: Microscope Slides, Movement, and the Work of Maintenance**
3. **After the Box Arrived: Breakage, Repair, and the Mobile Microscope Slide**
4. **Slides in Transit: Custody and Repair in Victorian Microscopy**

Avoid `safe transmission` because it is Beiermann's established phrase and historiographic territory.

## 1. Project in one sentence

**The project asks how nineteenth-century microscope preparations were kept usable and retrievable as they moved between carriers, cabinets and custodians, with particular attention to scientific-society practices of selection, re-arrangement, lending, return and re-entry into collective use.**

The governing question is frozen:

> **How were nineteenth-century microscope preparations kept usable and retrievable as they moved between carriers, cabinets and custodians?**

Expanded empirical form:

> **What had to be changed, and what had to remain stable, when a microscope preparation was remounted, selected into a society collection, re-arranged in a cabinet, lent to another user, returned, and re-entered into collection order?**

New evidence may change the answer and the weighting of cases. It should not reopen the project into a generic history of microscope-slide circulation, postal transport, glass, or scientific societies.

---

## 2. The decisive conceptual shift

The project began from `transport`: postal circulation, breakage, packing, and material affordances of glass slides.

That framing is no longer the article's centre.

The research run established that several neighbouring claims are already substantially occupied:

- postal microscopy as knowledge infrastructure;
- safe transmission, packing, breakage and liability;
- postal circulation as a test of mounting durability;
- standard dimensions and interoperability;
- mended slides in postal clubs;
- mobility and impairment of scientific instruments;
- repair as a problem of instrument identity;
- temporary museum loans and mobile collections.

Accordingly, **transport is now treated as a condition that produces changes of material and institutional setting**, not as the historical subject in itself.

The article's distinctive operation is:

`object enters a new setting`
→ `selection / reformatting / remounting / arrangement`
→ `temporary custody / lending`
→ `return`
→ `re-entry into a shared collection order`
→ `availability to another user`.

The key historical problem is therefore not simply movement outward but **reusable custody**.

## 3. Historiographic boundary

### 3.1 Postal microscopy

Lea Beiermann already establishes postal microscopy as an infrastructure of knowledge and practice, including packing, forwarding, breakage, liability, mending, mounting-material durability, box design, standardisation and British/American postal societies.

Therefore do not claim novelty for:
- slides moving through postal systems;
- transport changing material choice;
- safe transmission;
- broken or mended postal slides;
- standardisation enabling exchange.

Use the Postal Microscopical Society as a **control case in which circulation responsibilities became explicit**, not as the novelty site.

### 3.2 Instrument repair and mobility

Schaffer, Schickore and Baker already make breakdown, maintenance, repair and movement constitutive of instrument history.

Therefore do not claim novelty for:
- instruments being damaged in movement;
- repair destabilising material identity;
- maintenance as part of scientific use.

The slide project must remain empirically narrower: a preparation is detachable, independently mobile, divisible into carrier/specimen/mount/label, and repeatedly handled under temporary custody.

### 3.3 Collection mobility and temporary custody

Histories of mobile collections, museum loans and `temporary collecting` already show that nineteenth-century collections circulated, were lent, returned and reassembled.

Therefore do not claim that temporary custody or return itself is new.

The distinctive scale is:

**an individual prepared microscope slide leaving a working scientific-society cabinet for named member custody and then becoming available to subsequent collective use.**

The empirical conjunction to defend is:

**collection object + prepared scientific object + detachable working instrument component + recurrent member-level custody.**

## 4. Why this belongs in the history of scientific instruments

Do not argue that every microscope slide was itself a complete scientific instrument.

Use the narrower formulation:

> **Prepared microscope slides were detachable working components in nineteenth-century microscopy: their carrier dimensions, glass thickness, mount, labels and preparation state affected whether specimens could be observed, compared and reused with microscopes by successive users.**

The historical system under study is:

`prepared specimen / glass carrier`
↔ `microscope stage and optical use`
↔ `cabinet / tray / box`
↔ `label / register / catalogue address`
↔ `borrower / temporary custodian`
↔ `return to collective use`.

This is not merely collection history because the society cabinet supplied prepared working objects for microscopy. The question is how these objects remained usable across different microscopes, formats and custodians.

Two especially useful remit anchors are now controlled:

1. **Bolton:** society members encountered microscope stands/objectives and prepared materials within the same institutional ecology; the society then maintained a slide collection explicitly lent to members.
2. **Manchester:** the pre-PMS circulating-cabinet culture intersects extremely strongly with Thomas Armstrong, the Deansgate optical-instrument maker and 1871 RMS fellow. The 1866/69 `T. Armstrong` identity is A++ / near-direct, though not source-explicit because the early cabinet sources do not expand the initial.

Preferred SIS language:

> `Prepared microscope slides occupied an unusual position between scientific specimens, collection objects and working components of microscopy. This project examines how societies kept them usable as they were selected, catalogued, lent, returned, remounted and re-entered into collective use.`

## 5. The core empirical architecture

The project now has four principal cases with different functions rather than four interchangeable examples.

### A1. Bolton Microscopical Society — transaction-level centre

Bolton is the best empirical centre for the article and the clearest small-society case for the SIS grant.

Controlled 1881 sequence:

`Redmayne private collection`
→ `Mrs Redmayne places whole collection with President + Secretary`
→ `officer selection`
→ `72 Redmayne slides chosen`
→ `48 additional diatom slides added separately`
→ `Society collection being re-arranged`
→ `Society slides lent to members`.

Exact archive:
- `FZ/14/1/1` — General and Committee Minutes, 1 Aug 1877–29 Oct 1880;
- `FZ/14/1/2` — General and Committee Minutes, 5 Nov 1880–8 Feb 1900;
- `FZ/14/2/1` — Account and Subscription Book, 1880–94;
- **`FZ/14/3/1` — Register of Slides Lent Out, May 1882–Jul 1887;**
- `FZ/14/3/2` — Catalogue of Slides, Journals, and Books, 1898.

The five-year lending register is the crucial upgrade. Bolton is no longer an archive-promise case; it preserves the exact record type required to reconstruct named temporary custody.

Core field question:

> Can the same preparation / number / series be followed through lending, return or repeated borrowing and later catalogue persistence?

Minimum result sufficient for article:
- real borrower–preparation transactions;
- return/repeat-use patterns if recorded;
- an identifiable society address system.

Stronger result:
- loan-register identifiers persist or translate into the 1898 catalogue.

Highest-value but non-required result:
- non-return, damage, remounting, replacement, renumbering or intervention followed by continued use/catalogue identity.

Important correction guard:
**F. W. Sharpus / W. R. Hughes / twelve Echinodermata slides belong to Birmingham, not Bolton.** Do not use that material as a Bolton accession anchor.

### A2. Royal Microscopical Society / Oxford HSM — institutional-address centre

Oxford supplies a large society archive, historical catalogues/registers, financial records and a surviving RMS collection in one place.

Exact archive anchors:
- Box 31 — ordinary/annual minutes, Jul 1868–Feb 1883;
- Box 38 — council minutes, Feb 1863–Jul 1880;
- Box 28 — council minutes, Oct 1880–Dec 1893;
- Box 41 — ledger, 1841–96;
- Box 49 — Register of slides, 1897;
- Box 103 — catalogue of microscopes/apparatus + Donations to Collection;
- Boxes 107–110 — nineteenth-century cash books;
- RMS MS 5, 13, 18 — historical slide catalogues.

Use public event hooks to enter the archive rather than reading whole boxes linearly. Existing dated hooks include Browne 1870, Allen 1873, Naples/RMS 1880, Crisp 1881, Baker 1882, Bell 1883, Coppock 1883 and Norman 1884.

Core question:

> What internal custody status followed public words such as `presented`, `received`, `donated` and `exhibited`?

Oxford can distinguish:
- temporary demonstration;
- permanent accession;
- library/cabinet ownership;
- financial/material work;
- later register/catalogue address;
- current museum/cabinet survival where crosswalks close.

The fifth Oxford day is justified specifically by this difficult historical-event → internal-address → current-object crosswalk.

### A3. Quekett Microscopical Club / NHM — mature reference-cabinet control

Quekett supplies a mature comparison, not a second Bolton.

Controlled:
- exchange and comprehensive cabinet in founding programme;
- large accessions and transatlantic exchange by 1868;
- >2,000 slides by 1879;
- slides freely circulated;
- president explicitly distinguished the maker's knowledge of origin/preparation from what a first-time borrower could learn from the finished slide.

Analytical role:

- **Bolton:** how a heterogeneous collection becomes lendable and administratively trackable.
- **Quekett:** what a mature lendable/reference cabinet can and cannot transmit through the object alone.

London travel is self-funded.

### B1. Manchester — prehistory / instrument-maker network

Manchester is important but bounded.

Controlled sequence:
- 1865 Oldham-area circulating cabinet with trays, six objects per tray, protective fittings, one-week custody, periodic replacement, memorandum book and carriage finance;
- 1866 Manchester-area recruitment through `Mr T. Armstrong, Deansgate`;
- 1869 Manchester Circulating Microscopic Cabinet Society with `T. Armstrong` exhibiting prepared slides;
- independent 1867–68 records place Thomas Armstrong on Deansgate;
- official maker history identifies him as the Deansgate actor expanding the family business into optical instruments;
- Thomas Armstrong elected RMS fellow in 1871;
- Redmayne later presents a copy of his microphotograph work to Thomas Armstrong of Armstrong & Brother Microscopes.

Current evidence status:

`T. Armstrong = Thomas Armstrong` is **overwhelmingly likely / A++ near-direct**, but not source-explicit because the controlled 1866/69 cabinet sources do not expand `T.`.

Manchester fieldwork should test only:
- explicit Armstrong closure;
- predecessor/circulating-cabinet memory in `GB133-MIC`;
- cabinet/slide/home-examination/mounting-section material;
- later institutional memory of the 1860s culture.

If absent, stop. Manchester is not required for the Bolton/Oxford article to work.

## 7. Material continuity and object boundaries

The project retains a material-identity strand, but this is now subordinate to the custody argument rather than the universal thesis.

Controlled useful cases:

### Richardson 1862
Imported German preparations could be remounted because the original carriers did not fit local British cabinet/slide geometry.

Historical implication:
`preparation identity ≠ original glass carrier`.

Howard Lynk already reconstructs the German-injection/product-format episode. Use Richardson as an object-boundary premise, not a discovery claim.

### Postal failure cases
A glass slip can survive while the `object`/specimen is lost.

This supplies the inverse relation:
`carrier survival ≠ preparation survival`.

### Pacini comparator
A preparation could be remounted in new glass while remaining identified through its case/publication address.

### Cole
A fixed cabinet/index architecture could preserve a publication address while individual preparations were substituted or misassigned.

These cases show why the project must not define `the slide` as one indivisible thing. Historically relevant components include:
- specimen/prepared object;
- glass carrier;
- cover glass;
- mountant/cement/cell;
- label and maker attribution;
- cabinet position/number;
- catalogue/register address;
- temporary custodian;
- relation to publication/series/reference literature.

The fieldwork asks which of these relations societies actually maintained when objects moved in and out of custody.

## 8. The article's preferred narrative

### Opening — Bolton, summer 1881

A widow hands over an entire private slide collection. Society officers decide what becomes institutionally useful. Seventy-two slides are selected. Forty-eight more diatom slides enter. The collection is re-arranged. Members borrow slides.

Opening question:

> What work turned heterogeneous private preparations into a collection whose individual objects could leave the cabinet, enter temporary custody and later become available to collective use again?

### Section I — making preparations compatible with movement, 1862–73

Richardson remounting → Butterworth circulating cabinet → Manchester/Armstrong → Postal Microscopical Society as formalised control.

Function:
establish material compatibility, custody procedure and pre-PMS circulation without claiming postal novelty.

### Section II — Bolton, 1880–98

Redmayne posthumous transfer → officer selection → re-arrangement → lending register → return/repeated use → 1898 catalogue.

This should be the empirical centre if `FZ/14/3/1` is rich.

### Section III — Oxford RMS

Dated public receipts/donations → internal custody categories → ledger/register/catalogue → current collection/address where crosswalks close.

Redmayne `[1978-665]` gives one bounded material thread.

### Section IV — Quekett

A mature freely circulating reference cabinet and the recognised limits of the finished object alone.

Function:
show that collective usability depended on names, series, books and documentary relations, not only physical possession.

### Conclusion — reusable custody

The significance of movement lies not only in transmission. Scientific societies had to make prepared objects capable of successive departures and returns while preserving enough material and documentary identity for another user to find and use them.

## 9. Claim ladder

### Level 1 — secure now

**Nineteenth-century microscopical societies could operate slide cabinets as more than passive stores. Bolton preserves a dedicated five-year lending register, and Quekett described thousands of slides as freely circulated.**

This is safe before fieldwork.

### Level 2 — strong research proposition

**Societies kept preparations usable under changing custody through operations such as selection, re-arrangement, cataloguing, lending, return and re-entry into collective order.**

This is the central fieldwork hypothesis.

### Level 3 — archive dependent

If Bolton gives stable identifiers/repeat borrowing and Oxford yields compatible custody/register evidence:

**The scientific usefulness of a society slide depended not only on safe movement or preparation quality, but on institutional practices that restored a mobile object to collective order after a change of custodian.**

### Level 4 — high threshold

Only if material intervention plus later use can be shown in at least two independent contexts:

**Microscope preparations became maintainable instrument components because societies could preserve or translate their working identities across changes of carrier and custodian.**

Do not use Level 4 as a settled grant conclusion.

## 10. What would falsify or narrow the project

The project is designed not to collapse if the archive lacks dramatic broken-slide stories.

### Bolton failure modes
If `FZ/14/3/1` gives no usable object/address information and only borrower names or coarse batch loans:
- write the custody system at aggregate level;
- do not claim item-level return/re-entry.

If no return dates are recorded:
- do not speak of completed loan cycles unless another record supplies them.

If no Redmayne slides can be identified:
- retain the 1881 collection-transformation scene;
- drop Redmayne object-continuity claims.

### Oxford failure modes
If internal records distinguish donation/display poorly:
- use Oxford for institutional collection formation and historical/current address translation;
- do not force a fine-grained custody taxonomy.

If no historical-event → current-object crosswalk closes:
- retain archive/register history;
- do not claim surviving physical continuity.

### Manchester failure mode
If `GB133-MIC` gives no predecessor/Armstrong link:
- keep Manchester as controlled published prehistory only;
- do not claim institutional continuity into the later society.

### Global fall-back
Even with weak repair/material-intervention evidence, the project remains viable as:

**a history of society slide cabinets as systems of temporary custody, selection, lending and re-entry into collective use.**

## 18. Bottom line

The project has moved from a plausible transport-of-slides idea to a sharply bounded history of **working preparations under changing custody**.

Its strongest pre-fieldwork evidence is no longer a broken-slide anecdote. It is the combination of:

- a nineteenth-century society explicitly re-arranging a slide collection for lending;
- a surviving five-year register devoted to slides lent out;
- a later catalogue capable of testing persistence and re-entry;
- a large RMS archive/cabinet system capable of testing custody and address translation at institutional scale;
- a mature Quekett cabinet showing both free circulation and the epistemic limits of the finished preparation;
- a pre-PMS Manchester culture intersecting very strongly with an optical-instrument maker;
- Redmayne as a bounded actor/material bridge across private, society, library and instrument-network settings.

The project therefore has a viable minimum article even if no repair or breakage chain appears in the archives, while retaining a clear path to a stronger instrument-maintenance argument if fieldwork produces remounting, replacement or object-address continuity.

The practical grant case is correspondingly simple: **£677 funds seven high-density archive days outside London; London comparison is self-funded; Bristol is omitted; the research question is already stable.**

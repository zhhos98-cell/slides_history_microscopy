# SIS 2027 — bounded historiography check: lending, return, and re-entry v1

Updated: 2026-09-13
Status: TARGETED SEARCH / NOT AN EXHAUSTIVE ORIGINALITY CLAIM

## 1. Narrow question checked

The relevant historiographical question is not whether historians have studied:
- microscope-slide circulation;
- postal microscopy;
- Victorian microscopical societies;
- slide collections;
- cabinets;
- standardisation;
- repair.

All of those have clear precedents.

The bounded check asks instead:

**Has transaction-level lending and return from nineteenth-century microscopical-society slide cabinets been made the central historical mechanism — especially named borrowers, slide addresses, temporary custody, return, repeated use, and re-entry into a society catalogue/cabinet order?**

## 2. Nearest overlap A — Lea Beiermann

Lea Beiermann, `A method for safe transmission: the microscope slides of the American Postal Microscopical Club` (BJHS, 2021), is the strongest existing overlap for movement.

Her object is a deliberately postal club and its information/craft infrastructure. The paper treats:
- postal chains;
- packing / forwarding;
- slide and note circulation;
- transmission of preparation knowledge;
- material fragility and safe movement.

Her later dissertation also extends the postal/infrastructure analysis to British material and standardisation.

Boundary for this project:
Do not claim novelty for postal circulation, packaging, breakage, mended slides, standardised slide/box dimensions, or remote transmission of craft knowledge.

The Bolton register poses a different institutional question:

`Society collection → named temporary custodian → return/repeated borrowing → later collection order`.

That is not simply a postal circuit.

## 3. Nearest overlap B — Gibson collection study, 2025

The 2025 study of the Gibson microscope-slide collection in the Museum of Medicine and Health, University of Manchester, gives a close comparator for surviving Victorian slide collections.

It explicitly situates private and scientific-society slide collections within Victorian microscopy and reconstructs makers, provenance and collection formation from a surviving cabinet/slide assemblage.

Boundary for this project:
The Gibson study is important for collection formation and provenance, but it does not make a transaction-level historical lending register — named borrower, temporary custody, return and repeated circulation — its central unit of analysis.

Use it as a comparator for what surviving cabinets can reveal, not as evidence that Bolton-style lending history has already been written.

## 4. Other nearby material

Public histories of microscopical societies and surviving labelled slides sometimes discuss:
- cabinets built collectively;
- society collections open to members;
- exchange and display;
- slide labels possibly indicating society use;
- donations by private collectors.

These are useful context, but the targeted search did not locate a historical study centred on a continuous nineteenth-century **Register of Slides Lent Out** comparable to Bolton `FZ/14/3/1` and cross-walked to later catalogue order.

## 5. Safe originality statement

Do **not** write:
`No one has studied microscope-slide lending before.`

Safe formulation:

**Existing work has shown how microscope slides travelled through postal networks and how surviving slide collections preserve histories of makers and collecting. This project asks a different, transaction-level question: how did ordinary scientific societies make preparations leave a collective cabinet, enter temporary custody, and return to a retrievable institutional order? A targeted literature search has not located a study centred on a nineteenth-century society lending register of this kind.**

This is a bounded originality claim tied to the actual source form.

## 6. Why Bolton matters historiographically

`FZ/14/3/1 — Register of Slides Lent Out, May 1882–Jul 1887` is valuable not merely because it mentions circulation.

If the register records slide numbers/subjects and return transactions, it makes visible a repeated institutional operation normally flattened by the word `circulation`:

`collection address`
→ `object leaves cabinet`
→ `named borrower assumes temporary custody`
→ `time passes / object may move or be used privately`
→ `object returns`
→ `collection must recognize and re-place it`
→ `object remains available for another transaction`.

The historical problem is therefore **re-entry**, not only outward movement.

## 7. Relation to Oxford and Quekett

### Bolton
Transaction-level temporary custody can be tested directly.

### Oxford RMS
The problem is longer-duration institutional re-entry/re-addressing: public donation or exhibition → internal custody/register → surviving modern RMS/HSM object address.

### Quekett
The club supplies a mature reference/lending cabinet where slides were freely circulated and actors explicitly recognised that finished objects did not carry all maker knowledge.

Together, the cases move from `circulation` as a general social metaphor to distinct institutional operations of custody, retrieval and return.

## 8. Conceptual payoff

Preferred formulation:

**A lendable collection had to solve two directions of movement: how an object could leave, and how it could come back as the same retrievable preparation.**

This formulation is more specific than `knowledge circulation` and does not require every object to be repaired or broken.

It also gives the project a falsifiable archive test:
- if the Bolton register has no stable object addresses/return structure, the re-entry argument must be weakened;
- if it does, the source can support a transaction-level history of a society cabinet in use.

## 9. Stop rule

No further broad historiography search is warranted before archival inspection.

Resume only if:
- a publication specifically studies the Bolton register or an equivalent nineteenth-century microscope-slide lending register;
- archive evidence changes the mechanism from lending/return to something else;
- a reviewer/application form requires a more extensive literature statement.

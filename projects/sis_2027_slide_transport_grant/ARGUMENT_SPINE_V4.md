# SIS/article argument spine v4 — from circulation to temporary custody and re-entry

Updated: 2026-09-13
Status: SYNTHESIS AUTHORITY / RESEARCH QUESTION UNCHANGED

## 1. Governing question

**How were nineteenth-century microscope preparations kept usable and retrievable as they moved between carriers, cabinets and custodians?**

This remains the frozen question. Version 4 changes the narrative and historiographic architecture, not the question.

## 2. Preferred opening scene — Bolton, summer 1881

The article should now open with the Bolton Microscopical Society rather than the anonymous 1871 Pattern Post complaint.

Controlled contemporary sequence:
- after John Thomas Redmayne's death, Mrs Redmayne put the whole private slide collection into the hands of the Society's President and Secretary;
- they selected six dozen `choice Slides` for the Society;
- another forty-eight diatom slides were added;
- the Society's valuable slide collection was consequently `being re-arranged`;
- the report explicitly refers to members `to whom the Slides are lent`.

This scene is analytically dense because the preparations cross several boundaries at once:

`private property`
→ `temporary officer custody`
→ `institutional selection`
→ `society collection`
→ `cabinet re-arrangement`
→ `member loan`.

The archive then supplies `FZ/14/3/1`, a five-year Register of Slides Lent Out (May 1882–Jul 1887), and an 1898 catalogue.

Opening question:
**What work was required to turn a heterogeneous private slide collection into a collection whose individual preparations could leave the cabinet, enter another microscopist's home, and later become available to collective use again?**

This is stronger than opening with postal breakage because it immediately establishes the article's distinctive institutional problem.

## 3. Historiographic position — three literatures, none sufficient alone

### A. Postal microscopy / knowledge infrastructure

Beiermann establishes:
- safe transmission;
- postal organisation;
- packaging;
- breakage and mending;
- material durability;
- standardisation/interoperability;
- British and American postal societies.

Therefore postal movement is a control, not the article's novelty.

### B. Instrument mobility / maintenance

Schaffer, Schickore and Baker establish:
- instruments fail in use;
- repair/maintenance are constitutive practices;
- movement impairs instruments;
- material sameness is not a simple basis of instrument identity.

Therefore `repair changes object identity` is not the article's novelty.

### C. Mobile collections / temporary loans

Driver, Nesbitt, Cornish and related work establish:
- museums are not static endpoints;
- collections are distributed, exchanged and re-mobilised;
- circulation is fundamental to collection history.

Swinney's Edinburgh Museum study is especially close:
- from 1865 a `Register of Specimens on Loan` documented a collection process based on ephemerality rather than permanent accumulation;
- he explicitly calls this `temporary collecting`.

South Kensington/V&A circulation history establishes outward museum loans and circulating collections.
Gooday, King and Spurna show the 1876 Special Loan Collection of Scientific Apparatus as an immense temporary assemblage, with most borrowed instruments later returned to exhibitors.

Therefore neither temporary custody nor return is a novelty by itself.

## 4. Empirical conjunction that remains distinctive

The microscope slide sits at the intersection of these literatures because it is simultaneously:
- a prepared scientific object;
- a detachable instrument component used with microscopes owned by different people;
- a labelled/catalogued collection object;
- small enough for repeated individual lending;
- capable of being remounted onto a different carrier;
- capable of retaining or losing scientific identity independently of the original glass;
- embedded in cabinet, catalogue and comparative-series relations.

The article therefore asks what happens when **a collection object is also a repeatedly mobile instrument component**.

The key scale is not a travelling exhibition or an incoming museum loan. It is the recurrent transaction of a society-owned preparation moving into individual temporary custody and back.

## 5. Analytical sequence

### Operation 1 — make the object compatible

**Richardson 1862**
Imported German preparations could be remounted because their original carriers did not fit a British cabinet/slide geometry.

Payoff:
Preparation identity could survive carrier substitution.

Prior scholarship already knows the episode; use it as a premise, not a discovery claim.

### Operation 2 — make circulation procedural

**Butterworth 1865**
Oldham-area circulating cabinet:
- one tray per member;
- six objects per tray;
- elastic restraints and raised protective beads;
- purpose-built box;
- one-week custody;
- each member replaces the contents of their own tray each cycle;
- memorandum book + rules + stamp/carriage finance travel with cabinet.

Payoff:
Mobility was designed as a sequence of custody, replacement and documentation before the Postal Microscopical Society.

Guard:
Butterworth says `objects`; do not silently call every item a glass slide.

### Operation 3 — make a private collection lendable

**Bolton 1880–87**
External donations and Redmayne's posthumous private collection enter Society custody.

The 1881 transformation:
`whole private collection`
→ `President/Secretary selection`
→ `72 selected Redmayne slides + 48 additional diatom slides`
→ `re-arranged Society cabinet`
→ `slides lent to members`.

Archive test:
`FZ/14/3/1` should expose actual borrower–object–time transactions.

The 1898 catalogue provides a later institutional order.

Central empirical question:
Can the same slide/address be followed through repeated loans and later catalogue persistence?

### Operation 4 — maintain a large institutional address system

**Royal Microscopical Society / Oxford**
Public journals show recurring `presented / donated / received / exhibited` preparations.
Internal RMS minutes, council minutes, ledger, cash books, slide register and donation catalogue can distinguish temporary encounter from permanent custody.

The surviving RMS cabinet/HSM database makes long-term address translation testable.

Redmayne supplies a concrete crosswalk:
- 1877 RMS directly recorded his *Micro-photographs from the Diatomaceae* as an author donation to Library and Cabinet;
- a matching Redmayne volume survives at HSM as RMS Collection `[1978-665]`;
- same-copy accession continuity remains the exact archival test.

Payoff:
Oxford tests how one large society turned incoming preparations/works into durable, retrievable institutional addresses.

### Operation 5 — make circulating objects intellectually usable

**Quekett Microscopical Club**
By 1879:
- >2,000 slides;
- slides freely circulated;
- cabinet functioned as a reference collection;
- President explicitly warned that the finished slide did not automatically transmit the maker's knowledge of origin, relations and crude state.

Payoff:
Physical lending alone did not complete scientific transmission. Names, series, books and documentary relations made a mobile preparation usable as reference evidence.

## 6. What the article is now *not* about

Not:
- a national survey of slide societies;
- a history of the post;
- the first history of museum loans;
- safe packaging;
- a history of broken slides;
- a generic argument that collections move;
- a generic argument that repair changes identity;
- a generic material-culture history of glass.

The article is about **how repeated custody changes were made compatible with continued collective access to prepared microscopic objects**.

## 7. `Return` must be treated as an operation, not an endpoint

Avoid saying that return/re-entry is historiographically unprecedented.

Use it instead as the empirical focus of the Bolton records.

A successful transaction is not simply:
`cabinet → borrower → cabinet`.

It requires at minimum:
- an identifiable object or number leaves;
- responsibility/custody is assigned;
- the object returns or its non-return is noticed;
- its institutional address remains usable or is updated;
- another user can subsequently retrieve it.

This is why the lending register and later catalogue matter together.

If a slide returns physically but loses its number/label/series relation, the institution may recover material without recovering the same working collection object.

## 8. `Retrievability` — useful but not proprietary vocabulary

Modern collections-management literature uses `retrievability` for the capacity to locate objects and information through cataloguing/location systems.

The article may use the ordinary word because it accurately describes the historical operation being tested, but must not present `retrievability` as a newly invented theoretical concept.

Preferred historical formulation:
**return to collective use** or **re-entry into collection order**.

These phrases describe operations rather than announce a theory.

## 9. Revised article architecture

### Opening — Bolton 1881: selecting a lendable collection
A widow hands over an entire private collection; two society officers decide what becomes institutionally useful; the cabinet is re-arranged; members borrow the slides.

### Section I — before the loan register: compatibility and circulation procedures, 1862–73
Richardson remounting → Butterworth circulating cabinet → Manchester/Armstrong pre-PMS sequence → PMS only as formalised control.

Question:
How did microscopists make prepared objects capable of moving between users and material settings?

### Section II — Bolton: a cabinet in temporary custody, 1880–98
Redmayne transfer → selection/re-arrangement → lending register → 1898 catalogue.

This is the empirical centre if `FZ/14/3/1` is rich.

Question:
How did individual preparations repeatedly leave and return without ceasing to belong to a collective working collection?

### Section III — Oxford RMS: acquisition, institutional address and survival
Dated donation/exhibition events → internal custody categories → ledger/register/catalogue → current cabinet/database where crosswalks close.

Redmayne `[1978-665]` supplies one bounded material thread.

Question:
How did a large society translate events of receipt/exhibition into durable institutional addresses?

### Section IV — Quekett: circulation and the limits of the finished object
Mature freely circulating cabinet + reference authority + actor-recognised loss of maker knowledge.

Question:
What documentation and classification had to travel with—or be available around—the preparation for successive users to learn from it?

### Conclusion — from circulation to reusable custody
The significance of movement lies not only in transmission outward. Scientific societies had to make prepared objects capable of successive departures and returns while retaining enough material and documentary identity for further use.

## 10. Minimum thesis after historiographic stress test

Already safe as a research proposition:

**Nineteenth-century microscopical societies could organise slide collections as working systems of temporary custody rather than passive stores. Their preparations were selected, re-arranged, lent and returned through documentary and cabinet systems designed to keep them available to successive users.**

The word `designed` should be softened if the archive reveals ad hoc rather than formal procedure.

## 11. Stronger thesis — archive dependent

If Bolton records show stable identifiers/repeat borrowing and Oxford provides compatible custody/register evidence:

**The scientific usefulness of a society slide depended not only on preparation quality or safe movement, but on institutional practices that restored a mobile object to collective order after each change of custodian.**

## 12. Strongest thesis — high evidence threshold

Only if remounting/replacement/loss can be connected to later use in at least two society contexts:

**Microscope preparations became maintainable instrument components because societies could preserve or translate their working identities across changes of carrier and custodian.**

Do not use as a settled grant conclusion.

## 13. Grant-facing formulation

The grant should remain empirically modest:

**The project examines how nineteenth-century microscopical societies managed prepared slides as working collections: how preparations were selected, catalogued, lent, returned, re-arranged and, where necessary, remounted or replaced.**

Archive justification follows directly:
- Bolton gives transaction-level lending records;
- Oxford gives governance/financial/register records plus a surviving RMS collection;
- Quekett supplies a self-funded London comparison;
- Manchester tests the prehistory and instrument-maker connection.

# SIS 2027 — historiography and argument boundary v2

Updated: 2026-09-13
Status: REFINED BOUNDARY / REPAIR-IDENTITY NOVELTY REJECTED

## 1. Two novelty claims are now unavailable

### A. `Transport shaped slide materials, packaging and durability`

Unavailable because Lea Beiermann's work on American and British postal microscopy already establishes postal circuits as knowledge infrastructures and documents packing/forwarding rules, breakage, liability, parcel-post reform, materials, mounting durability, box design and slide/box standardisation.

Beiermann's 2021 article also already records repair inside postal circulation: in 1885 an American Postal Microscopical Club member received thirteen boxes containing **one mended slide and one slide damaged beyond repair**, while 1886 began with further broken slides. Therefore `slides were repaired after postal damage` is also **not** a novelty claim.

What remains open is whether **ordinary scientific societies and pre-PMS circulating cabinets** made repair, remounting, replacement and return-to-use into institutional work outside an explicitly postal club designed around slide circulation.

### B. `Repair asks whether an instrument remains the same object`

Also unavailable as a generic novelty claim.

Liba Taub's introduction to the 2011 *Isis* focus `Reengaging with Instruments` explicitly summarizes Simon Schaffer's repair intervention as raising the identity problem: whether an instrument remains the same object through repeated maintenance, adjustment, polishing and refitting.

Simon Schaffer, `Easily Cracked: Scientific Instruments in States of Disrepair`, *Isis* 102.4 (2011), 706–17, makes failure, repair, reuse and planned maintenance central to instrument history. His notes directly cite Jutta Schickore's 2001 article on microscopy as an example of instrument users acquiring repair/maintenance work.

Jutta Schickore, `Ever-Present Impediments: Exploring Instruments and Methods of Microscopy`, *Perspectives on Science* 9.2 (2001), 126–46, already treats microscopy as a practice structured by imperfect instruments and methods.

Alexi Baker, `“Precision,” “Perfection,” and the Reality of British Scientific Instruments on the Move During the 18th Century`, *Material Culture Review* 74–75 (2012), 14–29, likewise makes movement, impairment, mending and adaptation part of scientific-instrument use rather than external logistics.

Therefore the SIS project cannot claim to discover either `mobility matters to instruments` or `repair destabilises object identity`.

## 2. The narrower empirical difference

The microscope preparation creates a historically specific version of the repair/mobility problem because it is:

- **detachable from the microscope**;
- small enough to circulate independently in batches;
- simultaneously specimen, mounting operation, labelled object and instrument component;
- divisible into carrier, cover, mountant/cell, specimen and documentary address;
- capable of surviving scientifically after substitution of one material component;
- capable of failing scientifically while apparently robust material components survive;
- temporarily held by borrowers, correspondents or scientific societies rather than remaining with one instrument user or maker;
- often integrated into cabinets, publication sequences or registers whose addresses can outlive individual glass preparations.

The project therefore asks not the generic question `is it still the same instrument after repair?`, but an empirical one:

**Which parts of a mobile microscope preparation did nineteenth-century microscopists and societies treat as replaceable, and which relations had to remain intact for the preparation to return to scientific use?**

## 3. Why Richardson 1862 changes the project — and what is already known

B. Wills Richardson, `Transparent Carmine Injections`, *Quarterly Journal of Microscopical Science*, new series 2 (1862), p. 118, supplies the clearest British/Irish pre-postal example now controlled.

Richardson states that he had remounted all transparent carmine injections of that kind in his collection. A broad German carrier could sometimes be cut down with a diamond, but he preferred remounting, especially when it was shorter than three inches.

This is not repair after accidental breakage. It is **carrier substitution for interoperability**.

The preparation enters a different cabinet/use ecology by giving up the original glass support.

The case therefore establishes:

`scientific preparation identity ≠ original glass carrier`.

That distinction should become a premise generated from historical practice, not from philosophy.

### Factual precedent: Howard Lynk 2012

The historical facts of the imported German transparent injections and their inconvenient dimensions are **not newly discovered here**.

Howard Lynk, `The 1860s Smith, Beck & Beck transparent injections`, *Quekett Journal of Microscopy* 41 (2012), 701–12, reconstructs the German injections, identifies Karl Thiersch as their maker, discusses their sale by Smith, Beck & Beck, and explicitly notes contemporary complaints about their `clumsy size`. Lynk records suggested remedies ranging from complete remounting to cutting slides down with a diamond, and observes that Beck eventually supplied examples on standard 3 × 1 inch slips.

Therefore the SIS/article contribution cannot be `Richardson remounted German slides` or `German slides were non-standard`.

The interpretive difference is narrower:
- Lynk reconstructs maker/product/market history and the transition in physical format;
- this project uses Richardson's action to ask **what object persisted when the carrier changed**, and how such carrier substitution became connected to mobility, temporary custody, cabinet compatibility and institutional maintenance.

This is an interpretive and archival extension of a known historical episode, not a priority claim over the episode itself.

## 4. Historical vocabulary is analytically useful

The current primary cases distinguish several nouns that should not be silently treated as synonyms:

- `slide`;
- `glass slip`;
- `cover glass`;
- `object`;
- `preparation`;
- `cabinet`;
- `box`;
- `number / address / register entry`.

The postal-damage report in *The Northern Microscopist* is especially diagnostic because the glass slip can remain while the `object` disappears. Richardson gives the inverse: the original carrier can disappear while the preparation is retained through remounting.

The paper should therefore avoid defining `slide` ontologically at the outset. It should reconstruct what the relevant working unit was in different practices.

## 5. Relation to the main repository's `addressability` article

The main slide repository has already reached a broader synthesis:
microscope preparations became durable knowledge objects through systems of addresses; labels, numbers, cabinets, catalogues, publication references, microscope coordinates and institutional identifiers could preserve or translate identity across replacement, remounting and recataloguing.

This SIS satellite must remain distinct.

### Main-repository question
How did preparations remain historically and scientifically addressable across material and institutional transformations?

### SIS satellite question
How did **movement, temporary custody and incompatibility generate the need for those material interventions**, and how were altered preparations returned to use?

The satellite needs causality from mobility/custody, not merely evidence that identity survived a later material change.

## 6. Current empirical sequence

### Richardson 1862 — incompatibility
Imported preparations do not fit a local cabinet geometry; the carrier is replaced while preparation identity persists.

### Butterworth 1865 — designed replacement
A circulating cabinet has persistent trays/protocols but periodically changing contents under one-week custody.

### Manchester 1866/69 — circulating cabinet becomes local social/technical practice
T. Armstrong on Deansgate serves as a cabinet contact in 1866; by 1869 a named Manchester Circulating Microscopic Cabinet Society coordinates slide preparation and exhibition. The likely identity of Armstrong with the Deansgate optical-instrument maker is strong but still awaits direct closure.

### 1871 Pattern Post complaint — infrastructure can stop a scientific circulation
An unnamed microscopical society describes postal restrictions as a `death blow` because prepared subjects can no longer be sent.

### Postal Microscopical Society — explicit administrative control
Packing, liability, detention, insurance, parcel-post reform, mending and preparation durability make the postal society a **control case rather than a novelty site**.

### Cole 1882–84 — fixed address with replaceable contents
The surviving customized cabinet preserves a publication sequence despite subject/preparation substitutions; however, the agency/date of substitution are currently unknown.

### Oxford RMS archive — decision layer still missing
Potentially supplies the institutional records needed to connect movement/custody to expenditure, repair, replacement, registration and renewed use.

## 7. Three levels of claim

### Secure now
`Victorian microscopists did not consistently treat the original glass carrier as the indispensable identity-bearing part of a preparation.`

Evidence:
Richardson remounting; inverse postal-loss case; Pacini comparator; surviving Cole substitution architecture.

### Research hypothesis
`Mobility and temporary custody made component substitution an institutional rather than merely individual problem outside explicitly postal organisations.`

Needs:
Oxford / Manchester / Bolton / Quekett archival records assigning responsibility, cost, record changes or return-to-use decisions.

### Strongest possible conclusion — not yet earned
`Scientific societies stabilized mobile preparations by preserving working identity across replaceable carriers, mounts and custodians.`

Do not use as a settled finding until archival chains demonstrate both intervention and renewed scientific use.

## 8. What would count as a genuinely new archival chain

Ideal example:

`named slide/preparation or bounded collection`
→ `loan / circulation / incompatible format / transit event`
→ `damage or inability to use/store`
→ `council/custodian decision`
→ `cost or labour for remounting/replacement`
→ `same register/catalogue identity retained or translated`
→ `returned to cabinet / loan / teaching / comparison`.

A weaker but still useful chain can omit one step if object identity and return-to-use remain explicit.

A reference to `repair` with no object identity or later use is not enough.

## 9. Relation to `Using Up Old Slides`

The Orléans proposal asks what old glass could still do. Its historical unit is glass and its changing affordances: cleaning, scraping, cutting, revarnishing, remounting, salvage and postal damage.

The SIS/article project should instead ask:
**what made a preparation continue to count and work when the glass did not?**

This makes the two outputs complementary rather than redundant:

- Orléans: second lives of glass;
- SIS/article: continuity of preparations across movement and material substitution.

## 10. Preferred framing after the stress test

### Preferred working question
**What had to remain—and what could be replaced—for a microscope preparation to remain scientifically usable while moving through nineteenth-century cabinets, societies and postal systems?**

### Compact grant question
**How did nineteenth-century microscopists keep microscope preparations usable as they were remounted, circulated, damaged, returned and re-entered into scientific collections?**

### Avoid
- `safe transmission`;
- `transport shaped affordances`;
- `repair reveals object identity`;
- `slides circulated through networks`;
- `standardisation enabled exchange`;
- `postal breakage led microscopists to mend slides`.

Each is either too close to existing literature or too generic to justify the archival programme.

## 11. Provisional title candidates

1. **Keeping Preparations in Use: Remounting, Custody, and the Mobile Microscope Slide**
2. **What Remained of the Slide? Mobility and Material Substitution in Victorian Microscopy**
3. **Changing the Glass: Microscope Preparations, Mobility, and Maintenance in Nineteenth-Century Britain**
4. **After Remounting: Custody and the Working Identity of Microscope Preparations**

Current preference for a scholarly article is **Changing the Glass** because it begins from a concrete operation rather than an abstract concept. For an SIS application, **Keeping Preparations in Use** is more immediately legible.

## 12. Falsification

If the archives yield no nineteenth-century chain linking mobility/custody to intervention and renewed use outside explicitly postal societies, do not force this argument.

Fall-back article:
a tightly bounded history of **pre-PMS circulating cabinets in north-west England, 1865–73**, with Butterworth, Armstrong/Manchester, Pattern Post and the emergence of the PMS.

That fallback remains historically coherent and potentially publishable even if the institutional-maintenance hypothesis fails.

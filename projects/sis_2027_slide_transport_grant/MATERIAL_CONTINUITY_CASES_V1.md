# Material continuity cases v1

Updated: 2026-09-13
Status: EVIDENCE MATRIX / TOPIC COMPRESSION

## 1. Why this matrix exists

The SIS project should not treat every damaged or repaired microscope slide as the same historical problem. The useful distinction is between **what physically changes** and **what actors continue to treat as the scientifically relevant object**.

Working components:

- `glass slip / carrier`;
- `cover glass`;
- `mountant / cell / cement`;
- `specimen / prepared object`;
- `label / maker / taxonomic name`;
- `publication or series address`;
- `cabinet slot / institutional register address`;
- `box / tray / circulation enclosure`;
- `custodian / borrower / society responsibility`.

The project advances only when a case shows that these components can have different histories.

## 2. Richardson / German transparent carmine injections, 1862 — carrier substitution for compatibility

Primary source:
B. Wills Richardson, `Transparent Carmine Injections`, *Quarterly Journal of Microscopical Science*, new series 2 (1862), p. 118.

Direct primary text establishes that Richardson had no difficulty remounting the transparent carmine injections and had done so with **all of that kind in his collection**. He noted that a broad German slide of adequate length could be cut with a diamond to the necessary dimensions, but he preferred remounting, particularly when the German slide was shorter than three inches.

Historical context:
The injections were German preparations retailed in Britain by Smith, Beck & Beck; surviving scholarship identifies Karl Thiersch as the preparer of the series. The practical problem was incompatibility between non-standard/broad imported carriers and English cabinet/slide geometry.

Component history:
- specimen/preparation: **retained**;
- original glass carrier: **discardable / replaceable**;
- new glass carrier: **substituted**;
- cabinet compatibility: **gained**;
- preparation identity: **treated as persisting through remounting**.

Why it matters:
This is a direct nineteenth-century example in which the working scientific preparation is not identical with its original glass. Material substitution is performed in order to make the preparation fit a local storage/use infrastructure.

Interpretive ceiling:
Do not claim that every remounted Richardson slide can now be identified in a surviving collection. The primary source proves his practice at collection level, not an item-level provenance chain.

Potential object bridge:
Richardson later donated a box of slides to the Quekett Microscopical Club on 23 November 1867. Current specialist documentation also identifies Richardson slides in the present QMC collection. No currently controlled source proves that a surviving QMC Richardson slide is one of the specific German injections he remounted in 1862.

## 3. Butterworth / Oldham circulating cabinet, 1865 — contents designed to be replaced

Primary source:
John Butterworth, `Circulating Cabinet of Objects`, *Hardwicke's Science-Gossip* 1 (1865), p. 237.

Controlled mechanism:
- fixed tray allocation by member;
- six objects per tray;
- physical restraint by elastic bands and raised beads;
- purpose-made outer box;
- one-week custody;
- each member changes the objects on their tray when the cabinet returns;
- travelling memorandum-book;
- stamp-case and carriage finance.

Component history:
- cabinet/tray/circulation route: **persistent**;
- individual contents: **periodically replaced by design**;
- documentary/custody protocol: **persistent**.

Why it matters:
Replacement here is not an emergency repair after breakage. It is built into the life cycle of a circulating scientific collection. The system remains the same circulating cabinet while its material contents change.

Guard:
Butterworth writes `objects`; do not call every item a glass slide without item-level evidence.

## 4. Northern Microscopist postal-damage report, c.1880/81 — carrier survival without scientific object survival

Primary source family:
*The Microscopical News and Northern Microscopist* / `The Northern Microscopist`, controlled through indexed OCR and the Orléans proposal source note.

Historical statement:
For dry-mounted slides sent through the post, damage could leave `nothing but the glass slip`; occasionally the cover glass also survived, while the `object` was commonly lost or overlooked.

Component history:
- glass slip: **can survive**;
- cover glass: **may survive**;
- specimen/object: **lost**;
- preparation as useful scientific object: **fails despite surviving glass**.

Why it matters:
This is the inverse of Richardson. Richardson preserves the preparation by replacing glass; postal damage can preserve glass while destroying the preparation. Together, the two cases make it impossible to equate scientific continuity with continuity of the glass slip.

Source-control note:
The large digitized PDF could not be opened reliably through the present web interface for screenshot verification. Before formal quotation in publication, recover the printed page directly. The wording is also preserved in the submitted Orléans proposal.

## 5. R. H. Ward posting-box, 1878 — failure redistributed from enclosure to supported object

Primary British report:
`A New Posting-box for Slides`, *Hardwicke's Science-Gossip* 14 (1878), p. 87, reporting R. H. Ward's American posting-box design.

Controlled point:
Slides could arrive shattered while the external box remained sound. Ward attributed the failure to the inertia of the slide itself and changed how the slide was supported, redistributing support along the glass rather than assuming an intact box guaranteed an intact slide.

Component history:
- outer box: **survives**;
- glass slide: **can fail internally**;
- support geometry: **redesigned**.

Why it matters:
The relevant instrument system includes relations between glass and support, not enclosure alone. Movement makes a mechanical relation visible that stationary use could conceal.

Geography guard:
Ward is an American comparator reported in British print. Do not present this as a British institutional case.

## 6. Arthur C. Cole / *Studies in Microscopical Science*, 1882–84 — publication address survives substitution

Repository authority:
`docs/COLE_TOOVEYS_FULLRES_AUDIT_2026-08-10.md` and associated machine-readable audits.

Controlled surviving cabinet:
- purpose-built 1–52 publication architecture;
- printed index split across interleaved Animal / Botanical tracks;
- 46 slides visibly retain printed issue numbers matching slots;
- several slots now hold subject substitutions;
- slot 21 holds an Amos Topping `Section of Human Lung` replacing the expected preparation but preserving the publication address;
- fixed slot numbers are physically written into the wooden cabinet.

Component history:
- publication sequence / slot address: **persistent**;
- some original preparations: **lost/replaced/misassigned**;
- substitute preparations: **inserted into existing address architecture**;
- cabinet remains legible as a material publication despite imperfect original-object survival.

Why it matters:
The cabinet shows maintenance through **address persistence**, but the date and agency of substitutions are not currently known. It therefore demonstrates a surviving outcome, not yet a nineteenth-century repair decision.

SIS relation:
Use as a material comparator. The Oxford archive search should seek historical records that expose the decision process absent from the surviving Cole cabinet: who replaced, recorded or authorized a substitution and why.

## 7. Pacini / Florence cholera preparation — direct object identity through new glass

Repository authority:
`docs/PACINI_FLORENCE_SLIDE_PUBLICATION_REMOUNTING_UPGRADE_2026-08-11.md`.

Controlled museum/object-label chain:
A surviving Pacini cholera preparation is identified on its label with a specific case and page in the 1854 *Gazzetta medica di Firenze*. The same historical label records that the preparation was **remounted in new glass seven years after it had been made**.

Component history:
- preparation/specimen identity: **persistent**;
- glass carrier: **replaced**;
- case/publication address: **persistent**;
- historical object legibility: **maintained across remounting**.

Why it matters:
This is the cleanest currently controlled object-level proof that a nineteenth-century microscopic preparation could change its glass carrier while remaining the same identified scientific preparation.

Geography/use guard:
Pacini is an Italian comparator, not evidence for British practice. It defines the kind of archival/object chain the British project should try to recover.

## 8. Five distinct maintenance states now visible

### State A — carrier replaced, preparation preserved
Richardson 1862; Pacini comparator.

### State B — carrier survives, preparation lost
Northern Microscopist postal failure.

### State C — enclosure survives, carrier fails
Ward posting-box comparator.

### State D — fixed system/address survives, contents replaced
Butterworth cabinet as routine replacement mechanism; Cole cabinet as surviving publication-address substitution.

### State E — material object survives but generative/context knowledge does not automatically travel
Quekett Microscopical Club 1879, already controlled elsewhere in the repository.

These states should not be collapsed into `fragility` or `repair`.

## 9. British archival test generated by the matrix

Oxford RMS / Manchester / Bolton records should be read for events that can be coded as:

`DATE / ACTOR / PREPARATION OR COLLECTION / COMPONENT AFFECTED / MATERIAL STATE BEFORE / INTERVENTION / COMPONENT RETAINED / COMPONENT REPLACED / DOCUMENTARY ADDRESS BEFORE / ADDRESS AFTER / RESPONSIBLE CUSTODIAN / COST / RETURN-TO-USE EVIDENCE`.

The strongest archival find would be a nineteenth-century case where an institution explicitly records that:
1. a preparation or collection component was damaged/incompatible;
2. one material support was replaced or altered;
3. the object retained a catalogue/register/experimental identity;
4. it returned to teaching, comparison, lending, reference or display use.

## 10. Relation to the main repository argument

The main microscope-slide core already argues that addresses can preserve or translate object identity across remounting, replacement and recataloguing. This satellite project must not merely repackage that global synthesis.

Distinct SIS/article contribution:
**temporary mobility and custody create the interventions.**

The causal sequence to test is therefore:

`movement / incompatibility / circulation`
→ `material or documentary failure`
→ `decision under temporary or institutional custody`
→ `component-specific intervention`
→ `return to scientific use`.

If archival work cannot establish this causal sequence beyond explicitly postal societies, the project should narrow to a pre-PMS circulating-cabinet history rather than duplicate the main `addressability` article.

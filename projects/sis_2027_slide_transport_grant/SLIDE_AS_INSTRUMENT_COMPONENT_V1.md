# SIS 2027 — microscope preparations as detachable working components v1

Updated: 2026-09-13
Status: HISTORICAL CATEGORY / SIS-REMIT NOTE — AVOID ONTOLOGICAL OVERCLAIM

## 1. Problem

For the SIS project, do not solve relevance by asserting abstractly that `a microscope slide is a scientific instrument`.

That claim is unnecessarily strong and historically unstable: a preparation can also be specimen, collection object, commodity, publication component and teaching object.

A more historically defensible formulation is:

**prepared microscope slides were detachable working components within nineteenth-century microscope practice and instrument/apparatus economies.**

Their scientific usefulness depended on relations with microscope stands, stages, illumination, objectives, cabinets, labels and users. Precisely because they were detachable, they could move independently between instruments and custodians.

## 2. Commercial/instrument-trade classification

### South Kensington educational catalogues, 1857 / 1867

J. Parkes & Son appears under combined catalogue language for **microscopes and microscopic preparations**.

Use:
preparations were presented in the same educational/instrument supply ecology as microscopes rather than as a wholly separate natural-history category.

### Optical catalogue advertised in *Scientific American*, 1879

The optical-instrument catalogue's second part includes:
- spectacles / lenses / telescopes / stereoscopes etc.;
- **microscopes**;
- **microscopic preparations**;
- books on optics and microscopy.

Use:
prepared objects belong inside an optical-instrument dealer's catalogue architecture.

### R. & J. Beck advertisement, University of Pennsylvania catalogue, 1883–84

The advertisement offers:
- microscopes;
- **accessories**;
- **mounting materials**;
- **prepared objects**;
- apparatus for photomicrography.

Use:
this is especially useful because it places the chain from apparatus → preparation materials → finished prepared objects in one commercial instrument environment.

### Ross & Co., JRMS advertisement, 1890

The advertisement places in one sequence:
`MICROSCOPES, OBJECT-GLASSES, APPARATUS, MICROSCOPIC PREPARATIONS`.

Use:
late-Victorian instrument makers themselves did not organize preparations as a domain unrelated to microscopes/apparatus.

## 3. Complete microscope kit / storage architecture

### Buron optician catalogue, Paris, 1844 — comparator

A universal microscope's base contained drawers for:
- dismantled microscope;
- oculars;
- lenses;
- accessories;
- **collections of prepared objects**.

The catalogue's accessory list also directly includes:
`Prepared objects, transparent and opaque`.

This is not British evidence and should not carry the British claim alone. It is a strong comparator showing that prepared objects could be materially integrated into the architecture and accessory inventory of a microscope outfit.

Historical payoff:
prepared objects can be physically separable from the optical stand while still belonging to the working instrument ensemble.

## 4. Preparation properties alter instrument configuration

William B. Carpenter's *The Microscope and Its Revelations* discusses `Accessory Apparatus` and explains illumination arrangements in relation to different objects/preparations. In the discussion of opaque/semi-transparent objects, injected preparations appear as cases for which a bull's-eye condenser is particularly desirable.

Historical payoff:
what the preparation materially is affects how the microscope must be configured and illuminated. The preparation is not passive content placed after the instrument has already been defined; it participates in the working optical arrangement.

## 5. Mobility makes the component relation visible

The SIS project should exploit the detachable character rather than settle an ontology.

A preparation can:
- leave one microscope while the microscope remains behind;
- enter another user's microscope;
- fail to fit another cabinet or stage ecology and be remounted;
- be selected from a private collection into a society cabinet;
- be lent to a member who owns/controls a different microscope;
- return to a collective cabinet and become available for another instrument-user;
- retain specimen/preparation identity while its carrier or institutional address changes.

This produces a historically specific instrument problem:

**How did scientific societies manage an instrument component whose scientific use repeatedly crossed the boundaries of the instrument with which it was used?**

## 6. Bolton as the clearest SIS-remit case

Bolton is especially useful because contemporary evidence combines:
- a local society whose members owned/purchased microscopes and objectives from major makers;
- a Society-owned preparation cabinet;
- re-arrangement and selection of slides;
- explicit lending of Society slides to members;
- a surviving 1882–87 lending register.

Potential historical relation:

`Society-owned prepared component`
→ `temporary member custody`
→ `member-controlled microscope / optical configuration`
→ `return to Society cabinet`.

The first and last links are already strongly evidenced; the exact borrower–instrument link should be made only where a member's microscope ownership/use is directly documented.

## 7. Oxford as a second instrument ecology

Oxford RMS supplies a different scale:
- microscopes/apparatus catalogue and donations records;
- slide catalogues/registers;
- a very large surviving RMS slide cabinet;
- minutes/accounts for collection governance;
- modern HSM collection hierarchy.

The internal separation or conjunction of `microscopes / apparatus / slides / donations` is itself evidence to recover rather than prejudge.

Question:
How did RMS administrative categories distinguish or connect the optical instrument, prepared object and collection address?

## 8. Preferred language for SIS application

Strong and safe:

**Microscope slides were detachable working components of nineteenth-century microscopy. Instrument makers and educational catalogues supplied preparations alongside microscopes, objectives, mounting materials and accessory apparatus; microscopists then moved those preparations between instruments, cabinets and users.**

Then state the project's question:

**The project examines how scientific societies kept these mobile components usable and retrievable as they entered collective cabinets, passed into temporary member custody, and returned for further use.**

## 9. Avoid

Avoid:
- `slides are instruments, full stop`;
- `the slide is merely a specimen`;
- `slide = microscope`;
- claiming that every preparation was conceived historically as `apparatus`;
- using modern museum object-type classification as proof of nineteenth-century ontology.

The point is relational and operational, not taxonomic.

## 10. Historiographic payoff

This formulation links two literatures without collapsing them:
- history of scientific instruments: configuration, use, maintenance, mobility;
- history of collections/specimens: custody, cataloguing, provenance, lending.

Microscope preparations are useful precisely because they cross the boundary:
**a collection object that must repeatedly function as part of an instrument practice.**

That conjunction, not a universal definition of `instrument`, should justify the SIS project.

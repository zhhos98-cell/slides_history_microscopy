# SIS 2027 — primary-source control v1

Updated: 2026-09-13
Status: CONTROLLED SOURCE MAP / DO NOT PROMOTE PARTIAL ROUTES

Purpose: separate directly controlled nineteenth-century sources from claims currently known only through later scholarship. The project should use the Postal Microscopical Society (PMS) as a control case, not claim its transport system as a new discovery.

## 1. 1871 British postal restriction — prepared microscopical subjects blocked

**Source:** House of Commons debate, `Post Office—(Small Parcels)`, 14 March 1871, Hansard.

Controlled fact:
A speaker reported receiving a communication from an unnamed microscopical society stating that new restrictions on the Pattern Post had given the society its `death blow`, because its prepared subjects could no longer be sent through the post.

What this supports:
- postal regulation could determine whether an existing microscopical circulation practice was viable;
- movement of prepared subjects predates the foundation of the Postal Microscopical Society in 1873.

Evidence ceiling:
- the society is not identified in the Hansard passage presently controlled;
- do not identify it with Quekett, a Manchester group, or any other society without independent evidence;
- the passage proves blockage of circulation, not repair or replacement after damage.

Route:
https://hansard.parliament.uk/commons/1871-03-14/debates/96426daf-b212-408d-90c6-0ff04308905f/PostOffice%E2%80%94%28SmallParcels%29

## 2. 1873 — designed postal cabinet at the founding moment

**Source:** Alfred Atkinson, `Microscopic Postal Cabinets`, *Hardwicke's Science-Gossip* 9 (1873): 111.

Controlled through later reproduction and Beiermann's exact citation:
Atkinson proposed twelve correspondents, a manager, and a box holding at least twelve microscopic slides, explicitly requiring that the box be as light as compatible with safe postal transmission. Members would add slides and notes.

What this supports:
- circulation requirements entered the scheme at the moment of institutional design;
- the box was not secondary packaging added after the scientific object was complete.

Use:
baseline only. Beiermann has already integrated this into a knowledge-infrastructure history.

## 3. 1880 Seventh Annual Meeting — British PMS breakage and packing rules

**Source:** `Postal Microscopical Society.—The Seventh Annual Meeting`, *Microscopical News and Northern Microscopist* 1 (1881): 41–42, reporting meeting of 30 September 1880.

Directly indexed primary text establishes:
- membership stood at 155;
- about 690 slides were in circulation;
- a register had been made of 631 slides;
- Rule 15 was printed in bold type and attached to the lid of every box;
- members nevertheless sometimes wrapped the black box in additional white paper or fixed an address in ways that ignored the rule;
- the report said few breakages had occurred and attributed accidents to insufficient attention to the rule.

The following continuation on p. 42 is also controlled from the same source family:
- travelling boxes required a tough cement;
- a cement adequate for a private cabinet might be inadequate under travel;
- zinc-white was criticized as too brittle;
- many dry-mounted slides came to grief in the post;
- in some failures only the glass slip, and sometimes the cover glass, could be saved while the object was lost or overlooked.

Historical use:
This is the clearest British primary baseline for **component-level failure**. A failed preparation could leave different material residues: slip, cover, object, mount. It therefore makes the empirical question `what exactly was preserved or replaced?` historically legitimate.

Novelty guard:
Do not present travel-induced material selection or breakage as a new finding. Beiermann has already made that move historiographically.

Primary route:
Internet Archive / Wikimedia scan, `The Microscopical news and northern microscopist; an illustrated journal of practical microscopy`, vol. 1.

## 4. 1882 PMS governance — rules, liability, and an irregularity subcommittee

### 4.1 Published rules

**Bibliographic control:**
Postal Microscopical Society, *Rules and Names and Addresses of Members*, supplement to vol. 1 of *Journal of the Postal Microscopical Society* (1882), 17 pp.

This exact bibliographic item is independently recorded in the 1883 *Journal of the Royal Microscopical Society* current-research bibliography.

### 4.2 What is currently controlled only through Beiermann

Beiermann's 2023 dissertation cites `Rules`, p. 5, for the statement that by 1882 Alfred Allen and other Bath members formed a subcommittee responsible for detention of boxes, damage, non-circulation of slides and other irregularities. She also states that the expanded rules governed handling, packaging and forwarding, assigned liability for breakage, and allowed optional insurance.

Status:
- existence/title/date/length of the 1882 rules supplement: **DIRECT BIBLIOGRAPHIC CONTROL**;
- wording and structure of p. 5 on subcommittee/liability/insurance: **SECONDARY-ROUTED / PAGE IMAGE OR OCR STILL REQUIRED**.

Do not quote the rules' wording as primary until the supplement itself is inspected.

## 5. 1887 J. W. Measures — breakage becoming less exceptional

**Source:** J. W. Measures, `Presidential Address`, *The Journal of the Postal Microscopical Society* 6 (1887): 1–7.

Exact citation recovered from Beiermann's dissertation.

Primary text is also independently indexed in the digitised 1887 journal and in an American contemporary report. The controlled points are:
- Measures discussed the Society as serving microscopists isolated from urban meetings and practical mounting instruction;
- he recalled frequent slide breakages in the Society's early period;
- he attributed the near-disappearance of breakages by 1887 to the introduction of the British parcel post in 1883 together with sturdy boxes;
- the address also treats detention of circulating boxes as an administrative problem, including members becoming unintended `box-stoppers` when they travelled without notifying the Secretary.

Historical use:
This is important **negative evidence** for a failure-centred paper. By the later 1880s, successful infrastructure could make catastrophic breakage less visible. Archive work should therefore not assume that a low number of breakage reports means mobility was historically irrelevant; routine design and administration may have absorbed the problem.

## 6. 1889 H. N. Lyon — travel as durability test

**Source route:** H. N. Lyon, `Cements, Varnishes, and Cells`, *The Journal of Microscopy and Natural Science* 8 (1889): 244–249.

Beiermann uses this source for a slide collector assessing the effect of approximately 15,000–20,000 miles of travel on slides.

Independent bibliographic confirmation:
The *Journal of the Royal Microscopical Society* 1889 summary bibliography records `Lyon, H. N.—Cements, Varnishes, and Cells`, while citing a version in *The Microscope* 9 (1889): 69–74. This may represent republication or parallel publication.

Status:
- author/title/year: **CONTROLLED**;
- JMNS 8 (1889): 244–249 as Beiermann's cited route: **STRONG SECONDARY ROUTE**;
- exact 15,000–20,000-mile wording and relation between JMNS and *The Microscope* versions: **PRIMARY PAGE CONTROL STILL OPEN**.

Do not build a novel claim on this until the two versions are compared.

## 7. Richard H. Ward — American technical comparator, not British institutional evidence

**Source:** `A New Posting-Box for Slides`, *Hardwicke's Science-Gossip* 14 (1878): 87 ff., reporting R. H. Ward's box.

Controlled point:
Ward described cases in which slides were found shattered while the containing box remained sound. This led him to attribute damage to the inertia of the slide itself and to alter internal support rather than merely strengthen the outside of the box.

Historical use:
- demonstrates a mechanical interpretation of failure in which the box can survive while the slide fails;
- useful for distinguishing external enclosure from internal support and moving mass.

Geography guard:
Richard H. Ward is an American microscopist. Publication in a British periodical makes this a transatlantic technical comparator, not a British institutional case.

## 8. Cole — delivery failure at the boundary of publication

**Repository authority:** `data/analysis/slide_as_publication_cole_v1.json`.

Controlled repository evidence:
Arthur C. Cole's *Studies in Microscopical Science* coupled letterpress, image and a prepared slide. Subscribers complained about material arriving crushed when postal delivery folded parcels into letter boxes; Cole asked that parcels be delivered at the door.

Historical use:
A damaged preparation could make a serial issue materially incomplete even where its print survived. The maintained object was therefore a publication/preparation assemblage, not simply a glass slip.

## 9. Quekett Microscopical Club — circulating custody outside PMS

**Repository authority:** `data/analysis/quekett_club_cabinet_circulation_authority_upgrade_v1.json`.

Controlled repository evidence:
- 1865 founding functions included exhibit/exchange and a comprehensive cabinet;
- 1868 evidence shows transatlantic exchange and rapid cabinet growth;
- by 1879 the cabinet held more than 2,000 slides and slides were freely circulated;
- the President explicitly distinguished the preparer's knowledge of origin/crude state from the first-time borrower's encounter with a finished slide.

Historical use:
This is currently the strongest nineteenth-century **ordinary scientific society** control showing a material lending regime outside the PMS.

Open mechanism:
No exact surviving nineteenth-century loan register, circulation box, breakage record or repair/replacement documentation is yet controlled. Do not infer these from the fact of free circulation.

## 10. Butterworth / Science-Gossip — replacement designed into a circulating cabinet

**Repository authority:** `data/analysis/wmc_science_gossip_periodical_operations_2026-09-10.md`.

Controlled 1865 source:
John Butterworth's `Circulating Cabinet of Objects` specifies trays, six objects per tray, elastic bands, raised beads, purpose-made box, one-week custody, replacement of an object on return, travelling memorandum-book, stamp-case and carriage finance.

Historical use:
The striking feature is not simply packing. **Replacement is part of the operating procedure.** Circulation therefore anticipates changing material membership while preserving the cabinet/circuit as the continuing system.

Evidence ceiling:
Butterworth says `objects`; do not rewrite the whole cabinet as microscope slides without object-specific evidence.

## 11. Source-derived chronological skeleton

1871 — postal rule change can make an existing prepared-subject circulation impossible.

1873 — the new postal cabinet is designed around a lightweight protective box and reciprocal slide/notes circulation.

1880/81 — PMS has hundreds of slides in circulation; packing rules, cement brittleness and dry-mount failure reveal different levels of material loss.

1882 — the Society formalises irregularity management in rules and Bath-based administration; liability/insurance become part of the system (exact p. 5 still to inspect).

1883 — British parcel post changes the transport environment.

1887 — Measures describes early frequent breakage as largely overcome by parcel post + sturdy boxes; delay/detention remains an administrative problem.

1889 — circulation is explicitly read as evidence about the durability of mounting materials (Lyon primary pages still to control).

The project should **not narrate this as its main discovery**. This chronology is the baseline against which ordinary scientific-society custody/maintenance will be tested.

## 12. What remains genuinely open

The project still lacks nineteenth-century direct evidence for the strongest desired chain:

`ordinary society-owned preparation → movement/loan → identifiable failure → component-level repair or replacement → register/cabinet identity retained or changed → preparation returned to scientific use`.

Oxford RMS, Manchester, Bolton, Bristol and self-funded London work are justified only insofar as they can test this chain.

One positive instance would sharpen the paper. Two institutionally independent instances would support a comparative argument. Absence across well-targeted archives would require narrowing the project back toward postal regimes rather than asserting a general British maintenance system.

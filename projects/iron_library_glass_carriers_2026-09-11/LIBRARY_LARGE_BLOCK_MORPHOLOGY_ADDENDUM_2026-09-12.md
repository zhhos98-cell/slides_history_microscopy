Several large internal printed environments can already be distinguished without keyword-selecting authors or imposing a microscopy/metallography question.

- metallurgy;

- history of science;
- GF history;
- economic/industrial history;
- industrial heritage;
- **product catalogues and company publications**;
- encyclopaedias.

Historical quantitative descriptions also document large travel, world-exhibition, materials/testing and geoscience blocks.

## 2. Product/company literature is a major Library block, not merely corporate-archive duplication

The current Library page explicitly names `Produktkataloge und Firmenschriften` among its thematic priorities.

The Library's own discussion of Valerian Gillar's 1899 `Preisbuch` explains why this material matters structurally:

- company publications, product catalogues and price lists are deliberately collected;
- they are grey literature, produced by firms rather than ordinary publishers/book trade;
- they were often ephemeral-use documents;
- some Library copies are exceptionally rare, with no other copy visible in publicly searchable library catalogues.

Therefore distinguish two evidence regimes:

- **LIBRARY company/product literature** = retrospectively and systematically collected printed market/product evidence from many firms;
- **GFA corporate/product records** = records generated/preserved through GF or acquired-company provenance.

They may overlap historically but are not the same collection logic.

## 3. World exhibitions form a clean nineteenth-century thematic corpus

The historical holdings census explicitly counts **95 publications on nineteenth-century world exhibitions**, some in catalogue form.

This is unusually useful for a collection-first map because:

- the date span is intrinsically nineteenth-century;
- the corpus is event-structured rather than author-structured;
- exhibition catalogues/reports can connect products, firms, materials, machinery, classification and visual display without requiring a preselected person;
- the Iron Library has also historically treated world exhibitions as an institutional research theme (e.g. the 1993 technology-history conference / Ferrum issue), but that later research history must not be confused with the original printed corpus.

Current rating:

- scale: MEDIUM/HIGH;
- nineteenth-century date density: VERY HIGH;
- seriality: MEDIUM (event sequence);
- provenance: HIGH as a thematic Library collection, LOW as common original provenance;
- onsite necessity: MEDIUM / title-specific.

## 4. Research-travel reports are coherent as genre, not as one provenance series

The historical census counts **123 reports by research travellers** across the collection's chronology. Named nineteenth-century examples include mining/geoscience travel literature by Hermann Wedding and Louis Agassiz.

This is a substantial genre/classification block, but current limitations are important:

- 123 is not a nineteenth-century-only count;
- exact nineteenth-century subset size is OPEN;
- travel reports do not form one archival provenance or continuous serial run;
- many printed travel reports may be remotely available elsewhere.

Therefore retain as a **medium-scale coherent Library genre block**, not yet as an onsite core.

## 5. Materials/testing and geoscience blocks remain structurally important even before topic selection

The 1996 historical whole-collection morphology independently shows:

- foundry technology + `Metallkunde` + `Werkstoffprüfung` = **7.5%**;
- geology / ore-deposit science / geography = **6.0%**;
- chemistry / mineralogy / petrography = **4.3%**.

These percentages are historical controls, not current class codes. Still, they demonstrate that materials/testing and mineral/geoscience literature were already large institutional shelf environments, rather than clusters created by the present research agenda.

This keeps an eventual early-materials-microscopy question plausible, but it does **not** make microscopy the current retrieval rule.

## 7. Access consequence

Printed Library blocks require a different onsite test from EBA/GFA:

- the Library is a reference library, but its own literature can also be made available through interlibrary loan for consultation in other libraries;
- user photography and scanning are allowed onsite;
- therefore `held in Library` does not automatically equal `residency necessary`.

Potential exceptions are blocks where:

- copy-specific annotations/provenance/material construction matter;
- grey literature is exceptionally rare and not practically available elsewhere;
- a large cross-shelf comparison is operationally much more efficient onsite;
- exact local serial completeness/gaps are themselves evidence.

# Wedding / Bessemer flame — from skilled visual judgement to spectroscopy, 1860–1871

Date: 2026-09-12

Status: MAJOR PRIMARY-SOURCE BRIDGE. This file connects the image-controlled 1860 Wedding Bessemer notebook to Hermann Wedding's own 1869 paper on the Bessemer spectrum, the 1869–70 Rowan/Sorby/Silliman discussion, and J. Spear Parker's 1871 Cyclops Works spectroscopy. It deliberately avoids a replacement narrative in which instruments simply displace skilled workers. The sources instead show overlapping regimes of naked-eye judgement, coloured-glass filtering, spectroscopic observation, chemical interpretation and production control.

## 2. Major correction: Hermann Wedding himself published on the Bessemer spectrum

The apparently separate later spectroscopy story is not separate from Wedding.

**Dr H. Wedding, `Das Spektrum der Bessemerflamme`, p.117.**

The article occupies pp.117–134 and was also issued as a separate offprint with one plate; antiquarian metadata identifies it explicitly as by **Hermann Wedding** in Berlin.

Primary bibliographic route:

## 4. Wedding's intervention was chemical/interpretive, not merely observational

The 1870 Austrian summary reconstructs the controversy Wedding entered.

Earlier interpretations associated the characteristic Bessemer spectrum primarily with carbon / carbon monoxide. Brunner and others challenged this and argued that manganese and iron were central. The summary says Wedding's paper addressed whether the spectroscope could be used as an indicator of progressive decarburisation and whether characteristic lines belonged to carbon or to other pig-iron constituents.

The 1871 AIME paper by Thomas M. Drown provides independent English-language control of Wedding's position. Drown writes that in Germany it had become widely accepted that manganese caused the characteristic dark-band groups, adding that **Wedding and von Lichtenfels had produced the manganese spectrum and found it closely corresponding to the Bessemer flame**.

Source:
- Thomas M. Drown, `The Attainment of Uniformity in Bessemer Steel`, AIME Troy meeting, Nov. 1871: https://www.smenet.org/DOCS/PUBLIC/AIME-Transactions_vol_01.pdf

This is important: Wedding is not merely citing other spectroscopy. He is treated internationally as an experimental contributor to the manganese-spectrum interpretation.

## 5. Silliman 1870 directly engages Wedding's observations and argument

Justus M. Silliman's 1870 paper, based on observations at John A. Griswold & Co.'s Bessemer Steel Works in Troy, repeatedly cites Wedding.

Primary source:
- J. M. Silliman, `On the Examination of the Bessemer Flame with Colored Glasses and with the Spectroscope`, *American Journal of Science*, 1870: https://ajsonline.org/article/62386-on-the-examination-of-the-bessemer-flame-with-colored-glasses-and-with-the-spectroscope/attachment/140770.pdf

Directly controlled points:

1. Silliman says von Lichtenfels found close coincidence between manganese and Bessemer spectra in blue/green regions; he adds that **Wedding stated he had repeatedly seen the violet manganese line**, which Silliman himself could not detect.

2. Silliman explicitly compares his solar-spectrum calibration against **the sun-spectrum Wedding published together with the Bessemer spectrum**, showing that Wedding's paper included an actual graphical/calibrational comparison object, not only verbal discussion.

3. Silliman devotes a substantial passage to **Wedding's attempt to explain the non-appearance/disappearance of manganese lines**. He reports Wedding's use of Brunner's chemical analyses: manganese falls through successive stages in the metal, manganese protoxide changes in the slag, manganese is present in smoke, and volatilisation must be considered.

4. Silliman then criticises/refines Wedding's explanation. This is evidence that Wedding's 1869 article had entered an international experimental debate quickly enough to become a target of direct replication/critique in Troy.

The chain is therefore already transnational by 1870:

**Berlin Wedding paper/plate → Troy works experiment → explicit calibration/critique.**

## 6. The observation problem is explicitly a control problem

Thomas Rowan's Glasgow paper, read 29 March 1869, states the operational issue with unusual clarity. He calls the converter flame the index of changes in the molten mass and says the most important point is catching the moment when decarburisation has reached the desired state. Over-blowing and under-blowing both damage steel quality.

Rowan's purpose is to make the critical `change` in the flame easier to determine through:

- spectroscopic experiments;
- coloured glasses;
- a purpose-designed observing instrument.

Primary text route:
- *Chemical News*, 1869, `On the Examination of the “Flame” of the Bessemer Converter`, Thomas Rowan: https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/d/d7/The_chemical_news._Volume_19%2C_January_-_June_1869._%28IA_s713id13683410%29.pdf
- Royal Philosophical Society of Glasgow index fixes reading date: 29 March 1869.

This supplies the operational grammar needed for comparison with Wedding 1860: the flame is simultaneously process phenomenon, visual sign and control signal.

## 7. Sorby in 1870: instrument value without simple replacement of skilled judgement

A particularly important letter appears in *Chemical News*, 18 Feb. 1870, by **H. C. Sorby, Broomfield, Sheffield**.

Sorby says in substance:

- an experienced person can determine when the blast should cease from the general colour of the flame;
- a direct-vision spectroscope can be of great value;
- he describes continuous spectrum → sodium → additional lines/dark bands → disappearance of dark lines as a possible guide;
- he judges the spectrum method useful especially for a comparatively inexperienced observer;
- he is **not persuaded that it necessarily gives better results than experience from the naked-eye colour of the flame**.

Same-page context:
- Thomas Rowan says a coloured-glass combination makes changes more easily determined and can be safely used by inexperienced observers.

Source:
- *Chemical News*, vol.21, 18 Feb. 1870, p.80: https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/f/fe/The_chemical_news._Volume_21%2C_January_-_June_1870._%28IA_s713id13683430%29.pdf

Analytical consequence:

> The historical transition is not `subjective eye → objective instrument`. Skilled naked-eye judgement remains an explicit benchmark against which optical aids are evaluated.

The key issue is redistribution of competence: who can make a reliable decision, through which carrier, with how much training.

## 8. Coloured glasses are a competence-transfer device, not merely an optical instrument

Silliman 1870 and Parker 1871 show coloured filters as a distinct epistemic technology between naked eye and full spectroscopy.

Silliman at Troy:

- used a blue + two light-yellow glass combination;
- prepared a plate of roughly one hundred numbered colours/tints to reproduce successive flame appearances;
- explicitly correlated visible process stages with filtered colours and spectroscopic observations.

Parker at Cyclops in 1871:

- used cobalt-blue + amber glass;
- says filtered-glass changes derive from the same phenomena visible to the naked eye;
- explicitly states this method **cannot be more accurate than the method already in general use**, because the experienced workman's eye detects the change with surprising precision;
- gives the filters a different advantage: an inexperienced observer can use them as a safer guide, and the filters protect the eye from the intense glare.

Primary source:
- J. Spear Parker, `On the Examination of the Bessemer Flame with Coloured Glasses, and with the Spectroscope`, *Chemical News* 23 (20 Jan. 1871), p.25: https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/1/11/The_chemical_news._Volume_23%2C_January_-_June_1871._%28IA_s713id13683450%29.pdf

This distinction is analytically powerful:

**coloured glass = reformat/standardise an already-skilled visual distinction**

not

**coloured glass = discover a wholly new process state.**

## 9. Parker at Cyclops: spectroscopy has a `different basis`

The same 1871 Parker paper explicitly distinguishes spectroscopic indications from filtered visual appearance.

Institutional setting is unusually important:

> Parker says he had been investigating the Bessemer flame **at the Cyclops Steel and Iron Works on behalf of Messrs Charles Cammell & Co.**, and published the results with the firm's permission.

He used a direct-vision spectroscope with five glass prisms and argues that, if spectroscopy is to be placed in the hands of workers unfamiliar with scientific instruments, direct vision is preferable because once focused it can be used rather like a telescope and needs less readjustment.

He correlates:

- reddish-orange flame ↔ continuous spectrum;
- crimson phase ↔ sodium line;
- light-yellow filtered flame ↔ characteristic Bessemer spectrum;
- end of decarburisation ↔ characteristic changes/disappearance in the spectrum and renewed crimson appearance.

The instrument is therefore explicitly being redesigned/selected for industrial usability by non-specialist operators.

This is stronger than generic `science applied to industry`. It is a material attempt to translate a laboratory optical apparatus into a production-control interface.

## 10. Parker's 1871 follow-up: filter colour itself becomes an object of analysis

In the second half of 1871 Parker published a follow-up:

**`On the Causes of the Phenomena Observed when the Bessemer Flame is Viewed through Coloured Glasses`**

and is identified explicitly as:

**`Chemist to the Cyclops Steel and Iron Works, Sheffield.`**

He analyses why different glass combinations change the visible flame colours, including the absorption characteristics of blue/orange glasses and their relation to the Bessemer spectrum.

Primary source:
- *Chemical News* vol.24, 1871: https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/9/9d/The_chemical_news._Volume_24%2C_July_-_December_1871._%28IA_s713id13683460%29.pdf

The observation carrier itself is now being calibrated:

**flame → coloured medium → perceived colour → spectrum → process interpretation.**

This is directly relevant to the project's broader interest in later controlled illumination, prepared surfaces and microphotography.

## 12. Stronger project transformation chain

The earlier project chain can now be made materially more specific:

**1860 works inspection + skilled flame/time judgement → portable notebook inscription → c.1868/69 Wedding spectral re-analysis → coloured-glass / spectroscope control debates → industrially adapted optical observation at Cyclops → later prepared-specimen / microscopic / photographic comparison systems.**

This is not a smooth march from eye to instrument. It is a history of **reformatting process signals and redistributing observational competence**.

A more exact formulation:

> The object of standardisation is first not the steel specimen itself but the **observable process signal**: flame colour, temporal stage, filtered colour, spectral line, disappearance event. Only later does comparison increasingly migrate into prepared solid objects and images.

This may provide a major bridge between Wedding's early works notebooks and the later metallographic carrier system.

## 13. Particularly important conceptual result: `inexperienced hands`

Across Rowan/Sorby/Parker, a recurring problem is not merely accuracy in the abstract. It is whether a judgement can be made by someone **without the tacit experience of the skilled Bessemer worker**.

This gives a concrete social/epistemic dimension to optical instrumentation:

**expert embodied judgement → optical mediation → wider reproducibility / delegated judgement.**

The instrument is therefore part of labour organisation and knowledge transfer, not only measurement science.

This should be compared later with:

- prepared metallographic specimens;
- standardized illumination;
- numbered collections;
- microphotographs;
- reference atlases;

all of which similarly reduce dependence on co-presence with a uniquely experienced observer.

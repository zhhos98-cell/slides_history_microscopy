## 1. Why this source matters

Barraclough explicitly identifies Gruner & Lan as a source for the situation in **Sheffield around 1859–1860**, essentially contemporaneous with Wedding's 25 July 1860 notebook sequence.

This is not merely a later statistical retrospective. Barraclough's appendix states that the information on Gruner & Lan pp. 788–789 was given on the authority of:

**`Mr. Vickers, of the house of Naylor, Vickers and Co.`**

and adds that the textual context suggests the figures may refer to **1859 or 1860**.

## 2. Sheffield output structure reported for c.1859–60

Barraclough, citing Gruner & Lan pp. 788–789, reports estimated Sheffield-area steel production of roughly **50,000–60,000 tons per annum** and gives the following product breakdown:

- puddled steel and common spring steel — **50%**;
- common cast steel, superior to the previous category — **15%**;
- cast steel for mill spindles, spades, shovels, etc. — **10%**;
- better-quality cast steel, welded for various tools and cutlery — **10%**;
- good-quality cast steel for the same broad applications — **10%**;
- first-quality cast steel for drills, engraving tools, etc. — **4%**;
- extra-quality cast steel for very superior tools — **1%**.

## 3. The source also describes concentration into large works

Barraclough says the same Gruner & Lan passage notes that the major half-dozen Sheffield works were each capable of producing up to approximately **5,000 tons of steel per annum**. Taken together, these large works already produced more steel than the whole Sheffield area had produced roughly twenty years earlier.

This is important for Wedding's notebook because `Cammells Walzwerke (Cyclops Works)` should be understood as entry into a very large integrated works environment, not a small specialist crucible shop.

## 4. Raw-material regime: Swedish/Russian versus common iron

Barraclough's supplementary appendix adds a useful provenance-sensitive summary of Gruner & Lan pp.788–789:

- Yorkshire was said to produce roughly **50,000–60,000 tons of steel annually**;
- more than half was made from imported **Swedish and Russian iron**, brought through Hull;
- the remainder was described as `common steel`, produced from English and colonial cast and wrought irons.

This gives a near-contemporary material-selection regime against which Wedding's works notes can later be compared.

## 5. Consequence for the 25 July 1860 Cyclops visit

The strongest analytical gain is that the 1860 Cyclops visit should not be read through the later image of Cammell as a mature Bessemer rail/armour producer.

Current chronology is:

**1859 — Bessemer process technically stabilised at Sheffield works**

**1860 — John Brown takes Bessemer licence**

**25 July 1860 — Wedding visits Bessemer, then Cammell/Cyclops**

**1861 — Cammell takes Bessemer licence and begins rail / railway-material production**

The Gruner–Lan/Barraclough evidence shows that the Sheffield steel economy around Wedding's visit was still profoundly **mixed-process**: puddled/common spring steel constituted a very large share alongside multiple grades of crucible/cast steel.

Therefore the safest present interpretation of Wedding's `Cammells Walzwerke` is:

> **a large rolling/steel works embedded in a heterogeneous Sheffield process regime immediately before Cammell's own Bessemer transition.**

Do not silently recast the July 1860 Cyclops visit as a visit to an established Bessemer rail works.

## 6. Why the Vickers provenance is especially valuable

The attribution to a Vickers insider changes the epistemic status of the statistics. They are not simply French observers' distant aggregate estimates. At least part of the Sheffield section appears to be based on information supplied by a major local steel manufacturer.

That makes Gruner & Lan useful as a comparator to Wedding's own portable notebook:

- Wedding: travelling German metallurgist, site-specific notebook inscriptions;
- Gruner & Lan: French engineering survey, incorporating local manufacturer testimony;
- Vickers: local producer acting as an information node.

This creates a broader question about **how industrial knowledge was made portable through controlled disclosure, interviews, factory visits, quantitative tables and sketches**.

It also fits the emerging Bessemer access argument: Sheffield industrial knowledge was neither simply public nor simply secret. Different controlled channels transferred different kinds of information.

## 7. Relation to Cammell and Brown

Barraclough separately notes that Brown and Cammell were not instantaneously transformed into single-process Bessemer firms. Both continued older iron/steel activities while entering bulk steelmaking.

This is analytically important but should be kept distinct from the Gruner & Lan attribution unless the French original explicitly names the firms in the relevant passage.

Current safe statement:

- Gruner & Lan/Barraclough controls the **regional mixed-process structure** around 1859–60;
- independent chronology controls Brown 1860 / Cammell 1861 Bessemer adoption;
- Wedding's manuscript controls the actual sequence `Bessemer → Cammells Walzwerke` in late July 1860.

The conjunction is analytically strong, but it is a synthesis across sources.

## 9. Working result

The present source-controlled model of Sheffield in July 1860 is now sharper:

**not `old Sheffield steel → Bessemer suddenly replaces it`, but a dense mixed-process landscape in which high-grade crucible traditions, puddled/common steel, large rolling works and the newly licensed Bessemer process coexist and are being recombined.**

Wedding's one-day sequence therefore becomes especially valuable because it moves directly through different nodes of that transition while converting them into adjacent notebook inscriptions.

# Fischer 1845–1846 — practical knowledge, secrecy and reciprocal exchange

Status: active evidence note, 2026-09-11.

Source basis: supplied machine-readable Johann Conrad Fischer 1845 and 1846 travel texts. These are OCR/research texts, not manually verified diplomatic transcriptions.

## Core finding

The Fischer corpus makes the circulation problem explicit. Technical travel is not simply the transfer of published information from Britain to the Continent. The texts distinguish among:

- what can be learned from writing;
- what requires `Autopsie` / following a process in a works;
- what requires prior practical knowledge in order for written information to be useful;
- what manufacturers deliberately keep secret;
- what is legally protected as priority / patentable invention;
- what is selectively disclosed inside trusted technical relationships;
- what becomes available through recommendation letters and privileged factory access;
- what is exchanged through reciprocal questioning, samples, drawings and demonstrations rather than prose alone.

This distinction should become a primary coding layer for Wedding Mss 23–25.

## 1. Fischer's quoted Swedenstierna passage: writing is not enough

In the 1846 Sheffield account Fischer pauses during discussion of cast-steel manufacture to quote the Swedish metallurgist Eric Thomas Swedenstierna on his 1802–1803 British travels.

The quotation says, in substance, that Swedenstierna had the opportunity to follow the cast-steel process fairly closely in two Sheffield factories but did not intend to give a detailed description. Two reasons are given:

1. what was already known in writing, when combined with a certain practical knowledge of ironworking, was enough to put a competent practitioner on the right path;
2. there was advantage in keeping the art to oneself, and the many failed German and French attempts showed that the art remained unknown there despite what learned and unlearned writers had published.

The important structure is therefore:

`written description + prior practical competence != complete transfer`

and, separately:

`direct access to process != obligation to publish process in full`.

This is an explicit historical statement about the limits of textual transmission and the strategic withholding of process knowledge.

## 2. Fischer immediately performs reciprocal information exchange

Directly after the Swedenstierna passage Fischer records that a Sheffield manufacturer asked about wages in Fischer's own works and other matters concerning steel production. Fischer says that he answered these questions **in detail in order to be entitled to ask counter-questions** (`um zu Gegenfragen berechtiget zu sein`).

This is exceptionally useful evidence because reciprocity is not inferred by us; Fischer states the exchange logic himself.

Working sequence:

`give technical/business information -> acquire entitlement to counter-question -> receive information`.

The traveller therefore does not merely extract information. He trades information.

## 3. Patent priority and secrecy are alternative boundary strategies, not a linear sequence

### 3.1 1845: Fischer uses the British patent system to secure priority

In London in July 1845 Fischer goes to the Register Office for patent applications and files two caveats in order to secure the priority of two inventions. He writes the requests himself; on the following day Poole formally enters them in the register after payment of the fee.

The two caveats concern:

- an alloy intended to be mixed/fused with pig iron, steels, bar/forged iron or scrap iron;
- a new way of making horseshoes and shoes for other cattle.

Thus Fischer is not merely observing the British patent system from outside. He actively uses formal registration as a mechanism for staking a technical claim.

### 3.2 1846: Fischer explicitly recommends secrecy over patenting in some cases

At Cammell in Sheffield in 1846 Fischer criticises a patent for converting iron turnings into cast steel. He predicts technical failure from the rusty turnings and remarks on the speed with which people in England take out patents. He notes the high cost and litigation risk and states that one would often do better simply to keep the matter secret.

The pair is analytically useful precisely because it is not a contradiction that must be resolved. Fischer treats legal priority and secrecy as **different strategic responses to different technical/economic situations**.

Working boundary alternatives:

`formal priority claim / caveat / patent`

versus

`strategic non-disclosure / secret process`.

## 4. Secret knowledge can be selectively disclosed inside trusted relationships

In the 1845 account Fischer returns to the Gott network near Leeds and recalls an earlier visit roughly eighteen years before. He specifically remembers that a method **still being kept secret** for making cloth waterproof with a solution of caoutchouc had nevertheless been communicated to him.

This is important because `secret` does not mean universally inaccessible.

The evidence supports a more precise model:

`publicly secret -> selectively disclosed to trusted insider/contact`.

That makes personal relation and reputation part of the knowledge boundary itself.

## 5. Recommendation letters are technical access instruments

Fischer's introductions do not merely provide social hospitality. They repeatedly alter what he can see.

### 5.1 London -> Low Moor

In 1845 a London industrial contact promises Fischer a letter to Low Moor, explicitly directing him to the works where he can obtain the best and most certain information about coke/cold-blast practice. Fischer later arrives with a strong recommendation; the letter is read by a proprietor/manager and he is taken through the works for roughly two hours.

The recommendation therefore converts an information request into physical and conversational access.

### 5.2 Technical credibility is itself part of the credential

The same London contact tells Fischer he expects Fischer to be well received because Fischer is sufficiently capable of expressing himself on the technical subjects in question.

Access is therefore conditioned by more than possession of a letter. The visitor's perceived competence matters.

### 5.3 1846: recommendation letters are copied and carried as a documentary access package

Before leaving London for the northern works Fischer records copying the recommendation letters supplied by his contact, together with the covering letter containing additional indications.

This matters methodologically: the recommendation system leaves a documentary trail that Fischer deliberately preserves in copy before deploying the originals.

### 5.4 Privileged factory visibility is explicitly unequal

A Sheffield contact tells Fischer that, in an offer **not made to everyone in England**, they will show him whatever he wishes to see in their cementation, cast-steel, file and locomotive-spring works.

This is unusually explicit evidence that factory visibility is tiered rather than simply open/closed.

Working sequence:

`technical reputation + recommendation -> privileged admission -> selectable visibility inside works`.

## 6. Knowledge access is layered across carriers and situations

The 1845–1846 corpus now shows several distinct access mechanisms:

### Published / printed knowledge

Fischer repeatedly knows descriptions before arriving at a site. At the needle works he explicitly notes that the process is already described in technological literature, while still assigning value to seeing the operations directly.

### Drawings

Furnace and works drawings carry spatial/process information away from sites. Fischer is shown an Alfredton furnace drawing before visiting northern works; he carries Beck's drawings of his own Schaffhausen works into British conversations.

### `Autopsie`

Fischer's explicit term for the necessity of direct seeing at industrial scale. But direct sight is supplemented by questions, measurement and material samples.

### Shop-floor demonstration

An unnamed Sheffield melter takes Fischer into the crucible workshop and makes a crucible in front of him. This is procedural knowledge embodied in an operation, not merely verbally described.

### Material samples

Crucible fragments, clay, ores, coke, iron stages and Fischer's own steel/cast-wrought-iron pieces allow comparison to continue outside the original site.

### Reciprocal questioning

Information about Fischer's own works, labour and process can be exchanged for permission to ask questions about British practice.

### Formal priority / patent mechanism

Caveats establish a legally legible claim to priority without being equivalent to full publication of technical practice.

### Deliberate secrecy

Both the quoted Swedenstierna passage and Fischer's own 1846 patent critique give explicit rationales for keeping useful process knowledge out of full circulation.

### Selective disclosure

The remembered caoutchouc method shows that a secret can nevertheless move through trusted person-to-person channels.

### Recommendation-mediated visibility

Letters and named contacts alter whether a visitor can enter, who guides them and how much they are allowed to inspect.

## 7. Drawings and documents are also exchange objects

Fischer deliberately brings Beck's three coloured drawings of his works to Sheffield. The Huntsman family studies them at length, especially the interior of the steel/cast-wrought-iron foundry during casting. This viewing is followed by further access to Huntsman's hammer works.

The text does not prove that the drawings were a formal quid pro quo for access. But they clearly function inside the technical-social encounter as representations of Fischer's own practice rather than as private illustrations.

Brande's Royal Mint letter is another carrier: Fischer later shows it at Cammell while arguing that British cast-steel arrangements could be improved. An institutional judgement produced in London therefore re-enters the Sheffield exchange circuit as documentary authority.

Later photographic reproduction adds another circulation layer:

`works -> drawing -> photographic negative -> multiple positive copies`.

## 8. The knowledge problem is not identical with secrecy or tacitness

The Fischer evidence distinguishes multiple reasons why knowledge may fail to travel or become actionable:

1. **secrecy / strategic withholding** — process deliberately not fully described;
2. **selective disclosure** — information moves, but only inside trusted relations;
3. **legal boundary / priority claim** — invention is entered into a patent/caveat regime;
4. **insufficient practical competence** — a written description is not actionable without prior craft/metallurgical knowledge;
5. **inadequate access or mediator** — e.g. Butterley, where the technically weak guide makes Fischer distrust the information;
6. **unequal factory visibility** — privileged visitors may be shown what ordinary visitors are not;
7. **lack of reciprocity** — technical questioning may require giving information in return;
8. **transport/observation limits** — rapid railway travel can prevent adequate inspection and note-taking.

These mechanisms should not be collapsed into the general claim that industrial knowledge is simply `tacit`.

## 9. Knowledge-boundary coding for Wedding

Wedding's 1860–62 notebooks should be read for an **information economy**, not only an itinerary.

For every major works/person episode, ask:

- What did Wedding already know before arrival?
- Was the prior source a book, drawing, letter, lecture, introduction or previous journey?
- Was the information public, patent-protected, secret or selectively disclosed?
- What recommendation/credential produced access?
- Was the access ordinary or explicitly privileged?
- What was he permitted to see?
- Who explained it, and with what authority?
- What did he measure or copy himself?
- What samples/drawings did he receive?
- What did he give or disclose in return?
- Is there evidence of refusal, secrecy, omission or deliberate non-description?
- Does he distinguish what can be written from what has to be seen/demonstrated?
- Does later Berlin work contain knowledge that first appears only as a practical observation in Britain?

Add the following coding layer to the existing operation schema:

`PUBLICATION | PRACTICAL_COMPETENCE | PATENT_PRIORITY | SECRET | SELECTIVE_DISCLOSURE | RECOMMENDATION | PRIVILEGED_ACCESS | RECIPROCAL_EXCHANGE | DEMONSTRATION | DOCUMENT_COPY | WITHHOLDING`

## 10. Relevance to the later microscopy/material-testing problem

This creates a stronger route into the later Berlin story. The question is not merely whether microscopy reveals something smaller. It is whether the laboratory reorganises the **conditions under which knowledge becomes inspectable, recordable, comparable and transmissible**.

Potential contrast to test, not assume:

**works travel**

`personal recommendation -> privileged access -> skilled guide/worker -> observation/question/sample -> selective notebook record`

versus later **testing laboratory / metallographic collection**

`prepared specimen -> controlled apparatus -> labelled/ordered result -> photograph/plate -> repeatable reference object`.

The second regime may reduce some dependence on travel and personal access while creating new dependencies on preparation, apparatus, photographic technique and collection order. This remains a hypothesis for the Wedding/Martens evidence.

## Claim ceiling

The current Fischer texts support explicit claims about:

- practical knowledge as a condition for making written descriptions actionable;
- reciprocal technical questioning;
- strategic non-disclosure;
- legal priority claims through caveats;
- selective disclosure of still-secret techniques inside trusted relations;
- recommendation-mediated and explicitly unequal factory access.

They do not by themselves establish:

- that all Sheffield steel knowledge was secret;
- that written knowledge was ineffective in general;
- that patents and secrecy were mutually exclusive or chronologically successive regimes;
- that Wedding encountered the same exchange rules;
- that later metallographic institutions were designed to solve this circulation problem.

Those points require separate evidence.

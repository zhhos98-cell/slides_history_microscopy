# Fischer 1845–1846 — practical knowledge, secrecy and reciprocal exchange

Status: active evidence note, 2026-09-11.

Source basis: supplied machine-readable Johann Conrad Fischer 1845 and 1846 travel texts. These are OCR/research texts, not manually verified diplomatic transcriptions.

## Core finding

The Fischer corpus makes the circulation problem explicit. Technical travel is not simply the transfer of published information from Britain to the Continent. The texts distinguish among:

- what can be learned from writing;
- what requires `Autopsie` / following a process in a works;
- what requires prior practical knowledge in order for written information to be useful;
- what manufacturers deliberately keep secret;
- what becomes available through personal access and reciprocal questioning;
- what is exchanged through samples, drawings and demonstrations rather than prose alone.

This distinction should become a primary coding layer for Wedding Mss 23–25.

## 1. Fischer's quoted Swedenstierna passage: writing is not enough

In the 1846 Sheffield account Fischer pauses during discussion of cast-steel manufacture to quote the Swedish metallurgist Eric Thomas Swedenstierna on his 1802–1803 British travels.

The quotation says, in substance, that Swedenstierna had the opportunity to follow the cast-steel process fairly closely in two Sheffield factories but did not intend to give a detailed description. Two reasons are given:

1. what was already known in writing, when combined with a certain practical knowledge of ironworking, was enough to put a competent practitioner on the right path;
2. there was advantage in keeping the art to oneself, and the many failed German and French attempts showed that the art remained unknown there despite what learned and unlearned writers had published.

The important structure is therefore:

`written description + prior practical competence != complete transfer`

and, separately:

`direct access to process != obligation to publish process in full`.

This is an explicit historical statement about the limits of textual transmission and the strategic withholding of process knowledge.

## 2. Fischer immediately performs reciprocal information exchange

Directly after the Swedenstierna passage Fischer records that a Sheffield manufacturer asked about wages in Fischer's own works and other matters concerning steel production. Fischer says that he answered these questions **in detail in order to be entitled to ask counter-questions** (`um zu Gegenfragen berechtiget zu sein`).

This is exceptionally useful evidence because reciprocity is not inferred by us; Fischer states the exchange logic himself.

Working sequence:

`give technical/business information -> acquire entitlement to counter-question -> receive information`.

The traveller therefore does not merely extract information. He trades information.

## 3. Knowledge access is layered

The 1845–1846 corpus now shows several different access mechanisms:

### Published / printed knowledge

Fischer repeatedly knows descriptions before arriving at a site. At the needle works he explicitly notes that the process is already described in technological literature, while still assigning value to seeing the operations directly.

### Drawings

Furnace and works drawings carry spatial/process information away from sites. Fischer is shown an Alfredton furnace drawing before visiting northern works; he carries Beck's drawings of his own Schaffhausen works into British conversations.

### `Autopsie`

Fischer's explicit term for the necessity of direct seeing at industrial scale. But direct sight is supplemented by questions, measurement and material samples.

### Shop-floor demonstration

An unnamed Sheffield melter takes Fischer into the crucible workshop and makes a crucible in front of him. This is procedural knowledge embodied in an operation, not merely verbally described.

### Material samples

Crucible fragments, clay, ores, coke, iron stages and Fischer's own steel/cast-wrought-iron pieces allow comparison to continue outside the original site.

### Reciprocal questioning

Information about Fischer's own works, labour and process can be exchanged for permission to ask questions about British practice.

### Deliberate secrecy

The Swedenstierna quotation provides an explicit historical rationale for withholding detailed descriptions even after close observation.

## 4. Drawings are also exchange objects

Fischer deliberately brings Beck's three coloured drawings of his works to Sheffield. The Huntsman family studies them at length, especially the interior of the steel/cast-wrought-iron foundry during casting. This viewing is followed by further access to Huntsman's hammer works.

The text does not prove that the drawings were a formal quid pro quo for access. But they clearly function inside the technical-social encounter as representations of Fischer's own practice rather than as private illustrations.

Later photographic reproduction adds another potential circulation layer:

`works -> drawing -> photographic negative -> multiple positive copies`.

## 5. The knowledge problem is not identical with secrecy

The Fischer evidence distinguishes at least three reasons why knowledge may fail to travel:

1. **secrecy / strategic withholding** — process deliberately not fully described;
2. **insufficient practical competence** — a written description is not actionable without prior craft/metallurgical knowledge;
3. **inadequate access or mediator** — e.g. Butterley, where the technically weak guide makes Fischer distrust the information.

A fourth problem appears in rapid railway travel: physical movement can become so fast that the traveller cannot inspect or record passing objects adequately.

These mechanisms should not be collapsed into the general claim that industrial knowledge is 'tacit'.

## 6. Implication for Wedding

Wedding's 1860–62 notebooks should be read for an **information economy**, not only an itinerary.

For every major works/person episode, ask:

- What did Wedding already know before arrival?
- Was the prior source a book, drawing, letter, lecture, introduction or previous journey?
- What was he permitted to see?
- Who explained it, and with what authority?
- What did he measure or copy himself?
- What samples/drawings did he receive?
- What did he give or disclose in return?
- Is there evidence of refusal, secrecy, omission or deliberate non-description?
- Does he distinguish what can be written from what has to be seen/demonstrated?
- Does later Berlin work contain knowledge that first appears only as a practical observation in Britain?

## 7. Relevance to the later microscopy/material-testing problem

This creates a stronger route into the later Berlin story. The question is not merely whether microscopy reveals something smaller. It is whether the laboratory reorganises the **conditions under which knowledge becomes inspectable, recordable, comparable and transmissible**.

Potential contrast to test, not assume:

**works travel**

`personal recommendation -> privileged access -> skilled guide/worker -> observation/question/sample -> selective notebook record`

versus later **testing laboratory / metallographic collection**

`prepared specimen -> controlled apparatus -> labelled/ordered result -> photograph/plate -> repeatable reference object`.

The second regime may reduce some dependence on travel and personal access while creating new dependencies on preparation, apparatus, photographic technique and collection order. This remains a hypothesis for the Wedding/Martens evidence.

## Claim ceiling

The current Fischer texts support explicit claims about practical knowledge, reciprocal questioning and quoted arguments for strategic non-disclosure. They do not by themselves establish:

- that all Sheffield steel knowledge was secret;
- that written knowledge was ineffective in general;
- that Wedding encountered the same exchange rules;
- that later metallographic institutions were designed to solve this circulation problem.

Those points require separate evidence.

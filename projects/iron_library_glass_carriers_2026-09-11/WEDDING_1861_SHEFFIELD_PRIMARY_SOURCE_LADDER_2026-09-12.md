## 1. Meeting chronology: 31 July–1 August 1861

Henry Bessemer's autobiography explicitly recalls Sir William Armstrong's visit to Sheffield as President of the Institution of Mechanical Engineers and gives **31 July 1861** as the date of the summer meeting.

A directly controlled *Scientific American* report, 7 September 1861, states more fully that the Society of Mechanical Engineers met at Sheffield on **July 31 and August 1**, when Bessemer read his paper.

Sources:
- Henry Bessemer, *An Autobiography*, chapter 15: https://www.bibliomania.com/2/9/71/118/21374/2.html
- *Scientific American*, new series, vol. V, no.10, 7 Sept. 1861, printed p.148: https://www.survivorlibrary.com/library/scientific-american-1861-09-07-v05-n10.pdf

Chronological consequence:

- use **31 July 1861** as opening-day anchor;
- the Sheffield meeting extended into **1 August**;
- Bishop's broad phrase `In August 1861` should not replace this exact two-day control.

## 4. Scientific American p.148: contemporaneous re-publication of Bessemer's proof package

The *Scientific American* article is headed **`The Bessemer Process of Making Steel`**. It says Bessemer gave a full account of the process, steel quality/properties and uses. The American editors first compress the process principle into:

- molten cast iron in a vessel;
- air forced through the bottom;
- oxygen removes carbon;
- carbon is then restored to the desired level by addition of pig iron of known carbon content.

The article then prints substantial extracts from Bessemer's paper. The evidential architecture is especially important.

### Existing-use proof: Platt Brothers boilers

Bessemer points to six steel boilers already in daily use at Platt Brothers, Oldham, with dimensions and working pressure. The example functions as an operational user-case rather than a laboratory promise.

### Material proof through physical objects

For ordnance, Bessemer says test bars cut from recently forged guns were **on the table** before the meeting. A gun-muzzle specimen crushed cold under a steam hammer was also physically presented. The paper further invokes Belgian government firing tests.

Thus the formal paper's argument is materially staged through **specimens + test history + quantified comparison**.

### Timed production proof

For an 18-pounder gun, Bessemer says he personally noted the manufacturing time:

- molten cast iron tapped from the reverberatory furnace at **11:20 a.m.**;
- converted to cast steel in **30 minutes**;
- ingot cast 16 inches square by 4 feet long;
- forging completed by **7 p.m.**, ready for the boring mill.

The article also reports Bessemer's estimate that apparatus capable of producing steel for forty such gun blocks per day would cost about £5,000.

Source:
- *Scientific American*, 7 Sept. 1861, printed p.148, especially the article beginning `The Bessemer Process of Making Steel`.

Claim ceiling: this 30-minute value is tied to the 1861 ordnance-production example and must not be collapsed into Wedding's 1860 notebook figure `Der ganze Prozeß dauert cca 19–24 Minuten.` The intervals/products may differ.

## 5. Scientific American p.153: editorial conversion into industrial-success / patent judgment

On printed p.153 the editors return to the Bessemer extracts in an editorial note headed **`Bessemer's Process of Making Steel`**.

They frame the six-year history as movement from an initially doubtful innovation to evidence of **complete industrial success** and superior iron/steel qualities. They single out:

- the six Platt Brothers boilers;
- suitability for ordnance;
- Bessemer's claim about inexpensive plant capable of forty 18-pounder gun blocks per day.

The editorial then immediately shifts to the American patent situation: Bessemer's US patent had been displaced by William Kelly's priority, and the editors ask whether an American iron manufacturer should obtain the Kelly patent and start steel manufacture in the United States.

Source:
- *Scientific American*, 7 Sept. 1861, printed p.153.

Analytical consequence:

**Sheffield technical paper / specimens / quantified demonstrations → transatlantic editorial condensation → industrial-success judgment → patent/investment opportunity.**

This is a concrete example of technical evidence changing carrier and decision-function as it travels.

## 6. Comparison with Wedding's 1860 notebook: formal demonstration grammar, not direct copying

The year-before/year-after comparison is now unusually sharp.

### Wedding, 25 July 1860

Portable notebook inscription at/around Bessemer's works:

- sectional/tuyere-type sketch;

- total-process timing `cca 19–24 Minuten`;

### Bessemer / Sheffield meeting, 31 July–1 August 1861

Formal expert proof package:

- verbal process principle;
- existing user installation (Platt boilers);
- physical test bars and crushed gun specimen;
- government test history;
- exact production timing for an ordnance example;
- plant cost / throughput claim;
- broad application programme.

### Scientific American, 7 September 1861

Editorial reformatting:

- `industrial success`;
- selected performance exemplars;
- capital/patent opportunity in the United States.

The useful comparison is therefore not `Wedding copied Bessemer`. It is:

> **Which variables count as portable proof when a new process moves between works observation, expert notebook, institutional paper, physical demonstration and transatlantic technical journalism?**

Both Wedding and the formal 1861 Bessemer package privilege **time, material, process geometry/performance and situated operational evidence**, but they do so for different audiences and carriers.

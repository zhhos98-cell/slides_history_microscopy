# Wedding 1885–1891 — metallographic method / carrier chain

Date: 2026-09-12

Status: METHOD-HISTORY CONTROL. This file isolates the transition from Wedding's 1885 microscopic-structure paper to later explicit work on section preparation, image projection, illumination and microphotographic reproduction. It is designed to test whether the 1885 Sorby–Wedding disagreement was partly a dispute over how the metallographic object itself was made visible and transferable.

## 1. 1885 starting point

Wedding's paper:

**H. Wedding, `The Properties of Malleable Iron, deduced from its Microscopic Structure`, Journal of the Iron and Steel Institute, 1885, no.1, pp.187–204.**

Contemporary reports show that the paper described microscopic appearances of many iron specimens and attempted to infer metallurgical/mechanical properties from internal structure. The 22 May 1885 report criticised the presentation because no illustrations were shown, making the visual argument difficult to transfer to the audience.

A later review of Sorby's metallurgical work identifies an important material base: Wedding invited members of the Iron and Steel Institute to Berlin to inspect **A. Martens' collection of 120 metallographic sections** at the Royal Prussian Mechanical Testing Institute; the collection had provided the basis of Wedding's talk.

Source routing:
- Edyvean & Hammond, `The metallurgical work of Henry Clifton Sorby...`, Historical Metallurgy 31/2, pp.54–85.

This means Wedding's 1885 paper should be treated not as free-standing verbal description but as an argument built on a pre-existing prepared-specimen collection.

## 2. Martens' 120 Schliffe already constitute a reference collection

The collection is independently bibliographically controlled through:

**A. Martens, `Erläuterungen einer in der kön. Bergakademie zu Berlin befindlichen Sammlung von 120 Schliffen zur Darstellung des mikroskopischen Gefüges verschiedener Eisen- und Stahlsorten`, Berlin, 1884.**

A near-contemporary technical bibliography independently lists this object as the 1884 Martens collection. Later institutional histories state that the 120-section collection was created for **comparison and teaching** and later became materially important to Emil Heyn's extension of Martens' microscopic methods and the Martens–Heyn apparatus tradition.

Source controls/routing:
- Francis Scott Rice, `Micro-Structural Characteristics of Steel`, bibliography item 10;
- later accounts of Emil Heyn and the Royal Mechanical-Technical Testing Institute / Materialprüfungsamt.

Analytical consequence:

> Wedding's 1885 microscopy did not rest on isolated specimens alone; it was embedded in an emerging **standard reference collection of prepared sections**.

The collection is therefore an early institutional carrier:

**material object → prepared polished section → collection/reference identity → comparative visual judgement → teaching/reuse.**

The exact numbering architecture, preparation protocol and specimen provenance still require Martens' 1884 catalogue itself. Do not yet assume that `120` implies a formally numbered 1–120 reference series unless the catalogue proves it.

## 3. Sorby's technical criticism: preparation and illumination

The 1886 Journal of the Royal Microscopical Society reproduces Sorby's criticism of Wedding's 1885 work.

Sorby attributes the difference between Wedding's conclusions and his own partly to specimen preparation. He says Wedding's sections had **not been ground down with soft stone before final polishing**, and that Sorby himself only saw what he called the ultimate structure properly after adopting this step.

A later detailed historical reconstruction adds a second methodological criticism from Sorby: **direct/vertical illumination was essential**, and Sorby was convinced Wedding had not used it.

Source controls:
- Journal of the Royal Microscopical Society (1886), section `Microscopical Structure of Iron and Steel`;
- Edyvean & Hammond, Historical Metallurgy 31/2.

This gives a two-variable methodological divergence:

**surface preparation + illumination geometry.**

The historiographical consequence is strong:

> Different metallographic preparation/illumination protocols can create different visible structures and therefore different theoretical conclusions.

This is not merely disagreement over interpretation of the same image.

## 4. Sorby's own method as comparison baseline

The 1886 JRMS summary describes Sorby's optical practice:

- oblique side illumination with a parabolic reflector;
- direct illumination using a small silver reflector that sends light down through part of the objective and receives reflected light through the other part;
- polished surfaces appear black under oblique light but bright/metallic under direct illumination;
- about 60x linear magnification is generally suitable, though higher powers can be used.

The preparation/illumination problem is therefore structurally analogous to the earlier Bessemer-flame problem:

**signal exists → instrument/preparation selects/reformats it → observer interprets a transformed field.**

## 5. 1886–1888 Wedding continues the microscopic programme

The bibliographic sequence immediately after the 1885 controversy is dense:

- **1886** — H. Wedding, `Die Mikrostructur des gebrannten Eisens`, Stahl und Eisen 6, p.633;
- **1887** — H. Wedding, `Die Mikrostructur des Eisen`, Stahl und Eisen 7, p.82;
- **1888** — H. Wedding, work on the relation between chemical composition, microstructure and conductivity of telegraph wire in the Royal Technical Testing Stations publications.

A contemporary Dingler summary of Wedding's burned-iron work explicitly notes that he presented the results with excellent illustrations and drew conclusions about the disappearance of the bright network structure as burning/oxygen content and grain coarseness increased.

Routing source:
- Dingler Polytechnisches Journal, `Kleinere Mittheilungen`, discussing Wedding's 1885/1886 *Stahl und Eisen* plates.

This suggests rapid movement from the 1885 communication failure toward more image-supported presentation.

## 6. 1888 — projected micro-images solve the collective-viewing problem

A contemporary Austrian mining/metallurgical meeting report records Wedding giving the paper:

**`Beurtheilung des Eisens aus seinem Kleingefüge`.**

Crucially, it states that Wedding gave the lecture **with images shown in a darkened hall using magnesium light**.

Source:
- Austrian mining/metallurgical meeting report, 1888, p.493 context.

This is a major carrier control because it directly answers the social problem exposed in 1885.

1885:

**microscopic structure described verbally without displayed illustrations → audience finds argument difficult to follow.**

1888:

**microstructural images → optical projection → darkened collective viewing environment.**

The object is no longer confined to one observer at a microscope or to a verbal description of what was seen. It becomes a **publicly projected image** that can be inspected simultaneously by an audience.

Claim ceiling: the meeting report controls display practice, not the exact original medium of the projected images. Until the full lecture/publication is recovered, do not assume whether the projected source was photograph, drawing, transparency, or another reproduction process.

## 7. 1889 — method becomes the explicit subject

Wedding's 1889 paper is bibliographically controlled as:

**H. Wedding, `Ueber Fortschritte in der Lichtabbildung des Kleingefüges von Eisen und die Herstellung von Schliffen`, Stahl und Eisen 9 (April 1889).**

The original 1889 volume is securely located in full-view digital form through Google Books/Google Play and HathiTrust (`Stahl und Eisen`, vol.9, 1889; part 1 / nos.1–6 contains April). Francis Scott Rice's near-contemporary bibliography independently fixes the item to **vol. IX, April 1889**. The article text/page start still needs direct extraction.

The title itself is methodologically decisive. It places two issues side by side:

- **Lichtabbildung** — optical/image reproduction of iron microstructure;
- **Herstellung von Schliffen** — production of polished sections.

This is exactly the pair implicated in Sorby's earlier criticism of Wedding: **illumination/image formation + specimen preparation**.

Claim ceiling: until the 1889 full text is controlled, do not assert that Wedding explicitly cites Sorby's criticism or adopts Sorby's soft-stone/vertical-light protocol. The chronological/methodological alignment is secure; direct response remains open.

## 8. Wedding's metallography is explicitly textbooked by 1893

A contemporary review of the **second edition, first volume, second Lieferung** of Wedding's *Ausführliches Handbuch der Eisenhüttenkunde* says that the volume's final section treats:

- **investigation of microstructure by microscope**;
- **production of polished sections (`Herstellung der Schliffe`)**;
- **judgement of iron from microstructure**.

Source:
- Dingler's Polytechnisches Journal, 1893 book notice for `Ausführliches Handbuch der Eisenhüttenkunde`, 2nd ed., vol.1, Lieferung 2.

The full first volume is independently exposed as a free digital book through Google Play/Google Books, 1034 pages, with the second edition issued in Lieferungen from 1891 onward.

This is an institutional change in genre:

**research paper / controversy → handbook chapter / instructional method.**

By this point microstructural investigation is no longer represented only as an experimental specialty. Wedding places microscopy, specimen preparation and interpretive judgement inside a general iron-metallurgy handbook intended for students and practitioners.

## 9. A mature Wedding preparation protocol is demonstrably reproducible by 1899

A 15 Nov. 1899 *Stahl und Eisen* article, `Zur Beurtheilung des Roheisens nach dem Kleingefüge`, states explicitly that its **Schliffflächen were prepared according to Wedding's instructions in his `Handbuch der Eisenhüttenkunde`, I.2**.

The authors report:

- thin plates approximately **4 mm thick**;
- about **1 cm² surface area**;
- cut from pig iron using a **cold saw under cooling**;
- one surface preserving the original fracture normal to the longitudinal axis of the pig-iron bar;
- ordinary etching tests were tried but then **etching was omitted** in this particular comparison because they did not materially improve the microscopic relief.

This is unusually valuable because it proves that Wedding's handbook instructions had become sufficiently explicit to be cited as a reproducible preparation recipe by a third party.

### Critical evidence ceiling: preparation is Wedding-derived; illumination is not

The same 1899 paper says that its microscopy and photography were performed with **diffuse light**, because the apparatus did not permit rays to be directed parallel to the microscope axis by special devices.

Do **not** attribute this diffuse-light arrangement to Wedding's handbook. The paper separates:

- specimen preparation — explicitly `nach der von Wedding ... gegebenen Anleitung`;
- microscopy/photography illumination — the authors' own apparatus-limited choice.

This separation is methodologically important because it demonstrates that **a specimen prepared by a standard recipe can still yield a differently constituted visual field under a different illumination geometry**.

That is exactly the variable Sorby's earlier criticism had made epistemically consequential.

Source:
- *Stahl und Eisen*, 15 Nov. 1899, p.1062 and surrounding article text.

Working sequence:

**individual/collection-based observation → method publication → handbook codification → third-party reproducible preparation protocol → locally variable illumination/imaging.**

## 10. 1890/91 — microphotographic carrier; date discrepancy remains open

The major bibliographic object is:

**H. Wedding, `Das Kleingefüge des Eisens. Mikroskopische Originalphotographien mit Erläuterungen`.**

Two respectable bibliographic controls disagree by one year:

- Francis Scott Rice's technical bibliography gives **Berlin 1891**;
- Naturhistorisches Museum Wien's catalogue gives **Berlin 1890, 19 Tafeln**.

Until the title page is directly controlled, use:

**`PUBLICATION_DATE = 1890/1891 (open bibliographic discrepancy)`**

and secure form claim:

**microscopic original photographs + explanatory text; 19 plates according to the Vienna catalogue.**

This is a major carrier shift:

**described/projected microstructure → prepared section → original microphotograph + explanatory text → durable distributable visual object.**

A contemporaneous methodological environment is also visible in Royal Microscopical Society reporting: by 1889 apparatus was being designed specifically for photographing iron microstructures, including oblique-stage arrangements based on Wedding's observations that details could be brought out more strongly when the iron surface was inclined relative to the microscope axis.

Source routing:
- Journal of the Royal Microscopical Society, 1889, reporting Schmidt & Haensch apparatus and explicitly attributing the oblique-surface observation to Wedding.

## 11. Revised relation to Sorby

The direct relation is now stronger than `parallel traditions` but still not a personal-contact claim.

Secure public methodological relation:

**Wedding 1885 paper → Sorby technical criticism of Wedding's preparation/illumination → Wedding continues microscopic work → Wedding 1888 projects microstructural images publicly → Wedding 1889 explicitly treats image formation and section preparation → Wedding's method is textbooked in the 1890s → third parties explicitly prepare sections according to Wedding → Wedding 1890/91 publishes microscopic original photographs.**

Open:

- whether Wedding read/answered Sorby's criticism explicitly;
- whether he changed his grinding/polishing method because of Sorby;
- whether he adopted direct vertical illumination specifically from Sorby;
- whether the 1889 paper cites Sorby by name;
- private correspondence or co-presence between Wedding and Sorby.

Therefore use:

`PUBLIC_METHOD_RELATION = SECURE`

not

`DIRECT_PERSONAL_TRANSMISSION = SECURE`.

## 12. Strong analytical implication

The metallographic object is not naturally given. It is produced through a chain:

**sample selection → sectioning/orientation → grinding → final polishing → etching/tarnishing → illumination geometry → magnification → projection/photography → publication/reference.**

A change at any stage can alter the visible structure or its social availability.

This makes the 1885 Sorby–Wedding disagreement especially valuable for the Iron Library project: it turns `sample preparation` from a technical footnote into a historical variable governing what counts as evidence.

The 1888 projection adds another variable: **even after an image exists, it must be scaled and displayed for collective witnessing**.

The 1899 third-party use makes the point sharper still: **standardized preparation does not by itself standardize the image**, because illumination can vary independently.

The longer same-person sequence can now be reformulated:

**1860 — live-process flame/time signal**
→ **1869 — spectrum as decomposed optical process signal**
→ **1884/85 — prepared section + Berlin reference collection of 120 Schliffe**
→ **1885 — microscopic internal material field but failed collective transfer without illustrations**
→ **1888 — magnesium-light projection for collective viewing**
→ **1889 — explicit problem of section production + optical image formation**
→ **1890/91 — original microphotographic plate atlas**
→ **1893 — handbook codification**
→ **1899 — third-party reproducible Wedding preparation method under independently variable illumination.**

This adds three institutional middle terms:

**prepared reference collection + collective display + teachable/repeatable preparation method.**

## 14. Claim ceiling

Secure:

- Sorby publicly attributes Wedding/Sorby differences to preparation, especially absence of soft-stone grinding before final polishing;
- Sorby also treats direct/vertical illumination as essential and a later historical reconstruction says he believed Wedding had not used it;
- Wedding's 1885 argument was based on Martens' large Berlin collection of prepared sections;
- Martens' 120-section collection is explicitly documented in an 1884 catalogue and later functioned as a comparison/teaching collection;
- Wedding continued publishing microscopic work in 1886–88;
- an 1888 meeting report explicitly records Wedding showing images in a darkened hall by magnesium light while lecturing on judgement of iron by microstructure;
- Wedding's 1889 paper explicitly joins optical image formation and section production in its title and survives in full-view volume 9 of *Stahl und Eisen*;
- a 1893 contemporary handbook review says Wedding's vol.1 Lieferung 2 closes with microscope examination, preparation of polished sections and judgement of iron by microstructure;
- by 1899 another investigator explicitly followed Wedding's handbook instructions for specimen preparation, including controlled thickness, area, cold-saw cutting/cooling and orientation;
- the 1899 paper's diffuse illumination was its authors' apparatus-limited imaging choice and must not be attributed to Wedding;
- `Das Kleingefüge des Eisens` is an original-microphotograph work with explanatory text; Vienna catalogue gives 19 plates and 1890, while Rice gives 1891.

Strong inference:

- the 1885 communication failure forms a plausible immediate background to Wedding's move toward image-supported/projected presentations;
- the methodological disagreement exposed specimen preparation and illumination as independently consequential variables;
- Wedding's later work increasingly treats reproducible preparation, collective display and image production as part of metallurgical evidence-making;
- the transition from Martens' 120 prepared reference sections to Wedding's projected images, handbook method and microphotographic plates is a plausible institutional path toward standardized metallographic reference systems;
- standardizing specimen, image, and display are historically distinct operations.

- exact handbook grinding/polishing sequence in Wedding's own wording;
- exact preparation/reference architecture in Martens' 1884 collection;

- continuity between Martens' 120-section collection and Wedding's atlas plates;
- whether numbering/collection/image architecture becomes formally standardized across these objects.

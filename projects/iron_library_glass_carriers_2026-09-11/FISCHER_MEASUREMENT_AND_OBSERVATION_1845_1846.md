# Fischer 1845–1846 — measurement, observation and reliability

Status: active evidence note, 2026-09-11.

Source basis: supplied machine-readable Johann Conrad Fischer 1845 and 1846 travel texts. These are OCR/research texts, not manually verified diplomatic transcriptions.

## Working proposition

Fischer's `Autopsie` should not be reduced to visual inspection. Across the 1845–1846 journeys he repeatedly combines direct sight with counting, measuring, weighing, calculation, material sampling, technical questioning and explicit judgements about the reliability of observers and conditions of observation.

A useful working term is **travelling measurement regime**:

`see -> count -> measure -> ask -> weigh -> calculate -> compare -> sample -> record`.

This is not a claim that Fischer had a formal laboratory protocol. It is a description of recurring operations in the supplied texts.

## 1. `Vergleichungspunkte` and `Autopsie`

In the 1845 preface Fischer retrospectively describes early London work as giving him `Vergleichungspunkte` for comparing British industry with the Continent. He later states that the scale and perfection of English industrial production could not be understood without `Autopsie`.

These are explicit actor terms and should anchor the later Wedding comparison.

## 2. Autopsy has limits

### Furnace heat and changing illumination

At Sanderson in Sheffield a furnace `Auge` is opened so Fischer can inspect flame and heat. He immediately qualifies the visual judgement: the apparent degree of heat is deceptive under bright versus dark weather.

This is important because direct sight is valuable but not automatically reliable.

### Railway speed destroys observation

In 1845 Fischer complains that the speed of railway travel makes it impossible to examine passing objects properly, much less describe them. The transport system that expands the geographical range of travel can therefore reduce the quality of observation en route.

### Butterley guide reliability

At Butterley in 1846 the intended technical director Jessop is absent and Fischer is shown around by a young counting-house clerk. Fischer explicitly states that the guide lacks technical knowledge outside his office and that his statements should not be regarded as infallible.

Thus observation quality depends on the authority and competence of the mediator, not merely on physical access.

## 3. The measuring traveller

### Cheapside paving, 1846

Fischer does not simply describe a new paving system. He writes that he measured several granite blocks with his own `Massstab` and records dimensions of approximately 3 inches thick, 15 inches long and 9 inches wide.

The ruler is part of the travel method.

### Liverpool warehouse / quay structure

Fischer counts 52 iron columns, records their spacing at 18 feet, and uses this to estimate the structure at nearly 1,000 feet long; he also records its depth at 50 feet.

This is observation converted into scale by counting plus elementary calculation.

### Bettely & Roberts anchor-chain works

At the anchor-and-chain works Fischer:

- counts 16 fires;
- is told the chain will be 300 yards / 900 feet long;
- measures one link at 9 inches;
- estimates its section at about 2 inches;
- asks the proprietor for the link's weight;
- when the proprietor does not know it exactly, a link is immediately weighed: 20 pounds;
- Fischer then calculates the approximate weight of the complete chain.

This is one of the clearest examples of his travelling measurement regime because visual inspection is repeatedly converted into counted, measured, weighed and calculated quantities.

## 4. Production quantities as comparison

### Low Moor

Fischer records blast pressure, ore grade, limestone charge, fuel ratios and costs. These measurements become part of a comparison with other production systems.

The most important material-control practice is separate: a sample is taken from each tapping and the batch is numbered for delivery by specified quality.

### Sanderson

Fischer asks for coke consumption per quantity of steel, repeats the question because he doubts he heard correctly, then compares the answer with his own average consumption. The reported response is `You beat us!` repeated three times.

The repetition of the question matters: he verifies the quantitative datum before using it comparatively.

## 5. Apparatus makes material quality measurable

The Sheffield locomotive-spring `Probemaschine` converts quality into a controlled operation:

- a hardened curved spring is placed in an oiled iron channel;
- a slowly driven screw presses it until the bow is flattened;
- load is removed;
- the spring is placed against reference pins;
- return to the expected geometry indicates that it has neither permanently yielded nor broken.

Fischer also records dimensions of tested springs.

Here measurement is no longer only the traveller's operation. It is built into the industrial testing apparatus and reference geometry.

## 6. Embodied sensory measurement survives beside apparatus

The same Sheffield visit shows that controlled machinery does not eliminate skilled bodily judgement. In the file-hardening shop Fischer estimates the temperature of the hardening water by feel at roughly 15–18 degrees Réaumur. He then tastes the water and says the added substance used to strengthen its action is immediately recognisable to him.

This is analytically important. The observational regime is hybrid:

`instrument / reference geometry + hand/eye/taste + accumulated craft experience`.

The later transition to laboratory testing should therefore not be narrated as a simple replacement of subjective senses by instruments. One question for Wedding/Martens is which sensory judgements are displaced, disciplined, preserved or re-encoded by specimen preparation and optical apparatus.

## 7. Sample, number and measurement form different evidential operations

The Fischer corpus now requires these categories to remain distinct:

- **visual autopsy**: direct inspection of a process/site;
- **counting**: furnaces, columns, machines, outputs;
- **linear measurement**: ruler/known scale;
- **weighing**: direct material quantity established by balance;
- **calculation**: derived quantity from measured/countable inputs;
- **production sampling**: selecting matter from a batch/process;
- **batch numbering**: linking sample and production output;
- **external trial**: another institution or expert subjects the material to a test;
- **apparatus test**: controlled mechanical transformation and reference condition;
- **expert sensory judgement**: skilled assessment by eye, hand, taste or process familiarity;
- **provenance judgement**: assessing whether the material's known history is adequate to support quality claims.

Do not collapse all of these into the single label `test`.

## 8. Provenance as a condition of quality

In London in October 1846 the steel merchant Fenn tells Fischer that English cast steel is no longer as good as formerly. His explanation is competition: steel makers buy old railway carriage springs and similar material whose quality they themselves do not know, then melt the heterogeneous material together to obtain cheap steel.

This provides a useful negative control against Low Moor's sample-plus-number practice.

Low Moor:

`production event -> sample -> number -> retained identity / specified quality`

Fenn's complaint:

`old components -> unknown prior quality -> mixed remelting -> provenance loss -> declining / unreliable quality`

The contrast makes **material identity and provenance** a research variable rather than a background detail.

This may later matter for the parent glass-carrier problem as well, but no cross-material historical connection should be inferred without evidence.

## 9. Vision can itself become an object of reflection

The 1846 visit to the Polytechnic Institution offers a useful peripheral control. During dissolving views Fischer deliberately fixes his gaze on stable details in an attempt not to be deceived by the image transition, but reports that the transformation still defeats his effort. In the same institution, catalogue-numbered objects, demonstrations, lectures and `working models` are used to make scientific/practical knowledge perceptible and clear (`versinnlichet und verdeutlichet`).

This should remain a secondary representation/demonstration strand, not evidence of metallography. Its value is that Fischer explicitly reflects on how visual effects, models and demonstrations organise perception.

## 10. Comparison with later metallography: claim ceiling

The Fischer material supports a prehistory of **operations of comparison** but not a direct genealogy to microscopic metallography.

The later Wedding/Martens question should therefore ask what changes when:

- production-scale quantities become specimen-scale features;
- portable material samples become deliberately prepared sections/surfaces;
- the traveller's eye, hand, taste and ruler are supplemented/replaced/retrained by optical and mechanical instruments;
- batch or mechanical-test records become microphotographic/printed reference series;
- access to a factory becomes institutional access to a testing laboratory and collection;
- provenance is preserved through labels, specimen series and photographic records rather than only through the traveller's notes and memory.

## Wedding extraction rule

When indexing Wedding Mss 23–25, code every relevant passage with one or more of:

`SEE | COUNT | MEASURE | WEIGH | CALCULATE | SAMPLE | NUMBER | APPARATUS_TEST | EXPERT_JUDGEMENT | SENSORY_JUDGEMENT | PROVENANCE | IMAGE | ACCESS | RELIABILITY_WARNING`

Also record who supplied the datum and whether Wedding observed it himself, received it orally, copied it from a document/drawing, or inferred it.

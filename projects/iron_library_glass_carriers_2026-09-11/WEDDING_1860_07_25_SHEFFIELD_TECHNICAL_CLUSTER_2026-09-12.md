## 4. What is presently secure inside the Bessemer block

### Material regime

Wedding records that the works used pig iron (`Roheisen`) mostly from **Cumberland**. The exact continuation of the sentence remains unresolved.

### Converter/process geometry

PDF 298 contains a large pear-shaped tilting converter/vessel on a horizontal trunnion axis, with sectional/base/tuyere-type details and grouped blast/opening marks. PDF 299 contains a further vessel/spout sketch.

### Process route

The later cross-control supports, at B-level rather than diplomatic level, that the charge/process begins with iron melted in a small/cupola-type furnace and run/tapped into the converting vessel. Exact nouns, syntax and transfer sequence must still be checked against the page image before quotation.

### Process duration

The sentence **`Der ganze Prozeß dauert cca 19–24 Minuten.`** is image-legible and should be retained as the secure total-process timing statement.

### Flame/stage observation

The surrounding prose visibly divides the process into stages and refers to emitted material / `Flamme` and time. Exact stage wording is still open. Preserve this as a palaeographic target rather than normalising it from OCR.

## 5. Public description versus site calibration

The 1856 patent and the 1859 published Bessemer description already made the main process architecture public: air through molten iron, a rotating/axis vessel, general tuyere architecture, flame as a process indicator, Sheffield as a practical development site, suitable hematite raw material and rapid conversion.

The notebook should therefore not be framed as a cache of intrinsically secret Bessemer knowledge. Its stronger evidential function is:

**publicly describable process → situated inspection/demonstration → locally calibrated operational notebook record.**

Potential site-calibration features include current works material use (`meist aus Cumberland`), Wedding's own sketching, total-process timing (`cca 19–24 Minuten`), process-stage observations and the juxtaposition with other Sheffield works.

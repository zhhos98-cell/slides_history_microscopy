## 2. Working project problem

Do not frame this as a general history of metallography or as a simple diffusion story `Sorby → Germany`.

Preferred problem:

> **How did preparation, glass supports, illumination and representation make different microscopic structures of iron visible and credible in Sheffield and Berlin?**

Alternative sharper formulation:

> **When Sheffield and Berlin microscopists disagreed about the structure of iron, were they looking at the same material object?**

Analytical unit: **preparation regime** — the linked operations that turn industrial material into a microscopic object capable of comparison, storage, re-inspection and public argument.

## 3. Sheffield regime: Sorby, c. 1863–65

Controlled secondary/object-level reconstruction from the surviving Sorby metallurgical corpus:

- 115 metallurgical specimens survive in the catalogued corpus.
- Early samples were made into thin polished sections on roughly **40 mm square glass supports** and protected with cover glass.
- Preparation involved filing, hand grinding with powdered rotten stone on glass, polishing with fine rouge on wash-leather, dilute nitric-acid etching, washing/drying and mounting.
- A 10 November 1863 Parkgate Iron Works letter concerns a deliberately marked `series of samples` from one heat and warns against mixing the marks.
- Sorby and Sheffield photographer Charles Hoole produced early metallurgical photomicrographs c. 1863–65; four images of cast iron, armour plate, cast steel and meteoric iron were exhibited in 1864.

Working sequence:

**provenanced industrial sample → marked series → glass-supported polished section → etching → controlled illumination → microscopic comparison → image / collection.**

Glass is therefore not the topic by itself. It matters where it materially stabilises or transforms the microscopic object.

## 4. Berlin regime before 1885

Martens's microscopic work is early enough that the project does not need a 1890s extension.

Secure bibliography:

- A. Martens, `Ueber die mikroskopische Untersuchung des Eisens`, *Zeitschrift des Vereines Deutscher Ingenieure* 22 (1878), with continuations/pages cited in later technical bibliographies; further contribution in vol. 24 (1880).
- Related 1882 publication in the Verein zur Beförderung des Gewerbfleisses proceedings.
- **A. Martens, `Erläuterungen einer in der kön. Bergakademie zu Berlin befindlichen Sammlung von 120 Schliffen zur Darstellung des mikroskopischen Gefüges verschiedener Eisen und Stahlsorten` (Berlin, 1884).**

The 120 sections are not an inferred teaching collection: contemporaneous/later technical bibliography gives the exact 1884 title, and a 1900 *Stahl und Eisen* exchange still refers to the `120 Schliffen` in the Royal Mining Academy collection and to specimen **no. 36** specifically. The collection therefore remained individually addressable at least into 1900. Its present physical fate remains open.

## 5. Wedding–Martens: collection commissioned for an argument

Wedding's 1885 account states that Martens had, **at Wedding's request**, prepared a collection of **120 sections** for the Royal Mining Academy in Berlin, and Wedding's investigation was based on that collection.

Wedding's described preparation chain includes:

1. taking small test pieces from a larger iron mass by breaking, planing, filing or crushing;
2. rough grinding on a coarse emery wheel;
3. progressively finer grinding on cast-iron plates with emery;
4. fixing the sample with resin/wax cement to a thick piece of **looking-glass**;
5. using water to prevent heat from loosening the cement during grinding;
6. hand polishing with fine abrasives;
7. etching with strongly diluted reagents;
8. controlled heating to produce differential colours;
9. in some cases, faint gilding by Martens.

Wedding stresses that **differences of colour**, rather than absolute colours, are characteristic.

This creates a direct material relation:

**Wedding request → Martens preparation → numbered 120-section collection → Wedding microscopic investigation → public paper.**

## 6. Central event: London, 1885 — corrected sequence

### Wedding presentation

Exact article:

H. Wedding, `The Properties of Malleable Iron, Deduced from its Microscopic Structure`, *Journal of the Iron and Steel Institute*, 1885 no. 1, **pp. 187–204**.

A contemporary *The Engineer* report dated **22 May 1885** records the meeting reception. It says Wedding's paper consisted substantially of descriptions of microscopic appearances; **no illustrations were shown**, making the result unsatisfactory for the reporter. The live discussion, in that report, was confined to **Hilary Bauermann and Henry Bessemer**.

Important correction:

> Do **not** write that Sorby and Wedding directly argued face-to-face during the live discussion unless a primary meeting record proves it. The strongest current evidence indicates Sorby's criticism came subsequently in writing.

### Sorby response

The *Journal of the Royal Microscopical Society* (1886) summarises Sorby's technical response and explicitly cites **`Colliery Guardian`, vol. XLIX (1885), p. 908**.

Sorby's criticism is about making the object:

- Wedding's sections had not been ground down with **soft stone before final polishing**;
- Sorby says he himself only saw the `ultimate structure` properly after adopting this step;
- Sorby also insists on the importance of **direct / vertical illumination**, distinguishing it from oblique illumination.

Thus the disagreement is not usefully reduced to theory. It concerns the material and optical conditions under which the microscopic structure exists as visible evidence.

### Berlin's material answer

Later reconstruction records that Wedding invited Iron and Steel Institute members to Berlin to inspect Martens's **120 metallographic specimens** on which his paper was based. No evidence has yet been found that the invitation was taken up.

Working event sequence:

**prepared Berlin collection → verbal/textual description in London without adequate displayed images → difficulty of public verification → Sorby written critique of preparation + illumination → invitation to inspect the physical Berlin collection.**

This is currently the strongest centre for the fellowship proposal.

## 7. Representation is part of the problem, not decoration

The 1885 event exposes a second preparation problem: how a microscopic object becomes publicly inspectable.

- Wedding's London presentation was criticised precisely because no illustrations were shown.
- Wedding's published JISI paper contains engravings; later analysis notes problems in numbering/fit between some engravings and descriptions.
- Sorby's 1885 Iron and Steel Institute text existed as an **8-page preprint**.
- The fuller Sorby paper was not published in JISI until 1887, with **six plates / photographic and heliographic reproductions**; later reconstruction attributes the delay partly to preparing Woodburytype reproductions.

Therefore a bounded sub-question is justified:

> How did a prepared metal surface become an inspectable public image, and what happened when reproduction failed or lagged behind observation?

Do not turn this into a general history of scientific photography.

- Sorby/Hoole produced early metallurgical photomicrographs in Sheffield in the 1860s.

## 13. Current best proposal skeleton

Possible title:

**Making the Microscopic Object: Glass, Preparation and Metallurgical Knowledge between Sheffield and Berlin, c. 1863–1885**

Alternative:

**Different Structures: Microscopy, Glass and the Making of Metallurgical Evidence in Sheffield and Berlin, 1863–1885**

Three compact sections:

1. **Sheffield, 1863–65 — preparing the object:** industrial sample, provenance, glass support, grinding/polishing, etching, direct illumination, early photomicrography.
2. **Berlin, 1878–84 — making a collection:** Martens's methods and the numbered 120-section Royal Mining Academy collection commissioned by Wedding.
3. **London/Berlin, 1885 — making evidence public:** Wedding's paper without displayed illustrations, written Sorby critique of preparation/illumination, and the Berlin collection offered as a material ground for inspection.

Travel and transnational circulation remain mechanisms joining these local regimes; they are not a separate general topic.

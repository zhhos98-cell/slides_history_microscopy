## 1. Conceptual advance: evidential preparation

The strongest new bridge is not `microscopy` by itself but **preparation**: material is physically altered so that a selected property becomes inspectable, comparable and discussable.

Working non-teleological sequence:

**production record → traceable/staged material → travelling sample → expert judgement/external test → evidential preparation → exhibition arrangement/catalogue address → prepared microscopic section → numbered specimen collection → microscopic field → nature print/photomicrograph → printed/reproducible image → institutional reference archive.**

`Evidential preparation` is preferred to `pre-metallography`; the latter risks implying an inevitable genealogy.

## 2. Fischer 1851: Great Exhibition objects were deliberately prepared as evidence

The 1851 exhibition material should not be described simply as finished products sent to London. Contemporary catalogue descriptions show that Fischer's iron/steel objects were materially prepared to expose properties for inspection: examples include transverse cuts/fractures, acid treatment, bending/strain, and comparison of grain/crystallisation or Damascus patterning.

The analytical point is:

**material → cut / fracture / etch / bend / finish → selected visible property → public comparison.**

This remains macroscopic/material inspection, not microscopy.

The later jury/award language can compress a complex evidential display into a short bureaucratic class description. That contrast is useful: prepared material argument → catalogue description → jury/classification label.

## 3. Fischer 1851 diary reconnects exhibition objects to expert testing and privileged access

The GF digital edition of the 1851 travel journal supplies a continuation of the 1845–46 circuit.

### 25 June 1851, London

Fischer tells Swiss commissioner Colladon that his exhibition contribution includes Damascus/meteor steel and objects made from it, plus copper–iron alloy samples. He says he had initially been reluctant to exhibit and did so after Schaffhausen authorities asked for something to represent the canton.

He then visits Brande at the Royal Mint and shows him a copper–iron alloy sample (8 parts old iron, 3 parts copper). Brande engages with it as a chemist.

### 7–9 July 1851, Sheffield

- Fischer leaves meteor-steel razors with Rodgers & Sons for an expert opinion of their quality.
- At Johnson, Cammell & Co. he observes a spring test in which a new spring breaks before maximum straight compression.
- Johnson and the smelting foreman question him about preventing piping in cast steel; Fischer recognises an attempt to `pump me out` and deliberately withholds detailed process knowledge.
- Sanderson promises an accurate report on coke requirements for Fischer's ores and a hand drawing of a small blast furnace.
- At Turton & Sons Fischer is shown specimens that withstand rigorous tests; he receives privileged permission from Turton to visit all workshops freely.
- On 9 July Rodgers gives a favourable judgement on Fischer's razor blades and discusses steel, grain/crystal structure and Damascus appearance.

Thus 1851 extends the earlier circuit:

**prepared exhibition object → public classification → institutional/expert inspection → Sheffield test/judgement → privileged works access → selective technical disclosure.**

## 4. Great Exhibition process-series display was broader than Fischer

Targeted catalogue searching shows that Fischer was not unique in presenting materials/processes through staged or deliberately altered specimens.

Examples already located in the 1851 official catalogue include:

- Risca/iron exhibits showing iron in successive manufacturing states: pig iron → refined metal → puddled iron → merchant bar;
- Kirkstall railway tire bars/axles cut and broken to show method of manufacture and fibre, and bent cold to show toughness;
- Red Jacket Works specimens showing successive stages of copper smelting;
- cutlery/razor entries showing different stages of manufacture;
- gun-barrel displays presenting sequential stages and using acids to bring out the Damascus figure;
- Johnson, Cammell & Co. advertising/display material explicitly inviting the viewer to follow iron through carbonising, converting, melting and refining into a finished carriage spring.

A contemporaneous lecture on results of the Great Exhibition likewise states that many exhibitors illustrated processes with series showing progressive changes from raw material to finished product.

Controlled conclusion:

> `Evidential preparation` and staged process series were part of a wider Great Exhibition display repertoire, not merely Fischer's idiosyncrasy.

Do not yet call this a fully standardised exhibition-wide protocol; more systematic sampling across classes would be required.

## 8. Sorby: surviving specimens reveal a controlled sample-to-collection system

The Edyvean–Hammond catalogue of Sorby's surviving metallurgical work gives an unusually concrete object history.

Controlled findings:

- 115 metallurgical specimens survive in the catalogued corpus;
- early metallurgical samples were prepared as thin polished sections on roughly 40 mm square glass supports, etched and protected with cover glass;
- preparation involved filing, hand grinding with powdered rotten stone on glass, polishing with fine rouge on wash-leather, dilute nitric-acid etching, washing/drying and mounting;
- preparation was tedious and costly;
- a 10 November 1863 Parkgate Iron Works letter concerns a `series of samples` from one heat, deliberately marked to distinguish treatments/directions and warns that the marks must not become mixed;
- the surviving collection preserves specimen numbering/display arrangement;
- Sorby also used treated surfaces as `nature-printing` surfaces and, with Charles Hoole, made early metallurgical photomicrographs.

Analytical sequence:

**one heat → marked/provenanced sample series → differentiated mechanical treatment/orientation → prepared glass-mounted section → microscopic comparison → numbered collection / image reproduction.**

This is much stronger than describing Sorby simply as an early user of metallurgical microscopy.

## 9. Major Berlin bridge: Wedding commissioned Martens' 120-section collection

A contemporaneous 1885 English reprint of Wedding's `Properties of Malleable Iron Deduced from its Microscopic Structure` states that Martens had, **at Wedding's request**, prepared for the Royal Mining Academy of Berlin a **collection of 120 sections**, and that Wedding's ensuing investigations were based on this collection.

Wedding then describes the preparation procedure in detail:

1. small test pieces are taken from a larger iron mass by breaking, planing, filing or crushing;
2. rough grinding on a coarse emery wheel;
3. progressively finer grinding on cast-iron plates with emery;
4. fixation with resin/wax cement to a thick piece of looking-glass;
5. water used to avoid heat loosening the cement during grinding;
6. hand polishing with carefully washed ferric oxide, putty powder, tripoli, etc.;
7. etching with highly diluted reagents including platinum chloride, nitric/hydrochloric/acetic/salicylic acids or tincture of galls + acetic acid;
8. careful heating to produce differential colours;
9. in some cases faint gilding by Martens.

Wedding explicitly stresses that **the differences of colour, not the colours themselves, are characteristic**.

This materially strengthens the project:

> The 1884/85 Berlin microscopic argument rested on an intentionally prepared **collection**, not isolated observations.

The key relation is now documentary, not inferred:

**Wedding request/commission → Martens preparation → 120-section collection → Wedding investigation/public argument.**

## 10. Martens 1884 catalogue is now a high-priority missing primary source

A 1900 `Stahl und Eisen` discussion explicitly refers back to specimen no. 36 of this 120-section collection and says a gilding treatment produced different coloured deposits on softer and harder constituents. This confirms that the collection supported chemically/visually differentiated prepared surfaces, not merely polished passive samples.

Acquisition priority: very high.

## 11. Wedding–Sorby 1885 dispute is a dispute over how evidence is made

Secondary reconstruction from the Sorby specimen study records that Sorby attributed important differences between his results and Wedding's to:

- inadequate grinding/preparation (especially failure to grind down appropriately with soft stone before final polish);
- illumination, especially direct/vertical illumination.

Wedding formally invited Iron and Steel Institute members to Berlin to inspect Martens' 120 metallographic specimens underlying his paper.

Thus the disagreement can be framed as:

**which preparation + illumination regime yields a valid microscopic object?**

Do not reduce it to a theory dispute alone.

The invitation also matters institutionally: a specimen collection was offered as a material basis on which outsiders could inspect or contest the claim.

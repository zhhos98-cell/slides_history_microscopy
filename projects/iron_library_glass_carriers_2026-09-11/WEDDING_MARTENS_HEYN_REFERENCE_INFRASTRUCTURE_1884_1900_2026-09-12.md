# Martens / Wedding / Heyn — reference infrastructure and metallographic method, 1884–1900

Date: 2026-09-12

Status: PRIMARY / NEAR-CONTEMPORARY METHOD AND REFERENCE-SYSTEM CONTROL.

This note upgrades two previously open points in the Wedding metallography chain: (1) whether Martens' 1884 collection of 120 Schliffe had item-level reference identity, and (2) how the later German metallographic method explicitly located Wedding inside a preparation lineage rather than treating him only as an interpreter of microstructure.

## 1. Martens' 1884 collection had item-level reference numbers

The governing bibliographic object is:

**A. Martens, `Erläuterungen einer in der kön. Bergakademie zu Berlin befindlichen Sammlung von 120 Schliffen zur Darstellung des mikroskopischen Gefüges verschiedener Eisen- und Stahlsorten`, Berlin, 1884.**

Francis Scott Rice's 1897 bibliography independently records the title and the collection of 120 prepared sections.

More importantly, a *Stahl und Eisen* discussion published 1 January 1900 explicitly refers back to the 1884 Martens catalogue and identifies a specific object as:

**`Gießereiroheisen (Nr. 36)`**.

The same passage says Martens' 1884 explanation for this numbered specimen described gilding that produced a bronze-coloured coating on the softer constituent and a purple-coloured coating on the harder constituent.

Source:
- *Stahl und Eisen*, 1 Jan. 1900, discussion by E. Heyn, referring explicitly to Martens' 1884 `Erläuterungen ... 120 Schliffen` and `Nr. 36`.

### Consequence

The collection was not merely `120 specimens` in aggregate. At least some items possessed durable **reference numbers** capable of being cited sixteen years later together with their treatment/appearance history.

Therefore the earlier claim ceiling can be upgraded from:

`REFERENCE_COLLECTION = STRONG INFERENCE`

to:

**`ITEM_LEVEL_REFERENCE_IDENTITY = PRIMARY_CONTROLLED`**.

Do not yet infer that the complete sequence was exactly numbered 1–120 without the 1884 catalogue itself, but the existence of `Nr. 36` proves a persistent item-level indexing architecture.

## 2. The reference object stored more than material identity

The `Nr. 36` retrospective citation is methodologically important because it ties together:

**material category → item number → surface treatment → colour response → later textual retrieval.**

The prepared section therefore functions as more than a physical sample. It becomes a persistent reference object with an associated experimental/visual history.

Working infrastructure:

**specimen provenance → prepared Schliff → reference number → treatment record → observed response → later comparison/citation.**

This is directly relevant to the Iron Library problem of how material evidence becomes institutionally retrievable.

## 3. Heyn 1899 gives a mature contemporaneous preparation workflow

E. Heyn's `Einiges über das Kleingefüge des Eisens`, *Stahl und Eisen*, 1 Aug. 1899, pp.710ff, gives a detailed working preparation sequence.

Initial preparation:

- the surface is made plane and as smooth as possible;
- preparation continues on rotating wooden discs covered with emery papers of different grades until the surface is nearly free of scratches;
- final polishing is carried out on cloth-covered wooden discs using the finest polishing rouge (`Polirroth`) and water;
- a skilled worker can produce a perfectly mirror-bright, scratch-free surface;
- workshop preparation is said to take roughly **2–6 hours**.

The polished surface usually still does not reveal the structure and requires further treatment.

Source:
- *Stahl und Eisen*, 1 Aug. 1899, lines corresponding to printed p.710–711.

## 4. Relief polishing is explicitly placed in a Sorby → Osmond/Martens/Wedding lineage

Heyn then distinguishes three principal methods of further treatment. For the first, **Reliefpoliren**, he writes that it was:

**introduced by Sorby and substantially perfected by Osmond, Martens and Wedding.**

The procedure uses gentle pressure on soft rubber or parchment moistened with a small amount of polishing rouge. Harder constituents resist the elastic abrasive surface more strongly than softer constituents and consequently stand out in relief, allowing harder and softer structural components to be distinguished.

Source:
- *Stahl und Eisen*, 1 Aug. 1899, printed p.711.

### Consequence for Sorby–Wedding relation

This is later than the 1885/86 disagreement and does not prove direct personal transmission. It does establish that by 1899 a German practitioner inside the Martens institutional environment explicitly located Wedding inside a **method lineage beginning with Sorby's relief-polishing technique**.

Use:

**`SORBY → RELIEFPOLISHING ORIGIN; OSMOND/MARTENS/WEDDING → SUBSTANTIAL METHOD DEVELOPMENT`**

not:

`Sorby personally taught Wedding`.

## 5. Heyn separates three surface-development regimes

The same article distinguishes:

### a. Relief polishing

Differential mechanical removal creates topographic relief based on hardness differences.

### b. Etch polishing (`Aetzpoliren`)

Attributed to Osmond. The relief-polished specimen is polished further on rubber/parchment while water is replaced by another medium; structural constituents can acquire colour differences in addition to relief.

### c. Etching (`Aetzen`)

The cloth-prepolished section is degreased and immersed in an etching bath. Heyn lists possible reagents including:

- iodine in potassium iodide + water;
- strongly diluted nitric acid;
- hydrochloric acid strongly diluted with alcohol;
- related reagents.

Etching produces differential colour/attack that yields additional structural information.

This makes explicit that a `metallographic image` can be generated through materially different surface-construction procedures.

## 6. Illumination is a second independent methodological axis

Heyn then treats illumination for microscopy and photography separately from specimen preparation.

He describes multiple reflected-light geometries:

1. specimen inclined obliquely to the microscope axis and illuminated by oblique diffuse daylight;
2. specimen perpendicular to the optical axis, illuminated via a plane-parallel glass plate set at approximately 45°, which reflects incident artificial light onto the polished surface while returning reflected light along the microscope axis;
3. specimen perpendicular to the optical axis, with a prism above the objective redirecting incident light toward the object, the objective serving both to concentrate illumination and to form the microscopic image.

Source:
- *Stahl und Eisen*, 1 Aug. 1899, printed p.711.

### Consequence

This confirms at mature-practice level the distinction already exposed by the Sorby–Wedding dispute:

**standardising the specimen surface and standardising the optical field are separate operations.**

Even an identically prepared surface can become visually different under oblique diffuse illumination, coaxial reflected illumination, prism illumination, or other optical arrangements.

## 7. Image conventions themselves become standardised

Heyn explicitly tells the reader how to interpret figure lighting: the illustrations are arranged so that light is imagined as falling from the left, allowing raised/depressed structural features to be read from the direction of the light edge.

This is a further carrier layer:

**physical relief → illumination convention → graphic/image convention → shared interpretation.**

Thus standardisation extends beyond preparation and optics into representational convention.

## 8. Relationship to Wedding's handbook method

A separate *Stahl und Eisen* paper later in 1899 states that its specimen surfaces were prepared according to Wedding's instructions in his `Handbuch der Eisenhüttenkunde`, I.2. That paper gives controlled dimensions/orientation/cold-saw preparation, while its diffuse illumination is explicitly the authors' own apparatus-limited choice.

Together the two 1899 texts show:

- Wedding's preparation instructions had become reproducible by third parties;
- the broader German metallographic community explicitly credited Wedding as a method developer;
- optical/image conditions could still vary independently after specimen standardisation.

## 9. Revised infrastructure chain

The strongest institutional sequence is now:

**Martens 1884 prepared-section collection → item-level reference identity (`Nr. 36` securely controlled) → Wedding 1885 comparative interpretation → Sorby preparation critique → Wedding/Martens/Osmond method development → explicit preparation and illumination workflows → handbook codification → third-party reproduction → photographic/graphic reference objects.**

This is not just a history of microscopy. It is a history of a reference infrastructure composed of:

**objects + numbers + preparation recipes + optical arrangements + image conventions + publications.**

## 10. Updated claim ceilings

### Secure

- Martens published a 1884 catalogue for a Berlin collection of 120 iron/steel Schliffe;
- the collection had item-level reference numbering: `Nr. 36` is explicitly re-cited in 1900;
- that reference number was linked to material type and an earlier recorded surface-treatment/colour response;
- Heyn 1899 gives a detailed preparation workflow from planar grinding through mirror polishing;
- Heyn explicitly credits Sorby with introducing relief polishing and Osmond, Martens and Wedding with substantially perfecting it;
- Heyn distinguishes relief polishing, etch polishing and chemical etching;
- Heyn separately distinguishes multiple illumination geometries for observation/photography;
- Heyn standardises figure-light direction as an interpretive convention.

### Strong inference

- Martens' collection functioned as a persistent material/reference database rather than a merely demonstrative set;
- the German metallographic programme increasingly standardised not only specimens but retrieval, preparation, illumination and representation.

# Iron Library residency / digitisation triage — 2026-09-11

This note records the second research pass that grew out of the glass-carrier project. It is deliberately broader than the original three-plate problem, but it remains holdings-led: the purpose is to distinguish material that can be exhausted remotely from material that could justify an Iron Library Scholar in Residence application.

## 1. Decision rule

A residency case should not be built around material that is already completely available online. The strongest application corpus should satisfy several of the following at once:

- not digitised, or only partly digitised;
- too large/dispersed for a one-off reproduction request;
- requires comparison across several archival/library series;
- contains physical features that matter (glass plates, inscriptions, plate sequence, dimensions, mounting, retouching, envelopes, marginalia, working order);
- requires matching objects to printed illustrations or to other carriers;
- generates a research question from the holdings rather than using the fellowship as a reason to invent one.

## 2. Material that is already substantially remote-accessible

### Hermann Wedding manuscripts, Eisenbibliothek Mss 23–25

These are not a sufficient onsite rationale. All three have been fully digitised on e-codices since 14 December 2017 and have public IIIF manifests.

- `Mss 23` — 1856–1857 Freiberg smelting notes; 208 pp.; DOI `10.5076/e-codices-ebs-0023`; IIIF `https://www.e-codices.ch/metadata/iiif/ebs-0023/manifest.json`.
- `Mss 24` — 1858 metallurgical study tour through German industrial regions; 98 pp.; DOI `10.5076/e-codices-ebs-0024`; IIIF `https://www.e-codices.ch/metadata/iiif/ebs-0024/manifest.json`.
- `Mss 25` — 1860–1862 metallurgical journeys through Germany, Belgium and England; 328 + 48 pp.; DOI `10.5076/e-codices-ebs-0025`; IIIF `https://www.e-codices.ch/metadata/iiif/ebs-0025/manifest.json`.

Mss 25 is nevertheless an important remote baseline. The catalogue explicitly says that it documents Wedding's British journeys, operating facilities and production processes, first Bessemer-process steelworks, and connections with contemporary specialists. The Sheffield/person-network question should be tested against the manuscript pages, but the catalogue summary alone does not prove a specific Sorby encounter.

### Johann Conrad Fischer travel journals

The Iron Library's 2023 digital edition publishes seven travel journals (1794, 1814, 1825, 1825–1827, 1845, 1846, 1851) in a TEI Publisher environment, with English translation completed by version 1.2. The editorial guidelines state that the reference texts are Iron Library copies digitised on e-rara and that OCR was checked against those copies.

This means Fischer's repeated British/Sheffield travel is excellent contextual evidence but weak as an onsite-access argument. The digital edition already gives itineraries, indexed people/places/terms, and direct access to the underlying printed travel accounts.

## 3. Material that still matters precisely because remote access is incomplete

### Droste / Martens original microphotographic glass plates

Bernhard Droste states that he donated three original early microphotographic glass plates from the Berlin material-testing institution to the Iron Library. His 2024 article is public online, but the three physical objects are not yet resolved item-by-item in the public catalogue.

Current unresolved points remain:

- whether `G 745,1` is an object/accession signature or an image/photograph identifier;
- individual plate signatures;
- date, dimensions, photographic process and inscriptions;
- front/back markings, mounting and condition;
- exact match to Martens printed plates;
- whether any unpublished physical information survives that is invisible in the published figure.

This remains a strong onsite candidate because the object-level comparison may require the original plates even after a digital surrogate is supplied.

## 4. Historiographical ceiling: what is already written

The broad story `Sorby → Martens/Wedding → metallography` is not a novelty claim. The metallurgical work of Henry Clifton Sorby and the later history of metallography already discuss:

- Sorby's 1860s metallurgical microscopy;
- Wedding's 1885 paper, `The properties of malleable iron, deduced from its microscopic structure`;
- Sorby's criticism that differences in Wedding's results could arise from specimen preparation (especially grinding with soft stone before final polishing) and illumination;
- the wider place of Martens and Berlin material testing in metallographic practice.

Therefore the project should not be framed as discovery of an unknown transmission chain or as a priority dispute over the invention of metallography.

## 5. Stronger research problem now visible

The more defensible question is about **observational regimes across scale and media**.

Wedding's early notebooks and journeys belong to a world of industrial travel: works, furnaces, process layouts, plant comparison and technical drawing. Later metallography reduces the observational field to a prepared specimen and a polished surface. Microphotography then fixes a selected field onto another glass carrier and printed plates circulate it further.

A useful working sequence is:

`industrial travel / works comparison → material specimen → cutting/grinding/polishing → reflected-light microscopic field → photographic glass plate → printed plate → reference collection / archive`.

The possible continuity to test is not simply a technology transfer from Sheffield to Berlin. It is whether a comparative way of knowing materials survived while the scale, apparatus and durable evidence changed.

This also reframes the 1885 Wedding–Sorby disagreement. The dispute is useful because the visible structure of iron could depend on how the section was produced and illuminated. The epistemic object is therefore made through preparation, not merely revealed by a microscope.

## 6. Network leads: secure connection vs hypothesis

### Secure external connection: Percy ↔ Story-Maskelyne

The Royal Society's `Science in the Making` catalogue contains John Percy's 1865 referee report (`RR/5/143`) on Story-Maskelyne's paper `On new cornish minerals of the brochantite group`. This is a direct archival connection between the metallurgy/mineralogy worlds and should be treated as a documented relation, not as proof of a broader network by itself.

### New preparation lead: Story-Maskelyne ↔ Tutton, 1894

The Royal Society also preserves Story-Maskelyne's referee report (`RR/12/397`, 28 December 1894) on Alfred Edwin Howard Tutton's instrument for **cutting, grinding and polishing section-plates and prisms of mineral or other crystals accurately in desired directions**. This is potentially important for the preparation side of the project because it puts Story-Maskelyne directly inside a late nineteenth-century discussion of producing controlled mineral/crystal sections.

### Hypothesis to test: Wedding / Sheffield / Sorby

Mss 25 makes Wedding's 1860–1862 British/Bessemer investigation first-order, but a direct Wedding–Sorby encounter has not yet been demonstrated. The correct procedure is to establish Wedding's Sheffield dates and named contacts from the manuscript, then compare against Sorby's diaries and local industrial correspondence. Co-presence is not treated as contact.

## 7. Residency logic after the digitisation check

Wedding and Fischer should now be treated as **pre-visit corpora**: read them before applying. Their value is to generate precise archival questions and names.

A plausible onsite case would instead have to combine a thicker body of material such as:

1. unresolved original Martens/Droste glass plates and their physical metadata;
2. undigitised GF working papers or correspondence relevant to material comparison/testing;
3. nineteenth-century printed metallurgical/mineralogical holdings that permit plate-to-object comparison;
4. photographic or exhibition material whose carrier, sequence, annotations or working order cannot be reconstructed from catalogue metadata alone.

The application threshold is reached only when the onsite corpus is demonstrably too thick and materially specific to replace with a small reproduction request.

1. Harvest the public Wedding IIIF manifests locally and use them as a remote research corpus; do not mirror the full image set into the public GitHub repository without checking republication rights.

# Fischer 1845–1846 — sample, traceability and reference regimes

Status: active evidence note, 2026-09-11.

Source basis: supplied machine-readable Fischer 1845 and 1846 travel texts. OCR/research texts only; not manually verified diplomatic transcriptions.

## Core finding

`Muster` / sample should not be treated as a single evidential category in Fischer. The 1845–1846 texts show several materially and epistemically different sample regimes.

At minimum distinguish:

1. **demonstration sample** — a portable object shown to others in order to solicit recognition, judgement or business;
2. **production-trace sample** — matter taken from a specific production event and linked to a numbered batch;
3. **staged process series** — samples deliberately assembled across successive production stages;
4. **reference-quality sample** — a tested sample becomes the benchmark against which future deliveries are specified;
5. **prepared expert-judgement object** — material is transformed/finished by a specialist before quality is assessed;
6. **inscribed provenance object** — maker/source identity is physically carried on the object itself through travel and external transformation.

These categories are analytically useful for the later Wedding/Martens comparison because a metallographic specimen/reference collection should not be treated as merely the same thing at smaller scale.

## 1. Demonstration samples: Fischer carries novelty through a judgement circuit

In 1845 Fischer leaves home carrying pieces of his cast wrought iron and other products `als Muster`. He declares the pieces at customs because they matter to him materially and commercially.

He shows such pieces successively to industrial/scientific interlocutors. In Basel a sample is examined with file and hammer; in Paris Bréant and Barre inspect cast soft wrought-iron components; in London Brande examines related objects; Faraday later examines cast-wrought-iron pieces, platinum and Fischer's experimental crucible.

Working sequence:

`portable novel object -> show to recognised interlocutor -> tactile/mechanical inspection -> judgement / recognition / order / further conversation`.

The sample functions here as a travelling claim about what Fischer can make.

## 2. Production-trace samples: Low Moor links each tapping to a numbered identity

At Low Moor Fischer states that pig iron is not always identical in grain. A sample is therefore taken from **every tapping** and the tapping/output is numbered. Fischer directly connects this practice with supplying consumers with iron of specified quality.

Working sequence:

`production event -> sample -> batch number -> quality distinction -> customer delivery`.

This is the strongest pre-laboratory example in the Fischer corpus of sample identity being maintained through a production/commerce chain.

Its key feature is not simply sampling but **traceability**.

## 3. Staged process series: Low Moor supplies matter across the production sequence

At the end of the Low Moor visit Fischer receives ore, limestone, coal, coke and iron samples through all stages from pig iron to finished bar iron.

This is not the same operation as taking one sample from each tapping.

The staged series abstracts a process into a portable material sequence:

`raw inputs -> intermediate states -> finished iron`.

It therefore allows comparison away from the works by putting process stages side by side materially.

This is a useful structural comparator for later deliberately ordered specimen/reference series, but there is no evidence here of metallographic preparation or microscopy.

## 4. Reference-quality sample: the Royal Mint `Musterstange`

Brande's 1 January 1846 Royal Mint letter reports trials of the steel bar Fischer had sent and states that the Mint is satisfied. Brande requests further bars and makes future preference conditional on Fischer supplying steel **of the same quality as the sample bar** (`in gleicher Qualität, als wie die Musterstange`).

This changes the epistemic role of the object.

The sample bar is no longer only:

`an object that was tested`.

It becomes:

`a tested object whose quality specifies the required quality of future objects`.

Working sequence:

`sample -> institutional test -> favourable result -> sample quality becomes normative reference -> future batches judged against expected sameness`.

This is currently the strongest Fischer example of a portable material object becoming a **reference standard in commercial/technical practice**.

Do not equate it with a modern certified reference material. The source supports a practical normative comparison, not formal metrological standardisation.

## 5. Object inscription is a repeated provenance practice, not a one-off detail

Two separate journeys give explicit evidence that Fischer marks his own material with his name before sending it through external technical operations.

### 1845: named steel piece at Huntsman's hammer works

Fischer brings from Schaffhausen a piece of his own steel `mit meinem Namen bezeichnet` specifically so that it can be drawn/forged thinner at Huntsman's hammer works. Huntsman is absent, but Fischer identifies himself to the foreman, requests the transformation and obtains immediate access.

The useful sequence is:

`Fischer steel -> maker/name mark already attached -> travel to Sheffield -> external hammering/drawing -> object remains attributable to source`.

The name mark matters because the object is about to be materially transformed elsewhere. Provenance is not merely remembered in the travel narrative; identity is physically attached before the operation.

### 1846: named razor-blade blanks sent to Rodgers & Son

Fischer brings two razor-blade blanks made from his own steel. He says they had been forged in Schaffhausen, **marked with his name** (`mit meinem Namen gezeichnet`) and hardened before being handed to Rodgers & Son for grinding, polishing, fitting with scales and expert judgement of steel quality.

Working sequence:

`known Fischer steel -> forged/hardened in Schaffhausen -> maker/source inscription on object -> Sheffield grinding/polishing/fitting -> expert quality judgement`.

Taken together, the 1845 and 1846 examples support a recurring practice:

**maker-marked object -> external transformation -> retained material identity for comparison/judgement**.

This is stronger than treating inscription as an incidental razor-blade detail.

Do not infer that every Fischer sample was marked, or that the marking survived every operation in a technically unchanged form. The sources support repeated intentional name-marking on at least two externally transformed steel objects.

## 6. Prepared expert-judgement objects: preparation and provenance-preservation coexist

The Rodgers episode is especially valuable because the object is both **identified** and **prepared**. Its assessability depends on skilled external finishing, while source identity has been deliberately attached before that finishing begins.

This gives a useful structural control for later metallography:

`identified material -> preparation that changes material surface/form -> judgement while identity must remain recoverable`.

Later metallographic specimens may preserve identity by inscription, labels, numbers, mounts or associated records while cutting/grinding/polishing/etching materially alters the object. That later system must be demonstrated from Wedding/Martens evidence; Fischer only establishes that preparation and provenance-preservation were already compatible operations in industrial comparison.

## 7. Samples can lose epistemic value when provenance is destroyed

Fenn's 1846 complaint about declining English cast-steel quality provides the inverse case. Manufacturers buy old railway springs and similar scrap whose original quality they themselves do not know and remelt it `pêle-mêle` to obtain cheap steel.

Thus:

`known production identity / traceable or marked object`

has an explicit opposite:

`unknown prior quality -> heterogeneous mixing -> provenance loss -> unstable/declining resulting quality`.

This reinforces provenance as a controlled research variable.

## 8. Numbering and inscription are different identity technologies

The Fischer material now contains several distinct ways of maintaining or organising object identity.

### Low Moor: production numbering

Batch/tapping numbering preserves the identity of industrial output linked to routine sampling.

### Fischer steel objects: direct object inscription

At least two objects are explicitly marked with Fischer's name before entering external transformations in Sheffield: a steel piece in 1845 and razor-blade blanks in 1846. This anchors maker/source identity directly to the object.

### Polytechnic Institution, London: catalogue numbering

Fischer describes a collection of more than 2,000 objects in which objects are numbered in correspondence with a catalogue. Assigned staff explain scientific and practical aspects of the objects; working models supplement lectures.

This is not a material-testing reference collection. But it is a useful control showing **catalogue-numbered institutional ordering** of technical/scientific objects.

The later Martens/material-testing question can therefore distinguish:

- production numbering for traceability;
- inscription/marking on the object for maker/source identity across transformation;
- catalogue numbering for collection retrieval/order;
- later specimen/image numbering, labels or inscriptions for comparative scientific reference, if the evidence supports it.

Do not assume these are genealogically connected.

## 9. Current sample/reference taxonomy for Wedding coding

When reading Wedding Mss 23–25, do not code all `Muster`, `Probe`, specimen or carried objects simply as `SAMPLE`.

Use, where evidence permits:

`DEMONSTRATION_SAMPLE | TRACE_SAMPLE | BATCH_IDENTITY | STAGED_SERIES | REFERENCE_QUALITY_SAMPLE | PREPARED_TEST_OBJECT | OBJECT_INSCRIPTION | SAMPLE_DONOR | SAMPLE_RECIPIENT | PROVENANCE_LOSS | CATALOG_NUMBERING`

Also record:

- who selected the object;
- whether it came from one identifiable production event;
- whether identity is attached by batch number, writing/inscription on the object, tag/label, mount or associated document;
- whether identity remains recoverable after cutting, hammering, grinding, polishing, hardening or other transformation;
- whether its value lies in representativeness, traceability, novelty or reference quality;
- whether it was modified/prepared before judgement;
- whether preparation preserved or obscured provenance;
- whether future material was explicitly required to resemble/match it;
- whether it entered a collection or remained a travelling personal sample.

## 10. Relevance to later specimen/microphotograph collections

The Fischer baseline makes a more precise later question possible.

Instead of asking simply when `samples` became `specimens`, ask what changes across these operations:

**mid-century industrial/travel regimes**

`portable demonstration sample`

`production event -> trace sample + batch number`

`process -> staged portable material series`

`tested sample -> reference quality for future supply`

`maker-marked object -> external transformation -> identity retained`

`known object + source mark -> skilled finishing -> expert judgement`

versus later possible **laboratory/metallographic regimes**

`selected material -> source identity/label? -> cut/grind/polish/etch? -> labelled specimen -> microscopic field -> photograph -> numbered reference series/collection`.

The later sequence is a research hypothesis until controlled in Wedding/Martens sources.

## Claim ceiling

The Fischer sources support distinct practical roles for samples, numbering and repeated maker/source inscription. They do **not** establish:

- formal statistical sampling;
- modern metrological reference standards;
- direct institutional descent from Low Moor/Royal Mint/Fischer object marking to Berlin metallography;
- that the Low Moor staged samples entered a permanent ordered collection;
- that catalogue numbering at the Polytechnic Institution influenced metallurgical sample numbering;
- that all Fischer samples were individually marked or labelled;
- that the precise physical form of each name mark survived every subsequent transformation.

Those stronger claims require later evidence.

# Wedding / Bessemer — pre-1860 publication versus 1860 Sheffield site note

Date: 2026-09-11

Status: SOURCE-MODE CROSS-CONTROL. This note asks which features in Wedding's July 1860 `Bessemer's Stahlwerk` pages were already public before his visit and which are better treated as situated operational calibration. It is designed to prevent the newly located manuscript section from being over-read as secret or uniquely privileged knowledge.

## 1. Public technical knowledge available before Wedding's July 1860 visit

### Bessemer patent, 1856

Bessemer's US patent no. 16,082 (patented 11 Nov. 1856) already published a detailed vessel/process architecture with drawings. It describes:

- a vessel that can be rotated on its axis;
- tuyere passages / air delivery through the molten iron;
- visible flame and gaseous products as part of the process;
- process-state judgement from the **colour and volume of the flame**;
- trial ingots as another control method;
- an early whole-process duration of roughly **30–35 minutes** for malleable iron from crude pig iron.

Therefore neither a rotatable vessel drawing nor flame-based process judgement was intrinsically secret by 1860.

Source: Henry Bessemer, US Patent 16,082, 1856, esp. pp. 5–7 and drawings.

### Bessemer's Institution of Civil Engineers paper, May 1859; German circulation in 1859

A German version appeared in *Polytechnisches Journal*, vol. 153 (1859), p. 270, explicitly derived from Bessemer's Institution of Civil Engineers lecture of May 1859 and the July *London Journal of Arts*.

By this point the public description had been updated substantially. It states that:

- Bessemer had established steel works in **Sheffield** to develop the process in practical operation;
- suitable English raw material included hematite pig from **Workington**, with Cleator Moor, Weardale and Forest of Dean also named;
- the current converting vessels had the form of **glass retorts** and **rested in axes**;
- on rotation, about **twenty thin but powerful air jets** passed through the molten charge;
- the blast could bring the mass to very high temperature in roughly **10–12 minutes**;
- the published account presented a **gas meter / dial** as a means of regulating decarburisation.

Thus the main geometry of the current converter and the importance of low-phosphorus hematite pig were public before Wedding arrived.

### Public discussion of Cumberland hematite, July 1859

A July 1859 report of Bessemer's public presentation/discussion stated explicitly that the red hematite of **Cumberland** was suitable for his process. This matters because Wedding's 1860 notebook later says that the Sheffield works mostly used pig iron from Cumberland.

Conclusion: the bare association `Bessemer process ↔ Cumberland hematite` was already public knowledge by July 1860.

## 3. The 19–24 minute figure should not be collapsed into the public 10–12 minute figure

The 1859 public paper says that the blast produces the relevant high-temperature transformation in about **10–12 minutes**. Wedding writes that the **whole process** takes about **19–24 minutes**.

These are not necessarily contradictory measurements. They appear to refer to different operational intervals. The notebook's total-process figure is therefore useful as a possible **site calibration**, but it should not be presented as unpublished solely because no identical number has yet been found in the 1859 paper.

Earlier Bessemer patent literature gave still other durations (for example, roughly 30–35 minutes for the whole conversion to malleable iron), showing that duration depended on process stage, product target and plant development.

## 4. Public principle versus situated practical competence

The source distinction now looks like this:

### Already public before Wedding's visit

`PUBLICATION / PATENT`:

- air-through-molten-iron principle;
- rotating/axis vessel;
- tuyere architecture in general;
- flame colour/volume as a practical process indicator;
- Sheffield works as the practical development site;
- suitable hematite / Workington-Cumberland raw-material regime;
- rapid conversion on a minutes rather than hours scale.

### Wedding's 1860 notebook as situated re-inscription

`DEMONSTRATION / PRACTICAL_COMPETENCE / SITE_CALIBRATION`:

- what this Sheffield plant was actually using (`meist aus Cumberland`);
- how the plant sequence was laid out and operated in his visit context;
- his own converter/sectional sketching;
- current total-process timing (`cca 19–24 Minuten`);
- visible phase changes and flame/process staging as he chose to record them;
- juxtaposition with other Sheffield works visited in the same day.

The analytically important move is therefore **not** `secret Bessemer knowledge revealed to Wedding`. It is:

> **publicly describable process → situated inspection/demonstration → locally calibrated operational notebook record.**

## 5. A particularly useful epistemic tension: instrument versus skilled visual judgement

The 1859 published description foregrounds a gas meter/dial as a precise regulator of decarburisation. The older patent also says that colour and volume of the flame provide the workman with a practical guide.

Wedding's notebook visibly records flame/process stages and their timing. Once the exact sentences are controlled, this may allow a strong comparison between:

- **formal/public regulation** by measured air throughput;
- **shop-floor process judgement** by flame, gas, eruption and temporal sequence;
- **Wedding's travel-note choice** about which indicators were worth carrying away.

Do not promote this into a firm claim until the flame sentences on PDF 299 are transcribed conservatively, but preserve it as a priority question.

## 6. Consequence for the converter drawing

Because vessel drawings and axis/tuyere principles were already public in patents and technical literature, the presence of Wedding's sketch alone does not prove privileged access or direct copying from a private Bessemer drawing.

What makes the notebook sketch significant is its **documentary setting**:

- it sits under the named works heading;
- it is embedded in the 25 July Sheffield visit sequence;
- it is paired with local material-use and process-time notes;
- Wedding later stated explicitly that Bessemer personally enabled him to learn the process in Sheffield in 1860.

Safest formulation:

> The sketch is a contemporary site-associated re-inscription of the converter system; its exact visual source — direct observation, explanation by Bessemer/staff, or prior published diagram used during the visit — remains open.

About the project

A documentary research project for following microscope slides through objects, texts and circulation.

The project begins with surviving nineteenth-century microscope slides and follows the records created around them: making, selling, exchanging, exhibiting, teaching, collecting and later custody.

It also works in the other direction. Periodicals, newspapers, catalogues and institutional records identify movements and relationships that can sometimes be connected back to surviving material.

01

What this adds

Collection databases usually describe present custody. This project concentrates on the documentary chains that moved slides between preparers, dealers, microscopists, societies and institutions.

02

Method

Cases are worked in both directions, from surviving objects backwards and historical events forwards, until the records meet or fail to meet.

03

Contents

The public layer combines a frozen surviving-object catalogue with source registries, bounded circulation cases and downloadable crosswalks.

04

Limits

A shared taxon, maker, catalogue number or institutional location does not prove that two records describe the same physical slide. Open identities remain open.

Twelve slides from Naples

In June 1880 twelve preparations from the Zoological Station at Naples were shown before the Royal Microscopical Society in London. A price catalogue issued from Naples that August allows nine of them to be matched closely to numbered commercial preparations.

9 Jun 1880

The Royal Microscopical Society recorded twelve slides from the Zoological Station at Naples, sent through A. W. Waters and exhibited under microscopes.

Aug 1880

The Naples preparation department issued a price catalogue containing 423 numbered offerings. Fritz Meyer appears as director and organizer of the department; the catalogue does not establish that he personally prepared every offering.

Crosswalk

Nine of the twelve RMS descriptions can be matched exactly or strongly to catalogue numbers 42, 43, 67, 68, 71, 72, 86, 182 and 186.

Bounded

Three further identifications remain deliberately bounded: Ascetta bianca to 5|6, the starfish larva to 43–49, and Amphioxus lanceolatus to 231|232.

After 1880

Later British records show further Naples material in learned-society and exhibition contexts. Those events are kept distinct unless the sources support the same-object connection.

What the chain closes

A historical commercial catalogue can be connected to a named British physical-slide shipment and exhibition at item level.

What remains open

No surviving St Andrews or other museum slide is currently asserted to be one of the twelve objects shown in London in June 1880.

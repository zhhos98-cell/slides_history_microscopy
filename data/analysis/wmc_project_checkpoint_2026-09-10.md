## 3. Longitudinal first-order evidence now controlled

### 1861

Formal curricular differentiation is explicit. Cooke's `Physiological Botany` and Barwell's separate `Use of the Microscope` course run in the same term. Cooke's own sequence is seasonal and procedural: winter introduction -> vegetable physiology and morphology -> microscope -> spring fieldwork. This is stronger than a generic statement that WMC had an interest in microscopy.

### 1873 / 1890 / 1893 genealogy

A 1907 internal retrospective account states that the later Lubbock Field Club followed earlier Sunday rambles (1857–1862) and a club started in 1873 with Professor Flower as president, Litchfield vice-president, Ebenezer Cooke and Alfred Grugeon on committee, Fleming as curator, and Fotheringham as secretary/treasurer; Cooke succeeded Fotheringham in 1875. The same account states that Grugeon's botanical walks in 1890 generated enthusiasm and that the Lubbock Field Club was formed in March 1893.

Evidence state: `INSTITUTIONAL_RETROSPECTIVE_PRIMARY_TEXT`, not a substitute for 1873 minutes.

### 1898

Vol. V gives direct evidence for an institutional science ecology rather than an isolated instrument. Science lectures, Museum use, Botany, Physiology, microscope equipment, slides, lantern demonstration, and object circulation appear together. A. G. Tansley's lecture asks readers to imagine Amoeba in a drop of water on a slide under the microscope; Botany and Physiology are described as well equipped with microscopes; WMC Museum/Field Club material includes externally sent natural-history specimens requiring classification/preparation.

### 1903–1904

Vol. VIII describes students cutting plant sections and examining them under microscopes in botany teaching. It also records a proposal for a scientific club centred on photographic and microscopic work, experiments, displays, and current scientific discoveries. The same volume supplies retrospective links among M. C. Cooke, Alfred Grugeon, Charles Savage, early botany/geology teaching, field walks, and later Field Club activity.

### 1907–1908

Vol. X provides a particularly strong Field Club genealogy and records an internal dispute over whether the club had become insufficiently scientific because members covered too much ground for proper collecting. Suggested remedies included botanical versus athletic walks, renewed indoor meetings, a proposed College Scientific Society, practical biological work centred on the new Laboratory, and work on Museum collections. The College also received a complete set of lenses and a microscope and slides as gifts.

The first-order mechanism is therefore not `fieldwork and microscopy naturally belonged together`. The sources instead show recurrent organizational allocation and tension among walking, collecting, laboratory work, museum work, class technique, and display.

## 7. Current argument boundary

A defensible first-order formulation from the controlled corpus is:

> Across the WMC material now controlled, microscopy does not persist primarily as a stable course title.

# Working Men's College — Sunday excursions / field practice, 1856–1893 source control

Updated: 2026-09-09
Status: MULTI-LAYER SOURCE CONTROL__LONG-DURATION WALKING PRACTICE SECURE__NATURAL-HISTORY CLUB CONTINUITY PENDING
Scope: bounded WMC source bridge. This note controls evidence for Sunday excursions, walking parties, natural-history field practice and later field-club organization without converting repeated outdoor activity into an unbroken scientific institution.

## 1. 1856 — `The Sabbath Day` / Sunday-excursion controversy

### Exact source located, original text not yet ingested

A modern Maurice study gives the exact source as F. D. Maurice, *The Sabbath Day: An Address to the Members of the Working Men's College on Sunday Excursions* (London: Working Men's College, 1856), with Internet Archive identifier `sabbathdayaddres637maur`.

The same study states that the practical issue was the propriety of College members meeting on Sundays for excursions into the country; Maurice gave an address on that issue and later described it to Charles Kingsley as a `tract`.

Maurice's 1884 *Life*, in the table of contents for 1856 correspondence, independently identifies a letter `Maurice to Kingsley, on his pamphlet on the Sabbath Day`, p. 294.

A 1911 biography of Frederick Furnivall likewise calls the 1856 item `Maurice's` pamphlet and says it expressed Maurice's attitude toward the Sunday excursions, including a suggestion that walks be restricted to hours before or after Sunday service.

Evidence level: CONTROLLED RETROSPECTIVE / EXACT PRIMARY LOCATOR. The original title page and body text have not yet been ingested directly, so do not quote the tract or treat later summaries of its detailed argument as primary wording.

### Secondary claim about science classes — hold below primary level

An older Boston University dissertation describes the pamphlet as responding to Sunday excursions of `science classes` and says the objection was not to walking itself but to using the walks to study natural sciences.

Evidence level: SECONDARY / PENDING PRIMARY CHECK. This is potentially important but must not be promoted until the tract or contemporary correspondence explicitly supports `science classes` / `natural sciences`.

### Authorship catalogue anomaly

King's College London / AIM25's Furnivall archive catalogue lists *The Sabbath-Day: an Address to the Members of the Working Men's College* (1856) among Furnivall's publications. This conflicts with Maurice's 1884 *Life*, the 1911 Furnivall biography, and recent Maurice scholarship, all of which attribute the Sunday-excursion pamphlet/address to Maurice.

Current handling: preserve as a catalogue-level attribution anomaly. Do not silently correct the KCL record. Resolve from the original title page of Internet Archive item `sabbathdayaddres637maur`.

## 2. 1858 — Sunday walking as an operating and contested College practice

The same older WMC research cites John Ludlow correspondence from September 1858 protesting one Furnivall-led walk of forty-five miles because participants were late for work and one man reportedly stayed in bed the following day.

Evidence level: SECONDARY TRANSCRIPTION WITH MANUSCRIPT LOCATOR (Ludlow MSS, Cambridge University Library). Do not quote as primary until the letter is opened.

Historical use: the controversy was not merely theological in later recollection; the reported objection also concerned the physical and workday consequences of very long excursions. This remains a source target rather than body-secure event wording.

## 3. 1873 onward — WMC Sunday walking parties at Down, direct retrospective text

Henrietta Emma Litchfield, in *Emma Darwin: A Century of Family Letters* (1915), directly describes her husband Richard Buckley Litchfield's WMC walking parties. Darwin Online supplies a public corrected transcription of the printed text.

Litchfield states that:
- `six or seven times every summer` her husband organized a Sunday walking party of his singing class and members of the Working Men's College;
- parties travelled by rail to a place near London, walked several miles, then had luncheon and a tea picnic;
- singing, gathering flowers, games and tea occupied the day;
- after her marriage, Charles and Emma Darwin invited the party to Down several times;
- the first visit to Down was in summer 1873;
- attendance was often sixty or seventy;
- at Down the day included garden wandering and singing under the lime trees.

Evidence level: RETROSPECTIVE PRIMARY-TEXT WITNESS, published 1915 by a participant/organizer's wife and Darwin family member. Secure for what Litchfield remembered and published; not contemporaneous minutes.

Public text: Darwin Online, *Emma Darwin*, vol. 2, printed p. 213.

### Evidence consequence

WMC Sunday walking was a repeated seasonal social practice by 1873 and large enough to bring sixty or seventy participants to Down. The practice included collecting flowers, but Litchfield does **not** call these outings a Natural History Club, botany class, scientific excursion or specimen-collection programme.

Do not convert `gathering flowers` into formal botanical collecting without another source.

A Darwin reference companion independently records Working Men's College groups of roughly fifty or sixty visiting Down from 1873 onward; this is corroborative, not a substitute for Litchfield's more detailed first-person retrospective.

## 4. 1873 Natural History Social and Field Club — secondary exact lead

Peter Ayres's biography of Arthur Tansley states that WMC had a tradition of Sunday walks from its earliest days and that in 1873 a `Natural History Social and Field Club` was formed, making the walks more structured. Ayres says there were three or four walks every month, many botanical walks led by Alfred Grugeon, who collected plants and displayed them on return to the College.

Evidence level: CONTROLLED SECONDARY / EXACT WMC-SOURCE TARGET NEEDED. The claim is highly relevant but must not be merged with Litchfield's independently documented walking parties as though the two sources describe the same organizational body.

Questions for exact-source verification:
- formal club name and date of constitution;
- whether it was a College body, class adjunct, informal social club or later retrospective label;
- membership and officers;
- Grugeon's role and dates;
- whether collected plants entered Museum, classroom display, herbarium or temporary demonstration;
- relation, if any, to Litchfield/Furnivall Sunday walking parties.

## 5. 1893 — Lubbock Field Club

John Ramsbottom's 1917 notice of Alfred Grugeon states that Grugeon was president of the Working Men's College `Lubbock Field Club` from its foundation in 1893 until his death.

Evidence level: RETROSPECTIVE PRIMARY-TEXT WITNESS.

A late-1890s *Science-Gossip* notice independently and contemporaneously lists `Lubbock Field Club` in connection with Working Men's College, Great Ormond Street, and gives a recurring excursion/meeting schedule.

Evidence level: PRIMARY / SECURE for public existence, WMC address and routine operation in the late 1890s.

Marcella Sutcliffe cites WMC Archive `LMA/4535/E/02/06/001` for the Club's stated aims: collection/recording of natural-science facts and phenomena, periodical excursions, meetings, papers/questions, microscopic observations and exhibition of specimens.

Evidence level for the aims: CONTROLLED SECONDARY TRANSCRIPTION WITH EXACT ARCHIVE LOCATOR until the WMC/LMA file is read directly.

## 6. What the sequence does and does not establish

Secure at present:
- Sunday excursions were already sufficiently established/controversial at WMC in 1856 for a dedicated address/tract to be issued; exact detailed scientific content remains pending original inspection.
- WMC Sunday walking parties were a repeated and large-scale social practice by 1873; Litchfield directly remembers six or seven per summer and visits of sixty or seventy people to Down.
- a later WMC Lubbock Field Club existed by the late 1890s, with Grugeon retrospectively identified as president from its 1893 foundation.
- an exact WMC archive file is reported to define that later Club's programme in natural-science observation, excursions, microscopy and specimen exhibition.

Not yet secure:
- one unbroken institutional line from 1856 Sunday walks → 1873 Natural History Social and Field Club → 1893 Lubbock Field Club;
- that the 1856 excursions were specifically natural-history/science excursions, unless the original tract or contemporary correspondence says so;
- that the 1873 Down parties were the Natural History Social and Field Club;
- that flowers gathered on walks were scientific specimens;
- that Grugeon's later Lubbock practice preserved the same instruments, collections, rules or custody systems as earlier WMC activity.

## 7. Historical question now worth testing

The source problem is no longer simply `did WMC have field clubs?` It is whether repeated outdoor sociability at WMC was successively attached to different institutional and epistemic purposes:

- Sunday recreation and religious controversy in the 1850s;
- repeated College/singing-class walking parties by the early 1870s;
- a reported Natural History Social and Field Club with collecting/display practices in the 1870s;
- a formal Lubbock Field Club in the 1890s explicitly combining excursions, natural-science observation, microscopy and specimen exhibition.

Only exact rules, reports, minute books and periodicals can establish continuity versus reuse of the same social form.

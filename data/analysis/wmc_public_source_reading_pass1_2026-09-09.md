## 1. Working Men's College report, October 1866

Source identity:
- *The Working Men's College, founded 1854, 45 Great Ormond Street, Bloomsbury, W.C. Principal, Rev. F. D. Maurice, M.A. ...*
- London: The College, 1866, 8 pp.
- Internet Archive identifier: `workingmenscolle188work`.
- Public full scan: https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/7/75/The_Working_Men%27s_College%2C_founded_1854%2C_45_Great_Ormond_Street%2C_Bloomsbury%2C_W.C._Principal%2C_Rev._F.D._Maurice%2C_M.A._.._%28IA_workingmenscolle188work%29.pdf
- Open Library metadata notes a pencilled annotation at the head of the title: `This report was objected to by the Council and suppressed in its present form.` Treat the annotation as copy-level metadata until the manuscript hand/provenance is independently established.

### First-order institutional facts

The title-page Council list includes:
- `Mr. GRUGEON, Certificated Teacher of Botany (Science and Art Department of Privy Council)`.
- `N. S. MASKELYNE, Esq., M.A., Wadham Coll., Oxf.`
- John Ruskin, Lowes Dickinson, F. J. Furnivall, William Rossiter, Thomas Hughes, and others.

This establishes N. S. Maskelyne as a member of the WMC Council by the 1866 report and places a Science and Art Department-certified botany teacher inside the College's governing/teaching network. Do not infer Maskelyne's specific teaching activity from Council membership alone.

### Physical-science teaching was institutional, not incidental

The report contains a table `Showing the number of Class Entries in each subject for each year since the foundation of the College.` The physical-science columns are `Chemistry`, `Botany`, `Physiology`, `Geology`, and `Geography`; the average row gives 20, 16, 16, 17, and 24 class entries respectively. The table should be treated as class-entry counts, not unique student counts.

The examination regulations make physical sciences part of the College's credential structure. For Associateship, students could obtain certificates in subjects including `Botany`, `Physiology`, `Geology and Physical Geography`, `Mechanics`, `Hydrostatics`, and `Optics`. For Scholarship, the `Physical Science` field is explicitly defined as `Natural Philosophy; Botany; Physiology; Zoology; Geology; Chemistry. Any three of these subjects.`

The programme for the `Thirteenth Year, commencing October 22nd, 1866` lists three current physical-science classes:
- Friday 9–10: `Botany` — Mr. Grugeon.
- Friday 8–9: `Natural Philosophy` — Rev. A. Goalen, M.A.
- Friday 8–10: `Zoology` — T. Spencer Cobbold, M.D., F.R.S., F.L.S.

The same programme states that a `Conversazione` is held in January and July each year and strangers are admitted for one shilling.

### Financial / organisational facts

Maurice's signed report states that the College had worked for many years with small funds chiefly supplied by pupil payments and contributions from teachers and immediate friends. The 1866 classes account records student fees, adult school income and conversazione income; expenditure includes class teachers, library/printing/postage, house repairs/firing/lighting, rates/taxes, and salaries. This is useful for reconstructing the College as a low-fee teaching cooperative rather than a purely philanthropic lecture venue.

### Negative bounded searches

The searchable text of this eight-page report gives no hit for `Cooke` and no hit for `microscope`. These are document-level negatives only. They do not bear on whether Cooke taught at the College or whether microscopy occurred in other years, magazines, club records, inventories, or class programmes.

## 2. *Atlantic Monthly*, July 1861, `The London Working-Men's College`

Public full text: https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/11154/pg11154-images.html

The anonymous/unsigned article is a contemporary external witness by an author who states that he became a WMC member in autumn 1859 by paying half-a-crown. He gives the programme he knew in autumn 1859 and describes the College's social and publication infrastructure.

### First-order facts from the author's 1859 experience

The author states that:
- Ruskin taught landscape drawing on Thursday evenings, with four other drawing teachers working on other evenings.
- `There was a class in Geology the winter I knew the College,—there had been classes in Botany and Chemistry.`
- The College also had a class on `the Structure and Functions of the Human Body`, practice classes, preparatory classes, and an adult school.
- The office/reading-room held College information; the College published a monthly `Working-Men's College Magazine` devoted to its interests.
- The author subscribed to the yearly volume and `sat down to read up, from the Magazine, as to the working of the College.`

This is important for source criticism: the 1859–61 Magazine was not merely a retrospective house journal but a contemporaneous operational information medium used by a new member to learn how the College worked.

### Negative bounded search

No `Cooke` hit occurs in this Atlantic article. `Microscope` occurs elsewhere in the July 1861 Atlantic volume in an unrelated article, not in the WMC piece. Do not treat it as WMC microscopy evidence.

## 3. Levi and Tucker 2020 — controlled secondary bridge to Ebenezer Cooke

Donata Levi and Paul Tucker, `‘J after J. Ruskin’: line in the art teaching of John Ruskin and Ebenezer Cooke`, *Journal of Art Historiography* 22 (June 2020).
Public PDF: https://arthistoriography.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/05/levi-and-tucker.pdf

This source was opened and visually checked. It states that Ebenezer Cooke (1837–1913), son of a Norfolk village schoolmaster and apprenticed to a lithographer, `began attending Ruskin's drawing classes at the Working Men's College in 1855`; from 1865 he was responsible, under Ruskin's supervision, for drawing classes at the Working Women's College. The article cites Cooke's later `The Early Art Classes (part I)`, *Working Men's College Journal* 19 (1905–1906), p. 88.

Use this as a controlled secondary bridge. The 1905–06 WMCJ article remains an exact primary-source target and should be read before quoting Cooke's retrospective wording from the journal.

## 5. Immediate analytical consequence

The WMC natural-history/science line no longer rests on a single Cooke donation lead. By 1866 the College's own report shows a persistent physical-science curriculum, formal examination routes in botany/physiology/geology/optics and a scholarship category spanning natural philosophy, botany, physiology, zoology, geology and chemistry. Its current 1866 teaching programme includes botany, natural philosophy and zoology, while the 1861 Atlantic witness independently reports geology in 1859 and earlier botany/chemistry classes. The College therefore supplied an established institutional ecology in which a Cooke natural-history collection, if identity and dating are confirmed, would have entered an existing teaching and sociability system rather than creating natural history ex nihilo.

N. S. Maskelyne's appearance on the 1866 Council adds a separate named bridge into the wider scientific-instrument/mineralogical network. Keep this as a first-order institutional relation only until his WMC actions, attendance, lectures, correspondence or committee work are located.

# WMC governance / scientific association / resource allocation — 1872–1875

Scope: bounded analytical pass on the relation between the 1872–74 constitutional crisis/reorganisation of the Working Men's College and the 1873 Natural History Society and Field Club. The main source is the College's 1904 jubilee history, especially J. A. Forster's chapter `A Transition Period: R. B. Litchfield` and R. H. Marks's `The College Clubs`. The 1904 book reproduces dated resolutions/articles and participant memories but remains retrospective for the 1870s. The purpose here is to identify co-location, shared actors and shared institutional resources without asserting that governance reform directly caused the scientific society.

## 1. Student institutional labour preceded formal governance representation

Forster's 1904 reconstruction describes substantial student work before the 1872 crisis:

- students formed a Finance Committee and attended to College property and expenditure;
- students taught adult-school and preparatory classes;
- beginning in 1867, J. A. Forster and other students arranged, classified and catalogued a chaotic library of about 5,000 books, producing labels, subject arrangement and a manuscript catalogue;
- `good work` was also being done by students in the Museum.

Forster then explicitly links the experience of these men doing College work to their awareness of weak organisation in the governing system and to proposals/consultative meetings for reform.

This is important context for later scientific clubs. WMC students were already doing classificatory, custodial, financial and teaching labour before they received a formal place in the reconstructed Council.

## 2. 1872–73: students become formal participants in constitutional reconstruction

On 24 May 1872 the Council resolved that students of three years' standing who had attended classes for four terms should elect three representatives to work with the Executive Committee on possible changes in government and administration. T. Catterns, J. A. Forster and Frank Murray were elected; the later committee also included R. B. Litchfield and George Tansley.

The committee examined other colleges' constitutions, including Girton. According to Forster, the student members strongly urged:

- thorough reconstitution of the Council as governing body;
- a separate body of trustees for property and guardianship of the College's objects.

The College history identifies the representative students, Litchfield and Ludlow among the most active participants in constitution-making.

## 3. Litchfield is the shared actor linking governance, classes and the 1873 scientific association

On 13 February 1873 R. B. Litchfield, then chairing the Committee of Inquiry, was appointed Vice-Principal with what the 1904 history calls `dictatorial powers` to conduct College administration with chosen assistants. By 2 April resolutions embodying a complete reorganisation scheme had been adopted subject to confirmation; on 1 July 1873, after student Council members and delegates spoke, Thomas Hughes declared himself convinced by their arguments and the resolutions were carried.

During this same April–July 1873 window, the College arrangements announced the formation of the Natural History Society and Field Club. The 1904 club history names **R. B. Litchfield as Vice-President** of that Society, under W. H. Flower as President.

Thus Litchfield simultaneously appears as:

- principal operational actor in College administrative/class reorganisation;
- constitutional-reform actor;
- Vice-President of the new Natural History Society and Field Club.

This does not prove that Litchfield created the Society as part of his reform programme. It does remove the weaker picture of two institutionally unrelated events happening by coincidence.

## 4. The new Council formally institutionalised student and teacher participation

Resolution VII of 1 July 1873, later made an Article of Association, reorganised the Council into three groups. Especially relevant here:

1. qualifying teachers of College classes entered/remained on Council under stated conditions;
2. **twelve students** were to be elected by Council from Student Teachers, Associates, certificated students or other students, for three-year rotating terms;
3. additional members could be chosen from past teachers, past/present students or others whose assistance was useful.

While this reconstitution was being implemented, Litchfield continued to conduct College administration and classes. His secretary was a Fellow; his assistant secretary, E. Hicks, was a certificated student specifically remembered for work in preparatory teaching, Library and Museum; the Executive included Forster, Murray and George Tansley.

## 5. Scientific teaching was also re-staffed during the same administrative reconstruction

The 1904 history says Litchfield's first concern while administering the College/classes was to improve the teaching staff. It then notes that the October 1873 programme includes:

- S. D. Darbishire — Physiology;
- B. T. Lowne — Botany;
- J. L. Lobley — Geology;

and that Thomas Dunman subsequently taught in the Physical Science Division, where the science classes became popular.

This matters because the Natural History Society / Field Club should not be isolated from the formal-class reconstruction. In 1873 the College was simultaneously:

- redesigning its governing body;
- formalising student/teacher representation;
- repairing/recruiting the teaching staff;
- operating formal science classes;
- forming a Society for Museum study and open-air natural-history work.

Evidence ceiling: the source does not state that the science appointments were made because of the Natural History Society, nor that the Society was part of a single explicit science-policy plan.

## 6. 1874 Corporation separated property/objects from government/classes

The old Working Men's College Company was resolved into voluntary liquidation on 17 March 1874; a new Working Men's College Corporation was incorporated on 4 November 1874 under the Companies Act 1862.

The 1904 history reproduces the institutional division written into the new structure:

- the Corporation was a permanent trustee body for property/capital and guardian of the College's `Objects`;
- it had no part in ordinary government/administration or conduct of classes;
- government and current expenditure were vested in the Council, subject to limited Corporation powers over fees, teaching hours and a Domus/Fabric Fund.

The first Corporation included Thomas Hughes, R. B. Litchfield, Furnivall, George Tansley and others already embedded in the College's teaching/governance history.

This division is highly relevant to the project's material-history vocabulary: `property`, `Objects`, `classes`, current administration and club use were not necessarily controlled by one corporate organ.

Do not confuse capital-P/property `Objects` in the constitutional sense (institutional purposes) with Museum specimens. The phrase `guardian of the Objects` here refers to the College's constitutional objects, not object collections.

## 7. Room scarcity was structurally real, not a trivial anecdote

The Natural History Society / Field Club is remembered as ending because the Tuesday room it used was required for a class and no other room was available.

The wider transition chapter supplies the financial/material background:

- the Great Ormond Street freehold houses were mortgaged;
- money had been borrowed by debentures to complete the new rear class-rooms;
- the College had recurring annual deficits;
- expenditure had already been cut below what was considered efficient;
- buildings needed repairs for which funds were lacking;
- principal liabilities were about £2,175;
- substantial donations and the Maurice Memorial/Fabric arrangement in 1874–75 were used to reduce debt and support building maintenance.

This does not prove that debt mechanically caused the Tuesday-room decision. It does show that `no other room was available` belongs to a documented regime of spatial and financial scarcity rather than a neutral abundance of unused accommodation.

## 8. Revised mechanism: organisational form depends on resource allocation

The strongest first-order mechanism now available is:

`student/teacher institutional labour`
→ `pressure for governance reform`
→ `formal student/teacher representation`
+ `class administration/staff reconstruction`
+ `Natural History Society formation`
+ `shared actor: Litchfield`
→ `competition for finite rooms/time`
→ `scientific associational form ends when class receives the room`.

This should **not** be rewritten as:

- `the governance reform created the Natural History Society`;
- `student representation caused WMC science to expand`;
- `the Society was an administrative arm of Litchfield's reform`;
- `financial debt caused the Society to disappear`.

Those causal links remain open.

What is now safe is stronger and more concrete than chronological juxtaposition:

> The Natural History Society and Field Club emerged during the same constitutional reconstruction that formalised student and teacher participation in College government, while the same Vice-Principal who was directing administration and classes served as the Society's Vice-President. Its eventual disappearance was remembered in explicitly allocative terms: a class required its Tuesday room and no substitute room was available. Scientific association at WMC therefore depended not only on scientific interest but on the distribution of institutional offices, people, rooms and timetable priority.

## 9. Relation to the `reallocation` argument

This evidence changes the status of `reallocation` from a historian's longitudinal description to a partially actor-visible institutional mechanism.

At WMC:

- people can be reassigned among student, teacher, Council member, club officer and administrator roles;
- rooms can be reassigned between club meetings and formal classes;
- material work can be assigned among Library, Museum, class and club;
- property/capital and ordinary class administration can be assigned to different corporate organs;
- science teaching and scientific association can be rebuilt simultaneously without being constitutionally identical.

The changing placement of microscopy/natural history should therefore be read through **allocation rules and resource claims**, not only through changing course names.

# *Science-Gossip* `Simple Objects` — annual work calendar, 1865–1866

Updated: 2026-09-10
Status: PRIMARY_SEQUENCE__TEMPORAL_RELATIONS_MAPPED
Scope: page-level reconstruction of how the early `Simple Objects` series relates publication month to field availability, preparation, storage, later collecting and specimen requests. This note does not claim that every installment was seasonally selected or that the series followed one uniform temporal programme.

## 1. Why read the series as a temporal sequence

The first volume index lists `Simple Objects` at pp. 35, 58, 109, 158, 176, 201, 230 and 279. The printed articles themselves additionally number `Meadow-sweet Brand` as no. VIII at p. 253/255 in the November issue, so the index appears to omit one installment. Preserve this as an indexing irregularity; do not infer editorial intention from the omission.

Across the numbered series, publication date does not bear one fixed relation to the object's biological season. Some articles direct readers to an object available immediately; some open a time-limited specimen request; some extend field material into months of indoor observation; and one explicitly tells an October reader where to collect the object the following February–March.

The series therefore provides a bounded way to ask how monthly print allocated different kinds of microscopical work across the year.

## 2. February 1865 — no. I, `Star-spored Fungus (Asterosporium Hoffmanni)`, p. 35

The first installment describes a fungus common on dead twigs of beech, lime and other trees. The article becomes part of an editorial specimen-fulfillment sequence: the March correspondence page reports that available specimens had been distributed to the last fragment while nearly fifty applicants remained unsupplied; stamped envelopes were retained until new material could be obtained; the April issue later reports replenishment and supply.

Primary page:
https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Page:Hardwicke%27s_Science-Gossip_-_Volume_1.pdf/51

Temporal operation: the February article creates requests whose completion exceeds one issue. Biological stock and editorial backlog remain open into March and April.

## 3. March 1865 — no. II, `Jelly Animalcule (Ophrydium versatile)`, p. 58

The article tells readers to notice green jelly-like masses in bright clear streams and examine portions microscopically. It also states that Ophrydium can be kept in water for several months in an open vessel; detached animalcules then establish new colonies on the bottle.

Public text:
https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/6/6c/Hardwicke%27s_Science-Gossip_-_Volume_1.pdf

Temporal operation: a field-collected living object is made available for prolonged indoor observation. Publication does not merely point to a momentary collecting window; the article gives the reader a way to extend the object's observable life beyond collection.

## 4. May 1865 — no. III, `Fringed Scale-moss (Ptilidium ciliare)`, p. 109

The article describes the scale-moss as abundant in leafy places and gives microscopical preparation/examination instructions, including a method attributed to Dr Carrington.

Public text:
https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/6/6c/Hardwicke%27s_Science-Gossip_-_Volume_1.pdf

Temporal operation: no strong seasonal instruction is presently controlled. This installment is an important counterexample to any claim that `Simple Objects` was simply a monthly phenological calendar. Its operation is accessibility plus preparation, not an explicit collecting date.

## 5. July 1865 — no. IV, `The Four-horned Cyclops (Cyclops quadricornis)`, p. 158

The article requires only a wide-mouthed bottle, walking stick and india-rubber band, then tells the reader that the first dip into a neighbouring pond or roadside ditch `at this season of the year` will supply Daphnia, Cyprides and Cyclops in abundance, enough for weeks of investigation.

Public text:
https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Page:Hardwicke%27s_Science-Gossip_-_Volume_1.pdf/174

Temporal operation: issue date and field availability are directly aligned. The July article gives an immediately actionable field instruction and turns present abundance into weeks of later microscopical work.

## 6. August 1865 — no. V, `The Bramble-leaf Brand`, p. 176

Cooke describes a seasonal transformation on common bramble leaves: yellow rust during summer, darker bodies appearing as autumn approaches, and eventually dark spore clusters replacing much of the yellow material. He then gives a glass-slide preparation using alcohol, nitric acid, thin glass and heat, with glycerine or balsam for mounting.

A footnote says that a stamped envelope sent to the Editor `during the current month` will secure a specimen for examination. The September correspondence page subsequently lists more than forty recipients under `Bramble-Brand forwarded to:`.

Primary text:
https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/a/a0/Hardwicke%27s_science-gossip._Volume_1%2C_1865._%28IA_s457id13378710%29.pdf

Temporal operation: several clocks are synchronized: summer-to-autumn morphological change, August publication, a one-month request window, postal return, preparation on a glass slide, and a September fulfillment record.

## 7. September 1865 — no. VI, `Cosmarium botrytis`, p. 201

The article says the organism can be found in almost every pond and presents it as an interesting microscopical object. The presently controlled text does not make a strong seasonal claim.

Primary text:
https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/6/6c/Hardwicke%27s_Science-Gossip_-_Volume_1.pdf

Temporal operation: another counterexample to a rigid seasonal calendar. The series alternates between objects anchored tightly to the current season and objects framed as broadly accessible.

## 8. October 1865 — no. VII, `Dermestes and Pencil-tail`, p. 230

The article opens by stating that the two objects can be obtained beneath the bark of old elm trees during the last few days of February and first fortnight of March. This instruction appears in the 1 October issue, several months before the named collecting window.

Primary text:
https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/6/6c/Hardwicke%27s_Science-Gossip_-_Volume_1.pdf

Temporal operation: this is anticipatory rather than present-tense field guidance. The periodical can tell the reader what to look for in a future season, allowing winter reading, equipment/cabinet planning or memory to precede collection. The article separates publication time from collection time deliberately rather than through editorial delay.

## 9. November 1865 — no. VIII, `Meadow-sweet Brand (Triphragmium ulmariae)`, p. 253/255

The article refers back to the previously described bramble brand as a fungus commonly found `in the autumn`, then directs attention to a brand on meadow-sweet leaves in wet situations. It gives a preparation method: remove a pustule with a sharp penknife, transfer it to a glass slide in water or spirit, cover with thin glass, separate the spores by slight pressure/motion, and mount permanent specimens in balsam/chloroform. A footnote says microscopists desiring specimens may send stamped envelopes to W. M. B. at the journal office.

Primary text:
https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/6/6c/Hardwicke%27s_Science-Gossip_-_Volume_1.pdf

Temporal operation: autumnal field material, slide preparation and postal specimen request again occupy the same issue, though the footnote does not use the Bramble article's exact `during the current month` wording.

## 10. December 1865 — no. IX, `Pandorina morum`, p. 279

The volume index and printed sequence identify `Pandorina morum` as the ninth `Simple Objects` installment. Current public extraction controls the title and microscopical description, but no consequential seasonal instruction has yet been found.

Evidence level: PRIMARY TITLE/SEQUENCE SECURE; temporal operation presently limited.

## 11. January 1866 — no. X, `The Scale of the Perch`, p. 13

The second volume continues the numbered series. The article treats common freshwater fish scales as readily available microscopical objects and explains dry versus balsam mounting, especially for polarized-light examination.

Primary text:
https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/9/91/Hardwicke%27s_science-gossip._Volume_2%2C_1866._%28IA_s457id13663170%29.pdf

Temporal operation: winter microscopy need not depend on a currently active living field population. A common retained material can supply cabinet/preparation work.

## 12. March 1866 — no. XI, `Sphaeria herbarum`, p. 58

The article explicitly begins `At this season of the year, and for two or three months to come`: dead herbaceous stems and small twigs carry minute black fungal bodies useful as microscopic objects. It also distinguishes present state from later-season state, noting that the epidermis may still cover the body now but the cuticle will be shed later.

Primary text:
https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/9/91/Hardwicke%27s_science-gossip._Volume_2%2C_1866._%28IA_s457id13663170%29.pdf

Temporal operation: publication month, a multi-month field window and morphological change are explicitly joined inside the article. The object is not described as timeless; the microscopist is told what phase to expect now and what will happen later in the season.

## 13. Comparative control — Stainton's *Entomologist's Weekly Intelligencer*

The relation between publication rhythm and natural-history work is not unique to *Science-Gossip*.

Oxford's Constructing Scientific Communities project notes that the first volume of Henry Tibbats Stainton's *Entomologist's Weekly Intelligencer* stopped in September 1856 because most entomologists ceased collecting during winter, then resumed the following April; a temporary *Substitute* occupied the winter interval. The same account notes that in later years specimen exchange and techniques for finding hibernating insects supplied enough winter material to keep the weekly periodical running.

Public account:
https://conscicom.web.ox.ac.uk/article/the-substitute-1856-57

The *Intelligencer* itself gives the temporal division of labour more explicitly. On 6 February 1859 it calls this a slack season and instructs readers to prepare diaries, setting boards, cages, store boxes and nets in advance of March collecting: `what you want in March, get ready in February`. On 1 October 1859 it says the height of collecting season leaves too little time to digest observations; autumn and winter are the proper seasons to bring the spring/summer miscellanea into systematic form while they remain fresh in memory.

Public primary scans are indexed through Wikimedia/BHL.

Comparative consequence: a natural-history periodical may not merely reflect seasonal abundance. It can distribute different scientific actions across the year: field capture, equipment preparation, record keeping, retrospective ordering, exchange, indoor observation and cabinet work.

## 14. Current historical inference

The strongest formulation supported by the sequence is not `the periodical followed nature's seasons`.

In these pages, monthly and weekly print helped readers coordinate several unequal times:

- immediate biological availability (`at this season`);
- gradual morphological change (summer rust to autumn brand; present epidermis to later exposed fungal body);
- short editorial request windows (`during the current month`);
- delayed fulfillment across later issues;
- preparation and preservation that extended observation beyond collection;
- anticipatory instruction for a future collecting season;
- winter equipment, diary, classification and cabinet work.

The periodical could therefore redistribute natural-historical labour across the annual cycle. It sometimes synchronized issue time with object time and sometimes deliberately separated them.

Do not compress this into a universal `seasonal epistemology` or claim that the entire `Simple Objects` series was planned as a phenological calendar. Nos. III and VI, among others, currently lack strong seasonal language and prevent that stronger conclusion.

## 15. Relevance to microscope-slide history

Slide preparation changes the temporal status of an object. A seasonal fungus available briefly on a leaf can be scraped onto glass, treated, covered and mounted; an aquatic organism can be kept alive for months; a future-season insect can be anticipated in October and later turned into a cabinet object. The periodical sits before and around these transformations by telling the reader when to collect, how to preserve, when to request material and what work can be deferred.

This offers a concrete temporal complement to the project's address argument: labels/cabinets/registers stabilize where an object is; periodical instructions and issue dates can help determine when the object becomes collectable, requestable, preparable or revisitable.

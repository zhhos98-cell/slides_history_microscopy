OBJECTS OF THE COLLEGE.,The Students are, for the most part, working men, and the Teachers are, in general, members of the Universities and of the different professions, or those who have themselves been Students in the College. Its purpose is to unite these classes together by associating them in the common work of teaching and learning. It provides instruction at the smallest possible cost (the teaching being for the most part unpaid) in the subjects with which it most concerns English citizens to be acquainted, and thus tries to place a liberal education within the reach of &lt;span class=&quot;hitHighlite&quot;&gt;working&lt;/span&gt; &lt;span class=&quot;hitHighlite&quot;&gt;men&lt;/span&gt;.

( W ),SEVENTEENTH REPORT OF THE &lt;span class=&quot;hitHighlite&quot;&gt;WORKING&lt;/span&gt; &lt;span class=&quot;hitHighlite&quot;&gt;MEN&rsquo;S&lt;/span&gt; &lt;span class=&quot;hitHighlite&quot;&gt;COLLEGE&lt;/span&gt;.,-- The Executive Committee beg to present to the Council their Report for the year ending 31st December, 1893. In accordance with the decision arrived at by the Council in November, 1892, the Report now forms part of a Calendar containing the statistics of the year&rsquo;s work in the College, and supplying information for the guidance of present and future students. The numbers compare favourably in most respects with those of the preceding year. The total of class entries in the year 1892-3 was 2507, and of individual students 1177, as against 2370 and X013 respectively in 1891-2. In the last October term there were 1094 class entries and 796 individual students, the figures for the corresponding term in 1892 being 1030 and 789. In the tables at pp. 24-29 in the Appendix hereto, the entries are classified in various ways, and materials for a detailed comparison with previous years will be found. Three points may be observed which the Committee regard with satisfaction: (i.) The proportion of class entries to individual students has steadily increased, (ii.) The relative number of students already on the books of the College who again join classes at the beginning of the terms, though rather irregular, tends also to increase, and the average duration of a student&rsquo;s career, which a few years ago was little more than two terms, now exceeds three, (iii.) The falling off of class entries in January as compared with October, and in April as compared with January, is not so marked as it used to be. These signs indicate that the College student is becoming less the casual visitor, and more the habitual attendant disposed to pursue his education in confoimity with some settled plan. It may be noted that during the October term we had as many as 64 teachers, who were engaged in teaching 83 classes. The Plan of Study,* which was first published in the Calendar in February, 1893, has now come into operation, and has done much to systematize both the courses and the examinations. It will require periodical revision, but the changes that appear necessary this year are few, and of no great importance. In almost all the prescribed courses examinations have been held at various dates, and 170 certificates have been awarded, of which a creditable proportion, viz., 54, are maiked &ldquo; Excellent.&rdquo;,C,See post, p. 39.
There are now 9 classes in the College for Men and Women, and 27 classes in this College, which, under the Charity Commissioners&rsquo; Scheme of May 9th, 1891, are open to the students of either College. The Committee wish to express their sense of the kindness shown by the first-named College in affording us the use of their rooms at the end of last summer, when this house was in the hands of the builders. The Council of the College for Working Women in Fitzroy Street, in their last Report, mention that they have appointed several paid teachers from members of the staff of this College, and make the comment, which the Committee gladly reciprocate, that &ldquo; thus the two institutions, each of which owes its origin to the late F. D. Maurice, and each of which has been animated by his spirit and guided by his aspirations, will, it is hoped, be drawn more closely together.&rdquo; In relation to finance, which in former years used to contribute to our Reports so many an unsatisfactory paragraph, the Committee have the pleasure of recording that the Central Governing Body of the City Parochial Foundation has acceded to a request made last February for a further subsidy, and has granted ,&pound;100 per annum (beginning from Midsummer, 1893) to this College, in addition to its share of the funds allotted by the &iexcl;Scheme of May 9th, 1891. It will be seen from the annexed accounts that a sum of about ^212 has been taken from our invested capital. This sum was laid out in repairs and improvements, especially of the sanitary arrange&not; ments, which were insufficient and much behind the demands of modern experience, but which have now been thoroughly re-constructed. But whilst the improved condition of the house may be a matter for congratulation, it should be remembered that the accommodation which it offers, both for educational and for social purposes, is in&not; adequate for our present members, and we should keep alive the hope that the College may some day be enabled to take into its use the house that belongs to it next door. The Royal Commissioners engaged in considering the question of the establishment of a Teaching University in London have con&not; tinued their sittings throughout the past year, and have accumulated a great store of evidence and of opinions bearing on the subject, but their final conclusions have not yet been published. It seems reasonable to hope, however, that their Report may lead to much important action in the direction of organizing Evening Education of a University character. A Teaching University for London should include within its scope arrangements by which efficient teaching of that character may be assured to those already engaged in the work of their lives, and who, consequently have only their evenings in which to continue and extend their education. It is hoped, also, that provision will be made for enabling such students, if they reach the required standard, to obtain University degrees. This College claims to be not merely engaged in the work of pre-
paration, but to be actually giving University education to students within its own walls. Those engaged in carrying on the College are therefore naturally anxious that its students should share in the advantages which a Teaching University may be expected to bestow upon London, The following is a brief account of the growth of the Educational Policy of the College :&mdash; For about 12 years after the death of our founder, Frederick Denison Maurice, the work was continued on much the same lines as in his lifetime, but the progress made was not commensurate with the educational needs of the times. It was therefore in 1885 deemed necessary to reorganize the studies by forming three main divisions, and to lay down a Plan of Study for the guidance of the students in each. These efforts were most successful, particularly in the Preparatory and Lower Divisions. Better methods followed upon better organization, and better work upon better methods ; the students remained longer in the College and the results of the examinations showed that a higher standard had been reached. But the numbers were not high, until in 1888 our fees were considerably lowered, and our advertisements were considerably increased. An immediate and large accession to the number of students was the result, and in no year since have the numbers fallen below those of 1888. From the very beginning some men came to the College whose continuous interest had been already awakened in some subject or group of kindred subjects. To provide efficient teaching in the classes, to equip those classes adequately, and to test thoroughly the knowledge and training acquired in them, was a sufficient fulfilment of the responsibility of the College towards these. On the other hand, there was also a body of students, increasing in number with the growth of the College, who, while feeling the want of education, had as yet no special intellectual interest in any branch of study. It was important, therefore, to try to enable these men also to discover in what subjects their interest could be aroused and their faculties best employed, and to devise some general educational policy which would meet their wants as well as the wants of those who knew better their own intellectual requirements. The policy now adopted may be outlined as follows :&mdash; i. To give in the Lower Division a general English education to those men who, when joining, know too little to enable them to pursue with advantage any special line, or who have as yet no interest in any definite direction. To pass the men who have proved themselves competent in the subjects of the Lower Division into the Higher, there to pursue some special subject or subjects, or if they are still not prepared to specialize, to pursue a course marked out for them in that Division which will, by giving them a general view of the elements of various subjects, enable them to choose those for which their minds have a special bent. At present the course most suitable for this purpose appears to be one leading up to the Matriculation
Examination of the University of London as it now stands. The number of subjects to be followed at the same time, and the sequence in which they are taken, will vary of course, with the time the individual student has at his disposal, and the circumstances in which he is placed. Such students, if successful at the College examination, are to be encouraged to Matriculate in London, and then, if still desirous to proceed on more general lines, rather than to specialize, to pass upwards, as far as possible, towards a Bachelor&rsquo;s degree. 2. To maintain the efficiency of all teaching, whether volunteer or paid, and to adequately equip all classes fully up to the limits of our space and means, so that men desiring to specialize may find ample opportunities of working successfully, provided their interest is keen and continuous and their industry persistent. It was with the object of giving effect to this policy that in 1892 the Plan of Study originally laid down in 1885, was re-modelled, as mentioned in the last Report. The methods proposed by the Committee and approved by the Council will be found in the Calendar, pp. 39-54. The Committee recognise the advantage of keeping touch with other educational bodies, and are glad to say that the College is represented on the Council of the London Society for the Extension of University Teaching, on the Council of the National Home Reading Union, and on the Educational Committee of theBirkbeck. An application has been made for some share in the funds that are to be devoted to the promotion of technical education by the Technical Education Board recently appointed under the authority of the London County Council, but no definite answer has yet been received. The Lectures given on Saturday nights, which are arranged in conjunction with the College for Men and Women, have been well attended, and have fully preserved their high standard of excellence. The Joint Committee are much indebted for the kindness with which their requests have been responded to by many lecturers of note. Passing from the purely educational side to other features of the College, the Committee take the opportunity of acknowledging the zeal and perseverance of the Advertising Sub-Committee, to whose efforts the maintenance of our numbers is to a great extent due. The Old Students&rsquo; Club continues to be a rallying point for many who have ceased to attend on ordinary occasions, but wish to have still some link with the College, and to maintain the friendships formed under its roof. Its winter meetings are numerously attended, and the reports of its proceedings are read with great interest in the College Journal. The supper took place on the 16th December, and was attended by more than 140 past and present members of the College, as well as by many guests. The Club life of the College does not abate in energy, and exhibits itself in various forms.
Shakspere readings, which had almost died out, suddenly revived at the beginning of 1893, and the Shakspere Society now holds meetings on alternate Sundays in the winter months, which are spoken of as being well and enthusiastically attended. The Lubbock Literary and Social Debating Society has been lately formed for the purpose of reading and discussing papers treating of scientific, social and political subjects. The Society h is arranged a series of meetings in January, February and March, at which papers will be read or lectures delivered. The Economic Club meets every month for the discussion of questions belonging to or connected with the Science of Economics. Papers have been read on &ldquo;The Taxation of Ground Rents,&rdquo; &ldquo;Remedies for the Unemployed,&rdquo; &ldquo;The Economics of Coal,&rdquo; &ldquo;The Latest Theory of Interest,&rdquo; &ldquo;The Industrial Revolution in England,&rdquo; &ldquo; Ralph Waldo Emerson,&rdquo; &ldquo; Carlyle and Economics,&rdquo; and &ldquo; The Limits of Collectivism in Production.&rdquo; The Club is collect&not; ing a library of the literature of Economics. The Chess and Draughts Club maintains its strength and is continually engaged in matches with other clubs. The Gymnastic Society meets from 8 to 10 every Saturday even&not; ing at St. George&rsquo;s Gymnasium, Little Russell Street, and has about 38 members on its list. The Maurice Cricket Club well maintained the credit of the College during the past summer. The present members of the Common Room Committee are :&mdash; Messrs. Bishop, Cole, Filbey, Lynes, Marks, Pocock and Weatherell. It would not be right to conclude without some recognition of the care and skill devoted to the production of the &ldquo; W. M. C. Journal,&rdquo; which serves many useful purposes and has now acquired for itself a well established and, it may be hoped, permanent position. APPENDIX TO REPORT.,SHORT REPORTS FROM DIRECTORS OF STUDIES AND TEACHERS.,PREPARATORY DIVISION. The Director (Mr. Tansley), reports that the numbers for the October Term are 124, as against 125 for the corresponding term of 1892. At the December Examination 26 men attended (against 30 last year). Of these 10 passed into the Lower Division, one taking a scholarship, which enables him to attend two classes in that Division free of all fees. Also seven passed from the Second Section of the Preparatory into the first. These results are due to the careful attention of the teachers to the work of the Division. Mr. Cole, the assistant Director, is unremitting in his efforts to help the men in every way, and all students should avail themselves of his Practice Class, which gives valuable help in clearing away any difficulties in the work given out by the Class Teachers. Mr, Tansley also says, &ldquo; I would urge all students in this (or any other) Division to make a point of doing a fair amount of Home Work every week, for no one can expect to work effectively at his subjects unless he does this.&rdquo; LOWER DIVISION. Mr, L. Jacob (Director), reports The teachers of the various classes in this Division concur in praising the industry of most of the students and the intelligence with which they master their work. A very efficient system of home work papers has been during the last year extended to all the classes in this Division. The papers on each evening&rsquo;s work are posted to the teacher and corrected by him before the next class evening. This entails a considerable addition to the teacher s work, one however which all very cheerfully undertake. About two-thirds of the students send&#39;in their papers regularly; and these all make very satisfactory progress. In Mr, Rivers&rsquo; class in History and Geography, three passed the final examination in July. Of the Grammar classes Mr. Levinsohn&rsquo;s sat for the first stage examination in July when five passed, and mine in April when 20 passed. Of mine also 19 men took the final at Christmas and 14 passed. Of the Arith&not; metic, Mr. Tansley&rsquo;s class began in October, 1892. In June, 1893, it con&not; cluded its first stage, when there were 20 students examined. Of these seven took certificates, three being &ldquo;excellent.&rdquo; Of those who failed 10 entered again for the first stage in December, nine obtaining certificates, three being &lsquo; &lsquo; excellent. &rdquo; This makes a total of 16 first stage certificates. Mr. Tansley says, &ldquo; I have now 22 in the class working at the higher parts of the subject. All are steady and per&not; severing students. I get, on the average, 17 weekly papers from them, and I con&not; sider the success gained by the class up to the present due to the fact that the members of it work hard at their subject out of class as well as in it.&rdquo; Mr. Higgs&rsquo; class was examined in December for the first stage. Twelve attended the exami&not; nation, eleven obtaining certificates, seven of which will be marked &ldquo;excellent.&rdquo; A most satisfactory result. Mr. Brett&rsquo;s class began in October last with about 60. He reports that he is well satisfied with his class, and that 26 attended the usual class examination held at the end of the term.
HIGHER DIVISION. Mr. A. B. Shaw, Director of the First Section, reports &ldquo; Of the subjects included in this section, Latin has as usual attracted by far the most students. There are two good classes, the advanced under Mr. Crawley, numbering 15, with an average attendance of l&iacute;, and the elementary under Mr. Field, number&not; ing 17, with an average attendance of 10. There is also a practice class under Mr. Pratt. All these classes are I think very satisfactory. The shrinkage in the&#39;attendance in Mr. Field&rsquo;s class is not more than is normal with beginners. Mr. Crawley&rsquo;s class shows more regular attendance, partly no doubt because it is composed of seasoned hands. The Economics class under Mr. Armitage Smith has a list of 16 names, with an average attendance of II. This is I think a high per centage, especially in view of the fate that attends most Economic classes in London. Mr. Alston&rsquo;s Law class is of course smaller, numbering eight, with an average attendance of 5&middot; &ldquo; As regards the Greek class, it has been found necessary to increase the number of teachers to three, Mr. Wilde changing over from Latin to Greek. The attendance is very often 14, but the average is 12. Grugeon, Director, reports :&mdash;&quot;As to the classes under my care, I am fairly well satisfied. In November, 1874, a Corporation was formed and registered (under the Companies Acts, 1862 and 1867) by the name of the &ldquo; &lt;span class=&quot;hitHighlite&quot;&gt;Working&lt;/span&gt; &lt;span class=&quot;hitHighlite&quot;&gt;Men&rsquo;s&lt;/span&gt; &lt;span class=&quot;hitHighlite&quot;&gt;College&lt;/span&gt; Corporation &rdquo; as a company limited by guarantee, but as it was not formed for purposes of profit the word &ldquo; limited &rdquo; was by license of the Board of Trade omitted from the name. The Corporation, which took over the assets and liabilities of the former company, consists of a body of persons (whose number may not exceed fifteen) in whom the property of the College is vested. Members are elected for life, and vacancies have been filled up by the Council from amongst their own members; but the number of fifteen having been reached vacancies are now to be filled up by co-option. The income and property of the Corporation must be applied solely to the objects defined in the Memorandum of Association, and nothing by way of profit may be paid to any of its members. THE COUNCIL. The government of the College, subject to the right of the Corporation to make regulations on certain points, is assigned to the Council. This body has power to regulate its own com&not; position. At present it is composed as follows :&mdash; 1. &mdash;The Principal and Vice-Principal ex officio. 2. &mdash;Teachers of the College Classes, being graduates of some University, or holding a position which in the judgment of the Council gives an equivalent .qualification. These Teachers if unpaid, become members of the Council as soon as they begin to teach ; if paid, when elected by the Council, and remain members for one year after they have ceased teaching or rendering any active service which the Council may deem equivalent. 3. &mdash;Twelve Students, to be elected by the Council from among the Student Teachers of the College, the Associates, or the Certifi&not; cated or other Students. These Students to be elected for three years, and to retire by rotation, four each year, but to be eligible for re-election.
4.&mdash;The original Council, as revised in 1873, but when the members of the Council who are neither teachers according to clause 2, as above, nor students according to clause 3, shall be less than twelve in number, the Council shall elect whom they please to make up that number. The object of this is to obtain the help of past teachers, past or present students, or others whose assistance may seem desirable. A third of these members to retire by rotation annually, but to be eligible for re-election. The Council appoint the officers of the College, viz., The Principal, Vice-Principal, Bursar, Librarian, Curator of Museum, and Secretary. They also appoint annually 12 of their members who with the Principal, Vice-Principal and Bursar, form an Executive Committee. THE COLLEGE YEAR Contains four Terms, viz., three full Terms of about eleven weeks each, called respectively &ldquo;October Term,&rdquo; &ldquo;January Term,&rdquo; and &ldquo; April Term &rdquo; ; and a short Term called &ldquo; Midsummer Term,&rdquo; beginning in June. Many of the courses of the Higher Division are not continued during the latter Term. DIVISIONS OF THE COLLEGE. The Classes are grouped in four Divisions :&mdash; I.&mdash;The Preparatory Division. II. &mdash;The Lower Division. III. &mdash;The Higher Division, containing Six Sections. IV. &mdash;The Special Division.
I.&mdash;THE PREPARATORY DIVISION. For teaching the subjects required for entrance into the other Divisions, viz., Reading, Writing, Rudimentary Grammar, and the first four Rules of Arithmetic. Only those who need preparation for another division are required to enter this. Students can enter this division at any time. The time to be spent in this division before passing on to the others must largely depend upon the amount of knowledge possessed by the Student on his entrance. Examinations. &mdash;The whole of the Students in this division are examined during the last week of each Term, and all who obtain 80 per cent, of maximum marks will be eligible for entrance into the Lower Division. Scholarships &mdash;Of those who obtain over go% of maximum marks the two to whom the highest numbers have been awarded will gain Preparatory Scholarships. Preparatory Scholars will be entitled to attend any two classes in the Lower Division for one year free of charge.,II.&mdash;THE LOWER DIVISION. For teaching those subjects which the Council consider essential to be known before the Student enters on the studies of the Higher Division. They are :&mdash;Arithmetic, English Grammar, and the outlines of English History and Geography. Students entering this division who propose, when later on they join the Higher Division, to take up studies in the first or second Sections of that division, i.t Language, Literature, History, &amp;amp;c., should, in the Lower Division, attend specially to Grammar and the outlines of History, and those who intend to join the third or fourth Sections, /.&amp;lt;?., Mathematics and Science, should pay special attention to Arithmetic. They are earnestly advised not to attend any higher class until they have mastered the elementary subjects, and never to take up more classes than will leave them ample leisure to study each subject out of class. Students who contemplate preparing for the Matriculation Examination of the University of London should consult the tables at pp. 53 and 54. Students entering the class for English Grammar, or that for the outlines of English History and Geography, will be tested in reading and writing, and those entering for Arithmetic in the first four rules. Should their knowledge of these subjects not be sufficient to enable them to profit by the teaching, they will be transferred to the Preparatory Division. TEACHING AND EXAMINATION ARRANGEMENTS Adopted by the Council in 1885. Extended and unproved in accordance with the Report of a Studies Sub-Committee presented in fuly , 1892. Supervision by Directors. &mdash; Each of the Preparatory and Lower Divisions, and each of the six Sections of the Higher Division, has a Director of Studies, who is requested :&mdash; 1. To arrange with the Teachers as to the examination of their classes at the times and in the manner prescribed. 2. To see that the Teachers ascertain whether the Students entering Beginning Classes are sufficiently prepared in the essential preliminaries, and if not, to urge that they be advised to join the classes which will supply their deficiency. 3. To visit the Classes in their Division or Section twice a term or oftener. 4. To make a brief report to the Executive Committee as to the work of the Classes in their Division or Section in each of the three full terms. Paid and Voluntary Teaching. &mdash;Voluntary Teaching, besides being of very great financial assistance, preserves the connexion with Oxford and Cambridge, and with former students, and introduces into the College an element of much value, both intellectual and social. Competent volunteers for the classes in the Preparatory and Lower Divisions, and for History, Law, Philosophy, and Logic in the Higher Division can always be found. Greek, Latin, Mathe&not; matics, Art, Music, Shorthand, and Book-keeping, are also mostly taught by volunteers, but, being deemed permanent subjects, are provided with paid teachers whenever it is found necessary. The Modern Languages, of which French and German are per&not; manently on the programme, are, with very rare exceptions, taught by paid professional teachers. These teachers are almost always natives of the country the language of which they teach. The Science Classes are in most cases conducted by paid teachers holding the certificates of the Science and Art Department. Practice and Preparation Classes are taken by advanced students and other volunteers, and are of much advantage, especially in courses which can be commenced only once a year (in October), and in which, therefore, Students entering in January require addi&not; tional help to enable them to keep up with the teaching in a class that is one term ahead of them.
University Courses. &mdash;Pending the conclusion of any measure for connecting the College with a London University, the following arrangement is adopted. The requirements for the College Examina&not; tions in certain subjects of the Higher Division are the same as those for the Matriculation and other Examinations of the London University, and students passing these Examinations with credit may therefore be encouraged to enter for the University Examinations. Anyone desiring to take up a Course leading to Matriculation should pass the Senior Student Examination. He would then be expected to study in the Higher Division the subjects required for Matricula&not; tion. By consulting the Matriculation Tables, pp. 53 and 54, and the Plan of Study, pp. 38 to 46, he will find which these classes are, the periods for entering such classes, the length of the course in each, and the scope of the examinations. He thus has the Matriculation Course marked out for him. If successful so far, he would then choose and pursue the line in which he would endeavour to graduate. The details of this University Course and the requirements of the London University are printed each year in the Calendar. Following the London Matriculation requirements, the subject of Mechanics is always kept on the College programmes, and, when practicable, the following four subjects are also included : 1. Chemis&not; try ; 2. Botany ; 3. Heat, Light and Sound ; 4. Magnetism and Elec&not; tricity. Teachers are asked to keep in view the Examinations of the Science and Art Department, as well as those of the London University. Instruction in Science in the College is not necessarily confined to classes taught by teachers from the Department. Examinations are either:&mdash; I. Class Examinations held by Teachers of the particular classes examined on the work of the term, or by Teachers or Directors interchanged among themselves by mutual arrangement. These examinations are held at the end of each term, and must not be omitted without the consent of the Director ; no certificates are given, and the examinations are used merely as aids to the teaching, and as tests of progress for the guidance of the Teachers and Executive Committee. Directors are responsible for these examina&not; tions and are to report results. II. College Examinations are held at the end of the first stage and of the advanced stage by Examiners appointed by the Executive Committee, and who are not Teachers of the examined class, but who conduct their examinations after consultation with and in view of the course that has been taken by such teachers. The certificates are marked &ldquo;excellent&rdquo; when 75 per cent, of the maximum marks has been obtained. COURSES LEADING TO UNIVERSITY EXAMINATIONS.,INTRODUCTORY NOTE. The College Students may be considered as consisting of two divisions ; (i) Those who come to the College with the definite object of specializing upon some one branch of study. (2) Those, mostly the younger students, whose general education is not yet sufficiently advanced to enable them to specialize with advantage, or whose interest in any branch of study has still to be awakened, and the direction in which their capacities can be best employed still to be discovered. The Educational Policy adopted by the College (see Report, page 19) is therefore to provide :&mdash; For the first of these two classes of men, by arranging that the subjects of the Higher Division are efficiently taught, and the classes adequately equipped within the limits of our means, so that the Student can take up his special subject with every chance of success. For the second of these classes, by arranging courses of study by means of which the Student can lay the foundation of a general education (see Plan of Study, Lower Division, p. 40), then take up a course leading to the London Matriculation Examination, and thence upwards as far as possible towards a Bachelor&rsquo;s Degree in Arts, or in other faculties. After passing through the Lower Division, the Student may, during his time of study in the Higher, find that he has a more or less keen interest in, and that his bent is towards, some one branch of study. If so, he will probably be well advised to devote most of his energies and time in that direction. Following this policy, the scheme of studies and examinations adopted by the College provides that the requirements for College Certificates, both first and advanced stages, should be based on those for Matriculation, Intermediate, and other Examinations of the University of London. Therefore Students who attend courses in the subjects required for these examinations, and obtain College Certificates in them, may reasonably hope to be successful should they present themselves for examination by the University. Some now entering the College may desire to make the passing of one or more of the University Examinations a means of giving form and adding interest to their plans of study, and may be glad of information which will be of some use in enabling them to arrange
for a continuous scheme of work. The annexed Tables have been prepared with the view of giving such information, but they must not be taken as intended to be rigidly acted upon, as they will necessarily require variation according to the time the Student has at his dis&not; posal, the amount of knowledge he has when he enters upon a course, his special inclinations, and the circumstances in which he is placed. The first Table has been framed on the assumptions, first, that his leisure is very limited indeed, only enough to enable him to study two subjects at once during his first two years ; second, that he has no preliminary knowledge whatever, beyond that which is necessary to enable him to enter the Lower Division. Students in the Preparatory Division must date from the time of passing out of that Division. It must not be supposed that in sanctioning such a scheme the Council have any intention of discouraging special lines of study. They would, on the contrary, prefer that their students should, for the most part, take up one or two subjects, and pursue them because they are interested in them, and feel that they have some capacity for them. Experience, however, has shown that many of the younger Students come to the College with no defined views on the point ; but, while they are actuated by a strong desire to obtain some kind of education, have in many cases not sufficient general instruction to make specialization immediately desirable, and in others have not yet discovered what line of study they are best fitted for. It is believed that following a course similar to these proposed will be of considerable use to some men, not only in enabling them to obtain University successes&mdash;a comparatively small matter&mdash;but in the much more important aim of discovering what kind of cultivation will most surely promote the full development of their minds. It should also be noted that the periods at which the Students can take the various honours of the College, and of the Science and Art Department, are noted in the Tables ; and that the time given in the first Table for arriving at Matriculation standard is the longest likely to be required by any Student, although his leisure may be very limited, provided always that he works with persistency and diligence.

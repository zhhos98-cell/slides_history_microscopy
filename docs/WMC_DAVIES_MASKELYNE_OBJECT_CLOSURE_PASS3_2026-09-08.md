**Date:** 2026-09-08

## 1. Pass-3 result

The Davies–Maskelyne case no longer rests only on retrospective descriptions of Davies's microscopic skill plus archival target lists. A specific glass-mounted mineralogical object is now tied directly to Thomas Davies's handwriting and departmental custody.

The object is the residue of J. B. Hannay's 1880 claimed artificial-diamond experiments, later registered **B.M.87756**.

Bannister and Lonsdale (1943) state that twelve particles remaining from Story-Maskelyne's 1880 investigation were found **mounted in Canada balsam on a glass slide**. The slide was accompanied by a card label `Hannay's artificial diamonds` **in Thomas Davies's handwriting**.

Encoding rule:

```text
Davies -> label / documentation / custody relation = HARD
Davies -> prepared / mounted slide = NOT PROVEN
```

## 2. Why B.M.87756 changes the Davies argument

Before this find, Davies's material competence was reconstructed from:

- his museum grade and duties;
- Maskelyne's training of him in microscopic mineral identification;
- contemporaneous meteorite lists;
- later official diaries;
- institutional histories of thin-section microscopy.

B.M.87756 adds a physical-object relation:

```text
minute mineral/crystal particles
 -> Canada-balsam glass mount
 -> Davies handwritten identity card
 -> museum move
 -> delayed formal registration
 -> later optical/X-ray re-examination
```

The relevant skill is not only `seeing under a microscope`. It includes keeping a fragile microscopic preparation addressable inside a museum collection.

## 3. Object chronology

### Dec 1879 — MacTear control case

A prior artificial-diamond claim sent to Story-Maskelyne was rejected after microscopic and chemical testing. Bannister and Lonsdale reproduce Maskelyne's account that microscopic crystalline particles were placed on a **glass microscope slide** and examined in polarized light before further chemical testing.

This establishes glass-mounted polarized-light examination as part of the immediate departmental diagnostic repertoire before Hannay's material arrived.

### 19 Feb 1880 — Hannay material accepted by Story-Maskelyne

Maskelyne wrote to *The Times* reporting tests of Hannay's minute particles. His evidence included:

- appearance/lustre;
- refractive behaviour;
- hardness against sapphire;
- cleavage-angle measurement by goniometer;
- near-inertness in polarized light;
- combustion behaviour.

The problem crossed optical, crystallographic and physical/chemical testing.

### 1880 — glass-mounted residue + Davies card

Bannister and Lonsdale found twelve surviving particles mounted in Canada balsam on glass. Their article explicitly attributes the accompanying card label `Hannay's artificial diamonds` to Thomas Davies's handwriting.

The article does not say when Davies wrote the card, but the object/custody context makes it a direct departmental relation.

### June 1880 — museum removal

The Mineral collections moved from Bloomsbury to South Kensington. Bannister and Lonsdale specifically note the survival of the fragments **mounted on glass** through this move.

The glass preparation was therefore also a transport/custody object.

### 1901 — formal registration

L. J. Spencer formally catalogued/registered the surviving material as **B.M.87756**.

This creates a long interval between active working identity and formal accession identity.

### 1943 — epistemic reactivation

Bannister and Lonsdale re-examined the particles under a polarizing microscope before X-ray work. Eleven of twelve were confirmed as diamond.

A nineteenth-century glass-mounted residue thus became evidence under a twentieth-century crystallographic regime.

### Current-survival signal

A recent Gemmological Association of Australia history reproduces a photograph of one Hannay particle credited to the **Trustees of the Natural History Museum, London**, with collection number **BM.87756**.

This strongly supports current NHM custody of material under the historic number. It does not prove that the original slide/card ensemble survives unchanged.

## 4. Davies diary crosswalk becomes exact

NHM `DF/MIN/20/2/3` is Thomas Davies's official diary covering **April 1878–October 1880**. It directly spans:

- the Hannay arrival and Maskelyne tests in February 1880;
- the Bloomsbury-to-South-Kensington removal in June 1880.

This is now a P0 reading target.

Inspect at minimum:

- 16–29 February 1880;
- 19–20 February specifically;
- late May–June 1880;
- any subsequent 1880 references to Hannay/diamond/slide/packing.

Search operations, not only proper nouns:

`received | examined | mounted | labelled | card | glass | balsam | particles | diamond | polarised | goniometer | packed | removed | registered | drawer`.

A single dated Davies entry mentioning the object would close:

`physical preparation -> Davies hand -> Davies daily labour`.

## 5. The card label is an address-system object

B.M.87756 is useful even if the diary never mentions Hannay.

Bannister and Lonsdale say no surviving departmental account of Story-Maskelyne's original identification could be found in 1943 and Hannay correspondence had not been traced. Yet the material remained intelligible because the glass-mounted particles stayed associated with a Davies handwritten card.

The card therefore appears to have performed a pre-accession identity function before Spencer's 1901 formal registration.

Working proposition:

> A handwritten working label could preserve the identity of a microscopic preparation across relocation and institutional time before formal catalogue stabilization.

Do not generalize this from one object without comparative labels/registers.

## 6. Relation to Davies's WMC education

B.M.87756 is **not a Working Men's College slide** and has no WMC provenance.

Its relevance is prosopographical and infrastructural:

- Davies is independently documented as a WMC evening student who acquired French and German;
- his microscopic/mineralogical apprenticeship occurred under Maskelyne in the British Museum;
- B.M.87756 documents his hand in the custodial identity of a glass-mounted microscopic mineralogical object.

Thus the case now supports a materially anchored composite-career model:

```text
WMC general/language education
 + museum occupational apprenticeship
 + repeated collection/documentation labour
 + microscopic mineral practice
 -> material scientific competence
```

1. **training relation:** Maskelyne taught Davies microscopic mineral recognition;

3. **physical-object relation:** Davies's handwriting is attached to the identity chain of B.M.87756, a glass-mounted microscopic mineral object.

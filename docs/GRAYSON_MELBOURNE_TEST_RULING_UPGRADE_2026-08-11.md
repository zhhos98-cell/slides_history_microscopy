# Grayson Melbourne test-ruling upgrade — 2026-08-11

## Revised status

**S-level surviving microscope test-object and provenance chain; exact engine-to-plate linkage remains open.**

The earlier Grayson entry treated him mainly as a methodological control because the ruling instruments survived while his research notebooks and papers were destroyed. The current Ed Muirhead Physics Museum catalogue changes that balance. It exposes ruled objects whose labels and wrappers retain maker, scale, custody and later museum-management information.

## Decisive objects

Museum identifier **42** is a surviving Grayson microscope test plate. Its label reads `Test plate 10,000 to 120,000. Ruled by H.J. Grayson, Melbourne`, and a second label enumerates twelve bands from 10,000 to 120,000 lines per inch. The museum describes it as the only surviving ruling in the collection reaching 120,000. This closes maker + physical reference object + internal optical address at S level.

The **54.1/54.2** group preserves a different kind of address history. 54.1 carries `ONE INCH divided into hundredths`, `Dup. Beck. Retain.` and `Ruled on glass. H.J. Grayson. No. 2`. The Beck wording is retained exactly as an unresolved historical clue; it does not yet establish a Beck sale, commission or ownership. 54.2 is labelled `60,000 # good`. Its wrapper says `Grayson Test Ruling, given to me by the late Mr W Stone`, signed W. M. Holmes, 18 September 1950, and a later hand records placement in a display cabinet by J. J. McNeill on 13 August 1971. The object therefore preserves a Stone → Holmes → museum/display custody chain without requiring Grayson's lost notebooks.

A separate box, museum identifier 43.9, is explicitly described only as `Probably Grayson trials or William Stone`; that uncertainty is retained. One slide says `Heavy lines up to 70,000. Higher Lines broken up`, but the whole box cannot be silently assigned to Grayson.

## Instruments and chronology

Museum identifier **35** is the later Grayson diffraction-grating ruling engine, made at home with William Stone, transferred to the University and used by T. R. Lyle from 1917 to 1930. It must be kept distinct from Grayson's earlier microscope micro-ruling machine. The surviving diffraction engine is therefore S-level in its own afterlife, but it is **not** evidence that engine 35 made test plate 42.

The Australian Dictionary of Biography records the relevant historical sequence: Grayson first made microscope slides and test rulings; his early micro-ruling machine was largely glass and wood; Stone made central components for an improved form; Grayson's rulings became internationally known; Carl Zeiss commissioned a plate extending to 120,000 lines per inch; Grayson exhibited rulings from 5,000 to 120,000 in 1894; later he turned to diffraction gratings. After his death Stone gathered the research notes and papers, which were accidentally burnt, while Lyle bought the micro-ruling machine and ruling engine from the estate.

The 120,000 surviving plate must not automatically be identified as the Zeiss commission. Same capacity is not provenance.

## Mechanism

The case can now be described as:

`maker → ruled reference object → line-density address → custody inscription → museum address/display`

The important consequence is that archive loss was selective. Grayson's notebooks disappeared, but some operational and custodial information migrated onto glass, wrappers, boxes, calibrated scales and museum records. This gives a different survival regime from Cole. Cole's number preserves a publication slot; Grayson's numerical scale preserves an optical reference function, while successive inscriptions preserve the physical object's custody.

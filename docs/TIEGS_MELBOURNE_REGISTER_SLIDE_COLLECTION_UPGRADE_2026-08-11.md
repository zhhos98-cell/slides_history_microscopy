# Tiegs Museum register and slide-collection upgrade — 2026-08-11

## Revised status

**S-level museum/register architecture + S collection-level slide survival; slide-to-register item closure remains open.**

The important upgrade is chronological. The zoology teaching museum was founded in **1887**, while its principal historical register, `Register of Specimens in the Museum of the Biological School, University of Melbourne`, was begun only in **1893**. University historical research describes the register as a catalogue of most of the collection. For the first six years, apart from sketchy labels, collection growth preceded systematic written cataloguing.

That lag is visible at object level. A shell collected by Walter Baldwin Spencer in Queensland in 1892 was only entered later as **register entry 159**. In other words, the material object could precede its institutional address. Entry 1 in the new register was a `Bufo vulgaris` received from the British Museum in 1893; a later Prague Museum exchange appears as entries **1208–1217**, recorded as `received named`. The register therefore became a mechanism for absorbing field collections, donations, purchases and international exchanges into one departmental retrieval order.

The University independently confirms that the current Tiegs Museum still contains an **extensive microscope-slide collection**, including contributions from Spencer and other former departmental researchers, and that the museum remains a teaching collection. This closes slide survival at collection level, but it does not yet identify which historical register entries correspond to surviving nineteenth-century slides.

## Modern address translation control

Victorian Collections exposes one especially useful non-slide control: the Grey Currawong skeleton now **MUZD1116**. The historical museum register places it in a batch transferred from the `Nat. Sc. Dept` in September 1899; the modern catalogue reconstructs that batch as **MUZD1113–1126**. The specimen also carries an older physical number, `2259`, written on the sternum. This is direct evidence that the museum can preserve or reconstruct several address states around one object.

Because MUZD1116 is a skeleton, it is used only to demonstrate the institution's re-addressing practice. It is not evidence for a microscope-slide crosswalk.

## Mechanism

The Melbourne pattern is therefore:

`working teaching collection → delayed formal register → later transfer/reclassification → modern MUZD address`

This is different from Cole and Cajal. Cole's address system was designed together with the publication. Cajal's objects accumulated successive administrative addresses. **Tiegs begins with a collection that already existed physically before the formal address system arrived.** The register retrospectively made an active teaching collection retrievable.

## Next bounded test

The remaining slide-specific task is narrow: obtain the microscope-slide inventory filtered to **Spencer / other pre-1900 contributors**, record front/back label text and current box/tray address, then query two or three candidates against the 1893 Register and any later departmental slide registers. Until then, nineteenth-century slide survival is A+/S-candidate at item level even though the museum, register and slide-collection layers themselves are secure.

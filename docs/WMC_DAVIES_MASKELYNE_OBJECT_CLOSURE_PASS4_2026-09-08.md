# Davies–Maskelyne object-level closure — pass 4

**Date:** 2026-09-08  
**Branch:** `research/working-mens-microscopy-19c`  
**Status:** archive-surfaces around B.M.87756 and the 1861 thin-section instrument are now bounded to exact files/volumes.

## 1. Pass-4 result

Pass 3 closed the Davies case onto a surviving-object anchor: the residue of J. B. Hannay's 1880 artificial-diamond experiments, later registered **B.M.87756**, was found in 1943 as twelve minute particles mounted in Canada balsam on a glass slide, accompanied by a card `Hannay's artificial diamonds` in Thomas Davies's handwriting.

Pass 4 does not add a new claim about who mounted that slide. Instead it converts the next archival search from a broad Mineralogy-fonds search into a small set of exact, contemporaneous record surfaces.

For the **1880 Hannay object**, four independent departmental records now overlap the event:

1. Thomas Davies's official diary `DF/MIN/20/2/3`, Apr 1878–Oct 1880;
2. Davies's file `DF/MIN/20/2/14`, **`Minerals removed to the laboratory` for analysis**, 1875–1884;
3. departmental goods/services requisitions `DF/MIN/26/1`, 1879–1925, plus laboratory order book `DF/MIN/27/1`, 1868–1891;
4. the exact later accession volume `DF/MIN/32/2/19`, whose number range **87172–89918** contains B.M.87756.

For the **1861 meteorite thin-section microscope**, an additional contemporaneous route has been identified:

- `DF/MIN/19/1` — **Invoices and delivery notes relating to purchase of equipment and apparatus**, 1816–1898.

This is a much better equipment-purchase route than the previously rejected early use of `DF/MIN/21` preparation-account material.

## 2. Exact 1901 registration volume for B.M.87756

The NHM hierarchy for the British Museum (Natural History) Mineralogy accession registers identifies:

- `DF/MIN/32/2/18` — 85631–87171;
- **`DF/MIN/32/2/19` — 87172–89918**;
- `DF/MIN/32/2/20` — 89919–93038.

Therefore **87756 falls unambiguously in `DF/MIN/32/2/19`**.

Bannister and Lonsdale state that L. J. Spencer catalogued, registered and labelled the Hannay material as B.M.87756 in 1901. The register volume can now be inspected at one exact entry rather than searched generically.

Fields to transcribe diplomatically:

`number | registration date | object description | source/donor | old/working number | quantity | locality/origin | remarks | hand | later annotations`

Questions:

- Does the 1901 register mention Hannay, Story-Maskelyne or Davies?
- Does it say `glass`, `slide`, `mounted`, `Canada balsam`, `particles`, or only `diamond`?
- Does Spencer refer to the pre-existing Davies card?
- Is there an older number or drawer/case address?
- Was the object registered as one accession containing twelve particles or in another unit structure?

This should distinguish the **working identity** preserved by Davies's card from the **formal accession identity** imposed by Spencer in 1901.

## 3. `DF/MIN/20/2/14` — Davies's analysis-removal list spans the Hannay event

The catalogue dates `DF/MIN/20/2/14`, `Minerals removed to the laboratory for analysis`, to **1875–1884**.

This is much stronger than a generic Davies-paper target because the file's stated operation and chronology both fit B.M.87756.

Search for:

`Hannay | diamond | artificial | carbon | particles | slide | glass | balsam | removed | returned | laboratory | February 1880 | 87756`

But do not require the proper name. The file may address material through drawer/register/working numbers rather than sender name.

If a Hannay entry survives, it could close:

```text
object selected / removed
 -> laboratory analysis
 -> departmental working identity
 -> returned to collection / retained residue
```

The file still would not by itself prove that Davies mounted the particles unless the wording says so.

## 4. Laboratory procurement around 1879–80

### `DF/MIN/27/1` — laboratory order book, 1868–1891

The series description states that the departmental clerk recorded **all equipment and reagents ordered for the chemical laboratory**. Volume `/1` covers 1868–1891, directly including the MacTear (Dec 1879) and Hannay (Feb 1880) episodes.

Search 1879–80 for:

- glass slips / microscope slides;
- cover glass;
- Canada balsam;
- mounting cement;
- polarising apparatus;
- goniometer parts;
- platinum foil/capsules;
- hydrofluoric acid / ammonium fluoride;
- sapphire / hardness-testing materials;
- forceps, needles, cells, small boxes or card labels.

An entry would establish **departmental availability/procurement**, not necessarily prove use on B.M.87756.

### `DF/MIN/26/1` — requisitions, 1879–1925

`DF/MIN/26` contains departmental copies of requisitions for **goods and services** written by the departmental clerk. The first group `/1` begins in 1879.

This supplies a parallel administrative surface to the laboratory order book. Where possible, crosswalk requisition number/date/supplier/item against the order-book entry.

Working relation:

```text
internal requisition
 -> external order / supplier
 -> laboratory receipt/use
```

If the same 1879–80 purchase appears in both books, the material infrastructure becomes reconstructable at transaction level.

## 5. New early-instrument route: `DF/MIN/19/1`

The project previously rejected `DF/MIN/21` as an early preparation-invoice route because its explicitly identifiable preparation-account children are twentieth-century.

A separate Mineralogy laboratory series supplies the correct nineteenth-century route:

**`DF/MIN/19/1` — Invoices and delivery notes relating to purchase of equipment and apparatus, 1816–1898.**

This file directly covers 1861, when the later departmental history says that a microscope with revolving graduated stage, eyepiece goniometer and polarized-light capability was constructed under Story-Maskelyne's direction for meteorite thin sections.

Priority search window: **1860–1862**, with 1861 first.

Search for:

`microscope | maker | stand | stage | revolving | graduated | goniometer | eye-piece | polariser | Nicol | analyser | objective | thin section | mineral | aerolite | meteorite | delivery | alteration | fitting | repair`

Highest possible payoff:

```text
supplier/maker
 -> invoice / delivery note
 -> bespoke optical specification
 -> Mineralogy Department
 -> meteorite thin-section method
```

Until the invoice is found, the maker remains open.

## 6. False route closed: `DF/MIN/25` parcel books

`DF/MIN/25`, Mineralogy Parcel Books, is catalogued **1913–1983**. It contains complete records of incoming parcels, but begins thirty-three years too late for Hannay's 1880 submission.

Repository rule:

> Do not cite `DF/MIN/25` as an incoming-parcel route for Hannay, MacTear, Clifton Ward or any nineteenth-century Davies object.

If an earlier parcel route is needed, search correspondence, diaries, accessions, requisitions and object-specific files rather than projecting the later parcel-book system backward.

## 7. Apparatus series caution

`DF/MIN/19` also contains later inventories of apparatus, including `DF/MIN/19/36`, `Inventory of microscopes`, dated 1960–1969. This may eventually help test survival/retention of older instruments, but it is **not contemporaneous evidence for the 1861 purchase**.

Likewise `DF/MIN/19/24`, `List of apparatus held or purchased`, is dated 1948–1954.

For nineteenth-century purchase history, `/19/1` is the present priority.

## 8. Current B.M.87756 custody state remains bounded

A recent Gemmological Association of Australia history illustrates one Hannay diamond, approximately 0.4 mm long, credited to the Trustees of the Natural History Museum and numbered **BM.87756**.

This supports survival of material/current collection identity. It does **not** establish from the public image that:

- the original Canada-balsam glass slide survives intact;
- all surviving particles remain on one historical slide;
- the Davies handwritten card remains physically associated;
- the 1943 X-ray investigation did not remove/remount individual particles.

These remain object-enquiry questions for NHM Mineralogy/collection staff.

## 10. What would count as genuine closure

### Grade 1 — already closed

`glass-mounted microscopic object -> Davies handwritten working identity -> later formal accession -> twentieth-century re-examination`.

### Grade 2 — achievable in one archive visit

`B.M.87756 -> exact 1901 register entry -> Davies diary / lab-removal list -> current physical mount/card`.

### Grade 3 — strongest occupational-material closure

A dated 1880 Davies entry explicitly linking himself to receipt, labelling, mounting, examination, movement or return of the Hannay particles.

### Parallel 1861 closure

A purchase/delivery record for the special meteorite microscope would close:

`institutional constraint -> bespoke apparatus transaction -> thin-section practice`.

This would materially anchor the earlier phase of Davies's apprenticeship without inventing a surviving 1858–62 diary.

## 11. Analytical consequence

B.M.87756 is becoming useful for a reason broader than the authenticity of Hannay's diamonds. The object exposes several consecutive **address systems**:

```text
experimental particles
 -> temporary laboratory mount
 -> Davies handwritten working card
 -> relocation-surviving object ensemble
 -> Spencer accession number B.M.87756
 -> 1943 analytical sample
 -> current museum collection identity
```

The historical object is therefore not simply `a microscope slide`. Its evidentiary persistence depended on glass, balsam, card, handwriting, drawer/collection placement, formal register and later analytical interventions.

For the WMC project, this gives a concrete endpoint for the composite-career thesis: Davies's adult education can be kept separate from his museum apprenticeship while his actual scientific labour is reconstructed at the scale of a named, glass-mounted object.

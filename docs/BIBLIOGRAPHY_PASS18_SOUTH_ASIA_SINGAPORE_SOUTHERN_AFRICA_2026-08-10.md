# Bibliography pass 18 — South Asia, Singapore, and southern Africa

Date: 2026-08-10

Status: verified bibliography expansion only. The frozen 307 / 155 surviving-object survey remains closed and unchanged.

## Admission rule

Pass 18 follows the existing **physical-slide first** rule. A current institutional page enters only when it identifies a bounded microscope-slide or mounted-preparation collection, an object-level slide set, or a material workflow in which physical glass slides are explicitly selected, prepared, retained, digitised or used. Broad museum pages, generic laboratories, virtual-slide platforms without a recoverable physical substrate, and gross pathology museums remain outside the bibliography.

This pass is deliberately narrower than a geographic census. It follows the frontier recorded after pass 17: named histology/pathology glass-slide collections and registers in South Asia, Sri Lanka, Singapore and southern Africa.

## Included records

### India

1. **Hansraj College, University of Delhi — Zoology permanent-slide collection (current institutional record).** The museum publishes a dedicated count of **561 permanent slides**, including histological sections, developmental whole mounts and organisms across multiple phyla. Its category table keeps the 561 slide count separate from jar specimens, skeletons and charts. This is a substantially stronger record than a generic statement that a teaching laboratory possesses slides.

### Sri Lanka

2. **Zoological Museum of the University of Colombo — Histological / slide Collection.** The museum explicitly maintains a slide collection from single-celled through multicellular organisms, covering invertebrates and vertebrates. Most slides were prepared within the department and a few came from external sources. The museum-wide 9,000-plus specimen count is a separate namespace and is not assigned to the slide collection.

3. **University of Sri Jayewardenepura Zoology Museum — Histological preparation (mounted on slides).** The collection page explicitly identifies mounted preparations in Histology, Parasitology and Embryology, again spanning single-celled to multicellular and invertebrate to vertebrate material. No slide count is published, and none is inferred from the museum’s broader 8,000-plus specimen figure.

### Singapore

4. **Krishnan, Rahmanzadeh and Dheen, “Digital transformation of histology – A new trend in medical teaching” (2024).** This source closes the physical-object problem left open in pass 14. The NUS-HALO platform itself is digital, but the study states that its histology sections were selected from the Department of Anatomy’s **existing physical collection** and that **160 histology slides across 13 organ systems** were digitised. The authors also discuss reducing the preparation and replacement of damaged glass slides. The 160 slides are a digitised subset, not a total collection count. This entry therefore does not reverse the pass-14 exclusion of generic NUS Pathweb/virtual-slide resources; it admits a distinct physical-slide-to-digital workflow.

### South Africa

5. **University of Cape Town Pathology Learning Centre — student histopathology slide set.** UCT’s Digital Pathology catalogue preserves object-level records for numbered members of a long-used teaching set. Current cases identify slide 21, slide 35, slide 64 and slide 85 and attach modern UCT catalogue numbers. The record language varies between “approximately 100” and “100” slides, so the bibliography records the historical set as **about 100** and does not claim an exact current surviving total.

6. **Cecil Jackson, _The incidence and pathology of tumours of domesticated animals in South Africa_ (1936).** Jackson describes a programme at Onderstepoort to establish a complete collection of microscopic preparations representative of the pathology of domesticated animals in South Africa, coordinated with the macroscopic pathology museum. More than 18,000 pathological specimens had been submitted for histological diagnosis over about fifteen years. That 18,000 figure is a diagnostic-workload/specimen figure, not a slide count, and remains separate from the proposed microscopic-preparation collection.

## Duplicate checks and screened exclusions

- **India Office `IOR/F/4/2450/135466a` (1851)**, the Timothy Lewis slide display record, and the **J. C. Wagner histological-slide cabinet** were already admitted in pass 13 and are not duplicated here.
- **NUS Pathology Museum** remains excluded because its public history is centred on gross specimens in pots/jars rather than a bounded historical microscope-slide register.
- **NUS Pathweb** remains excluded as a generic virtual-slide platform. The newly admitted 2024 NUS-HALO study is different because it explicitly documents selection and digitisation of 160 physical histology slides from an existing departmental collection.
- Generic current Indian teaching laboratories that merely list permanent slides among equipment remain outside. The Hansraj record crosses the threshold because it gives a bounded 561-slide museum inventory and category structure.
- Current South Asian facilities with slide-set counts but no collection history or object/register structure were held out in this pass to avoid converting the bibliography into a directory of teaching laboratories.
- UCT gross pathology collections and clinical photograph collections remain outside unless a physical microscope-slide set is explicitly identified. The student histopathology set qualifies because the object records name numbered physical slides and their long teaching use.
- Geological/petrographic thin sections remain outside pending the separate medium-scope decision.

## Research significance

The useful change is less geographic than infrastructural. These records add three distinct preparation regimes that were thin in the previous bibliography: a counted zoological teaching cabinet in Delhi, museum-based histological slide collections in Sri Lanka, and a pathology-teaching lineage in southern Africa that can be followed from historical preparation programmes into numbered current object records. NUS adds a fourth relation, physical teaching slide → bounded digitisation subset → digital pedagogical resource, without treating the digital image as the historical object.

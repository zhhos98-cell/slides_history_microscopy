# B.M.87756 after 1943 — serial reactivation of a nineteenth-century glass preparation

**Date:** 2026-09-08  
**Branch:** `research/working-mens-microscopy-19c`  
**Status:** object-afterlife layer; separate from the nineteenth-century Davies labour ledger.

## 1. Why this needs a separate layer

The Hannay material later registered as **B.M.87756** is not historically exhausted by its 1880 mounting or by its 1901 formal accession. From 1943 onward the same material repeatedly became an active analytical specimen under changing techniques.

The distinction matters:

```text
1880 preparation / working object
    !=
1943 X-ray specimen
    !=
1959 comparison specimen
    !=
1962 retrospective synthetic-diamond evidence
    !=
1975 cathodoluminescence specimen
```

The physical fragments may be continuous across these episodes, but their mounting, orientation, sampling and analytical status cannot be assumed to have remained unchanged.

## 2. 1943 — historical slide becomes an X-ray specimen

Bannister and Lonsdale reported finding **twelve minute particles mounted in Canada balsam on a glass slide**. The slide was accompanied by Thomas Davies's handwritten card `Hannay's artificial diamonds`.

Before X-ray work, each particle was examined under the polarizing microscope. Eleven were isotropic, colourless and diamond-like; one was birefringent.

The paper then subjected at least one Hannay diamond to:

- Laue photography;
- a rotation photograph;
- comparison against natural diamond diffraction data.

The plate caption is materially important: the Hannay diamond used for the rotation photograph is described as **`mounted in random orientation`**.

This proves a 1943 analytical mounting/orientation operation at the level of an individual fragment. The public text presently recovered does **not** say:

- how the crystal was freed from the historical Canada-balsam slide, if it was;
- whether the historical slide itself could be placed into an X-ray geometry;
- what support/cement was used for the X-ray mount;
- whether the crystal was returned to the Canada-balsam slide afterwards;
- whether all twelve particles remained on one slide after the work.

Repository rule: do not write `Bannister and Lonsdale removed the crystals from the original slide` unless a method note, conservation record or object photograph closes that operation.

Source:
F. A. Bannister and K. Lonsdale, `An X-ray study of diamonds artificially prepared by J. B. Hannay in 1880`, *Mineralogical Magazine* 26 (1943), 315–324.

## 3. 1959 — at least one Hannay fragment is reactivated again

Kathleen Lonsdale, H. Judith Milledge and Eric Nave, in `X-ray studies of synthetic diamonds` (*Mineralogical Magazine* 32, 1959, 185–201), explicitly returned to the historical Hannay material.

Their Hannay section states that it seemed useful to take a **random rotation (Cu-Kα) photograph of one of the Hannay diamonds** to test for crystalline inclusions that might indicate laboratory formation.

The result showed:

- no nickel diffraction lines;
- one faint unidentified powder line;
- a very well crystallized diamond;
- no X-ray distinction from a natural diamond.

They therefore leaned toward interpreting the Hannay specimen as a **natural rather than synthetic diamond**.

The object has now supported two different evidentiary claims under related X-ray methods:

```text
1943: crystal structure confirms diamond identity
1959: absence of diagnostic synthetic inclusions weakens synthetic-origin claim
```

The same material changes historical meaning as the comparison corpus changes.

## 4. 1962 — Lonsdale revises the historical conclusion

Lonsdale returned to the issue in:

`Further Comments on Attempts by H. Moissan, J. B. Hannay and Sir Charles Parsons to Make Diamonds in the Laboratory`, *Nature* 196 (1962), 104–106.

Later technical histories summarize her revised position as treating the Hannay diamonds as natural and doubting that the nineteenth-century processes had produced synthetic diamond.

For this object history, the important point is not who was `right` about Hannay. It is that B.M.87756 was repeatedly re-read against a changing technical benchmark after successful high-pressure synthetic diamond became available in the 1950s.

Working relation:

```text
new synthetic-diamond reference material / theory
 -> re-read old X-ray evidence
 -> nineteenth-century preparation receives a new provenance interpretation
```

## 5. 1975–76 — cathodoluminescence and a reduced analytical set

A 1976 *Journal of Gemmology* review of A. T. Collins, `The Hannay Diamonds`, *Industrial Diamond Review* (Dec. 1975), 434–437, reports that Collins **re-examined nine of the original diamond fragments**.

The review explicitly contrasts this with the **twelve fragments** mounted on the historical slide and examined in 1943.

Do not automatically infer that three particles had been destroyed or lost. The statement may mean only that nine were available/selected for Collins's experiment. But it creates a hard conservation/provenance question:

> What happened to the analytical unit between the twelve-fragment 1943 description and the nine-fragment 1975 examination?

Collins used **cathodoluminescence**:

- electrons accelerated at 50 kV;
- focused on individual diamond material;
- luminescence analysed with a Hilger Monospec spectrometer;
- result characteristic of natural diamond, attributed to aggregated boron/nitrogen donor-acceptor behaviour.

This is a different mode of intervention from ordinary slide microscopy or X-ray diffraction. The old preparation enters an electron-beam / spectrometric regime.

Source route:
- review in *Journal of Gemmology* 15.3 (1976), p. 138;
- primary target: A. T. Collins, `The Hannay Diamonds`, *Industrial Diamond Review*, Dec. 1975, 434–437.

## 6. The historical slide cannot be treated as a static carrier after 1943

Current safe chronology:

```text
1880
12? retained experimental particles -> Canada-balsam glass mount
 -> Davies handwritten working card

1901
 -> L. J. Spencer registration: B.M.87756

1943
 -> 12 particles explicitly found on glass slide
 -> polarizing microscopy
 -> individual-crystal X-ray/Laue/rotation work
 -> at least one crystal described as mounted in random orientation

1959
 -> one Hannay diamond again given random-rotation X-ray study
 -> interpretation shifts toward natural diamond

1962
 -> Lonsdale formally revisits provenance/synthesis interpretation

1975
 -> Collins examines nine original fragments by cathodoluminescence

modern
 -> at least one surviving fragment publicly illustrated under BM.87756
```

The chronology demonstrates **object continuity without warranting mount continuity**.

## 7. New object-condition questions for NHM

A current collection/conservation enquiry should now ask more than `does B.M.87756 survive?`

Request:

1. current object count under B.M.87756;
2. current number of fragments;
3. whether any fragments remain in Canada balsam;
4. whether a nineteenth-century glass carrier survives;
5. slide dimensions and edge treatment;
6. whether Davies's handwritten card survives and whether it is physically associated with the object;
7. any 1943 treatment/remounting notes;
8. any 1959 loan/analysis/remounting note;
9. any 1962 analysis note;
10. any 1975 Collins loan, sampling, cathodoluminescence or return documentation;
11. current container / capsule / mount / drawer arrangement;
12. whether conservation records account for the difference between twelve particles in 1943 and nine examined in 1975.

## 9. Analytical consequence for slide history

B.M.87756 is a particularly sharp example of a principle already visible in the Sorby material:

> A durable preparation can survive long enough for the **instrumental regime around it to change repeatedly**.

The object therefore has at least two histories:

- **history of preparation/custody** — glass, balsam, card, number, cabinet;
- **history of analytical activation** — polarized light, X-ray diffraction, new synthetic reference materials, cathodoluminescence.

These histories intersect but are not identical.

A historical slide should therefore never be treated as a neutral container whose evidentiary content remained fixed from the date of preparation.

# Oxford HSM / Royal Microscopical Society cabinet — upgrade 2026-08-11

## Result

Upgrade to **S-level collection survival + address architecture**. Oxford’s History of Science Museum states that its microscopical collection contains about ten thousand slides and that **over three-quarters are contained in one single cabinet on permanent loan from the Royal Microscopical Society**, with specimens prepared between 1860 and 1930. The same museum also preserves RMS manuscript catalogues and the Society’s internal archive.

The important distinction is that the cabinet survives while its objects have also been translated into a modern digital retrieval hierarchy. The 2007 `Small Worlds` database contains **10,084 records**, with **227 containers → 513 drawers → 4,293 slides → 5,051 specimens**, accompanied by 1,955 images. A modern record therefore embeds a slide inside nested parent coordinates rather than treating the glass slip as an isolated object.

## Documentary ecology

The Museum’s manuscript list makes the historical crosswalk unusually tractable. `MSS Royal Microscopical Society` includes, among other material:

- no. 5: catalogue of microscope slides of molluscs mounted by H. M. Gwatkin, 1883–90;
- no. 13: catalogue of microscope slides, mainly diatoms, 1860–69;
- no. 18: `Histological Catalogue`, a microscope-slide catalogue dated 1892.

The separate `MSS RMS Archive` preserves the Society’s internal records and has been deposited on loan since 1991 with continuing additions. These manuscript catalogues and the surviving cabinet occupy the same museum ecology, but they should not yet be treated as a proven one-to-one catalogue of that cabinet.

## Address-history significance

This case differs sharply from Cole. **Cole** is a purpose-built publication cabinet: issue/product numbers remain meaningful because they bind glass to a serial text. **Oxford RMS** is an institutional cabinet accumulated and preserved at society scale, then re-described by a museum database as `container → drawer → slide → specimen`. The historical object therefore survives while its retrieval system changes.

A useful compact formulation is:

> The RMS cabinet survived as a collection, but its modern legibility depends on translating cabinet position into a nested museum address.

That makes Oxford an especially good test of whether old catalogue relations survive re-addressing. A modern slide can carry a museum inventory number, drawer ID and slide ID while its earlier society catalogue address may still sit in a manuscript list waiting to be crosswalked.

## Grades and guards

- RMS cabinet survival at Oxford: **S**
- Scale/date range of surviving cabinet: **S**
- Modern hierarchical database architecture: **S**
- Same-institution RMS manuscript/archive ecology: **S**
- Historical RMS catalogue entry ↔ specific surviving cabinet slide: **open**
- Project journal/society event ↔ specific surviving slide: **open**

The `Small Worlds` database covers the Museum’s wider microscopical collection, so no individual database record should be assigned to the RMS cabinet merely because it appears there. Likewise, manuscript catalogues nos. 5, 13 and 18 cannot yet be declared catalogues of the surviving cabinet. The nineteenth-century and modern namespaces must remain separate until maker, subject, number, label wording or order supplies an explicit bridge.

## Next bounded move

Start with one historical catalogue only. No. 13 is probably cheapest because its date range is narrow and diatom labels tend to be diagnostic; no. 5 is attractive because Gwatkin is a named preparer. Extract two or three entries, then search `Small Worlds` for exact maker/subject/label matches. One successful old-address → modern container/drawer/slide crosswalk would promote the case from architecture-level S to **item-level institutional re-addressing S**.

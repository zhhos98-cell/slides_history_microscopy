# Nevil Story-Maskelyne, Thomas Davies, the Working Men's College, and thin-section microscopy

**Date:** 2026-09-08  
**Branch:** `research/working-mens-microscopy-19c`  
**Status:** high-value actor/institution case; primary-source boundaries explicit.

## 1. Why this case matters

Thomas Davies provides an unusually compact nineteenth-century example in which **working adult education, museum labour, scientific apprenticeship, thin-section microscopy, cataloguing and occupational mobility** can be followed in the same career.

The evidence does **not** say that the Working Men's College taught Davies thin-section microscopy. It supports a more interesting and more precise relation:

```text
brief elementary education / manual-museum employment
    -> British Museum attendant under N. S. Maskelyne
    -> mineral arrangement, cleaning, registration and labelling
    -> workplace apprenticeship in meteorite / rock-section microscopy
    + Working Men's College evening education in French and German
    -> junior / first-class scientific assistant
    -> practical petrology and assistance to wider geological community
```

The new institutional hinge is that **Maskelyne himself appears as a member of the Working Men's College Council in the official 1866 programme**. Thus Davies's museum mentor and Davies's adult-education institution belonged to the same London institutional orbit, although no evidence presently shows that Maskelyne taught Davies at the College or directed him to attend it.

## 2. Davies enters the British Museum as labour, not as a gentleman specialist

Lazarus Fletcher's 1893 obituary describes Davies as having received only a brief elementary education. After several years at sea, he entered the newly separated British Museum Department of Mineralogy in **1858 as a third-class attendant under Maskelyne**.

Davies's early work was materially intensive. He assisted in the arrangement of the mineral collection, including cleaning, registering and labelling large numbers of specimens. This should be treated as **epistemic labour** rather than background employment: the ordering and documentary stabilization of specimens created the collection on which identification and comparison depended.

Sources:
- L. Fletcher, `Thomas Davies, F.G.S.`, *Mineralogical Magazine* 10 (1893), 161–164: https://rruff.info/doclib/MinMag/Volume_10/10-46-161.pdf
- NHM Archives, Story-Maskelyne papers: https://www.nhm.ac.uk/CalmView/Record.aspx?id=DF%2FMIN%2F20%2F1%2F4&src=CalmView.Catalog

The NHM catalogue independently states that at the Museum Story-Maskelyne **worked with Thomas Davies on the proper documentation of mineral specimens** and later attempted a scientific catalogue incorporating crystallographic and chemical data.

## 3. Thin-section microscopy is learned in the workplace

Fletcher states that during Davies's early Museum years Maskelyne was studying **thin sections of meteorites** and initiated Davies into the microscopic characters of rock-forming minerals. Davies became exceptionally skilled at identifying minerals in rock sections and recognizing rock structure.

This is one of the strongest skill-transfer statements in the current project because it specifies:

- mentor: **Nevil Story-Maskelyne**;
- learner: **Thomas Davies**;
- material: meteorites / rock sections;
- medium: transparent thin section;
- operation: microscopic recognition of mineral characters and structure;
- setting: British Museum Department of Mineralogy;
- result: practical petrological expertise.

Fletcher's text therefore fixes the apprenticeship relation. It should not be rewritten as autonomous self-training or as WMC instruction.

Source:
- https://rruff.info/doclib/MinMag/Volume_10/10-46-161.pdf

## 4. Maskelyne's published method confirms the material operation

Maskelyne's 1869 Royal Society paper `On the Mineral Constituents of Meteorites` explicitly begins with **The Application of the Microscope to the Investigation of Meteorites**. He explains that, because the constituent minerals were minute and imperfectly crystallized, he sought the aid of the microscope by having **sections of small fragments cut sufficiently thin to be transparent**.

The method is therefore materially specific:

```text
meteorite fragment
 -> cutting / thinning
 -> transparent section
 -> optical examination
 -> comparison of minute constituent minerals
```

Source:
- *Proceedings of the Royal Society of London* 18 (1869–70), pp. 146 ff.: https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/0/04/Proceedings_of_the_Royal_Society_of_London_%28IA_proceedingsofroy1818roya%29.pdf

A later British Museum meteorite history adds that by **1861** a microscope with revolving graduated stage and eyepiece goniometer had been constructed under Maskelyne's direction for thin-section examination. It also describes the Department as operating with Maskelyne and a single assistant, Thomas Davies, during the early rearrangement and scientific study of the collection.

This makes the chronology compatible with Fletcher's apprenticeship account.

## 5. Davies attends the Working Men's College to enlarge his general competence

Fletcher then makes a separate point: Davies did not neglect his general education. He attended **evening classes at the Working Men's College in Great Ormond Street** and acquired French and German.

The separation in the obituary matters analytically:

- **microscopical petrology**: learned in the Museum under Maskelyne;
- **French and German / general education**: pursued at the WMC.

This is precisely why the case is valuable. Scientific competence is assembled across institutions rather than delivered by one institution.

The languages could plausibly matter for reading mineralogical literature, catalogues and correspondence, but that functional use must be demonstrated from Davies's papers or work rather than inferred automatically.

Source:
- https://rruff.info/doclib/MinMag/Volume_10/10-46-161.pdf

## 6. New finding: Maskelyne is on the WMC Council in 1866

The official Working Men's College programme for the **Thirteenth Year, commencing October 22nd, 1866** lists:

`N. S. MASKELYNE, Esq., M.A., Wadham Coll., Oxf.`

under **Council**.

This is hard institutional evidence. The same programme lists Alfred Grugeon as a certificated teacher of Botany and Council member. Its timetable includes Physical Sciences, and the wider examination structure recognizes subjects including Botany, Physiology, Zoology, Geology/Physical Geography, Mechanics and Optics.

Source:
- Working Men's College, official 1866 programme/report: https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/7/75/The_Working_Men%27s_College%2C_founded_1854%2C_45_Great_Ormond_Street%2C_Bloomsbury%2C_W.C._Principal%2C_Rev._F.D._Maurice%2C_M.A._.._%28IA_workingmenscolle188work%29.pdf

### Evidential boundary

The programme proves:

- Maskelyne belonged to the WMC Council by 1866;
- the College possessed an organized physical-science curriculum;
- former students/certified instructors such as Grugeon could enter governance and teaching.

It does **not** prove:

- Maskelyne taught a WMC class;
- Maskelyne taught microscopy at WMC;
- Davies studied with Maskelyne at WMC;
- Maskelyne arranged Davies's WMC attendance;
- meteorite thin sections were taken to the College.

All five are now searchable questions rather than claims.

## 7. College ideology fits the cross-institutional case unusually well

The 1866 report defines the College as a cooperative society for the interchange of intellectual resources. It explicitly states that classes should connect with pupils' daily engagements and that **study and work should illustrate each other**.

Davies is therefore a concrete test of the College's stated model. His occupational competence did not disappear when he entered evening education; workplace skill and general study proceeded in parallel.

A useful hypothesis to test is:

> For some working scientific employees, the WMC functioned less as a substitute laboratory than as a complementary educational infrastructure that made workplace technical apprenticeship more extensible—through languages, literacy, mathematics, social connection or credentials.

This should remain a hypothesis until student records and correspondence are recovered.

## 8. Museum and college are institutionally adjacent but functionally differentiated

John Ruskin's 1866 defence of the British Museum, written specifically in support of Maskelyne's mineralogical work, argued that a national museum was not a `working-men's college`: the museum's function of treasuring rare national specimens should be distinguished from elementary public instruction.

Source:
- Ruskin, `The British Museum`, 1866, later collected in *Arrows of the Chace*: https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/2/2e/Arrows_of_the_chace%3B_being_a_collection_of_scattered_letters_published_chiefly_in_the_daily_newspapers%2C-1840-1880_%28IA_arrowsofchacebei00ruskrich%29.pdf

This creates a productive institutional tension. The same social network could span both bodies while allocating different functions to them:

```text
WMC: general / cooperative adult education
British Museum: collection custody + specialist workplace apprenticeship
```

Davies crosses the boundary personally. Maskelyne crosses it organizationally through his Council membership. The slide/thin-section object may or may not have crossed it physically; that remains open.

## 9. NHM archival route: papers, equipment and `slides`

The current NHM catalogue for Story-Maskelyne's papers is `DF/MIN/20/1/4`, 1848–1911. It states that letters relating to the donation of **papers, minerals, laboratory equipment and slides** are at legacy reference `DF1/55`.

The same fonds includes:

### Reference warning

The catalogue's phrase `DF1/55` appears to be a legacy archival reference. Current hierarchy/ref numbering should be confirmed with NHM Archives before requesting material. Do not silently map the old number onto a current `DF/MIN/1/55` record without confirmation.

### Priority question

What are the `slides` in the 1951 donation documentation?

Possible categories include microscope slides, mineral/rock thin sections, photographic/lantern slides, or mixed material. The catalogue wording alone cannot settle this. The donation correspondence and object registers are therefore high-priority exact-source targets.

## 10. Davies after apprenticeship

Davies was promoted from attendant to Transcriber / Junior Assistant and later First Class Assistant. The later literature describes him as a highly accomplished practical mineralogist and petrologist. His expertise was valued because he could identify minerals and structures in rock sections and assist other workers.

The case therefore crosses a meaningful occupational gradient:

```text
attendant / collection labour
 -> tacit visual and material skill
 -> adult general education
 -> scientific assistant status
 -> recognized practical expertise
```

In 1880 Davies received the balance of the Wollaston Fund; Fletcher's obituary records Sorby's public recognition of the practical value of his work. This is a useful Sheffield connection, but it is an endpoint of professional recognition, not evidence that Sorby trained him.

## 11. Comparison with other WMC material-competence routes

Davies should be read alongside, not collapsed into:

- **Alfred Grugeon** — woodturner -> WMC botany student -> certified botany teacher;
- **Harry Govier Seeley** — WMC English/mathematics student + College museum secretary -> Sedgwick's Woodwardian Museum assistant at Cambridge;
- **Henry Swan** — Ruskin WMC pupil / engraver -> curator of St George's Museum at Sheffield;
- **George Allen** — working carpenter/joiner -> WMC drawing pupil -> engraver / visual producer / publisher;
- **Cooke network** — WMC microscope pedagogy -> amateur botanical society -> Quekett slide circulation.

The common feature is not a single curriculum. It is **mobility of material competence across institutions**.

## 13. Provisional thesis contribution

This case weakens a simple diffusion model in which elite scientists carry microscopy into a Working Men's College and working men receive it there.

A stronger formulation is:

> **Victorian scientific skill could be assembled across an institutional ecology.** The museum supplied objects, repetitive handling, specialist apprenticeship and technical instruments; the Working Men's College supplied complementary adult education and cross-class institutional connection; promotion and publication converted the resulting competence into recognized scientific labour.

The physical thin section is especially useful because it forces the analysis to include the worker who makes, handles, labels and learns to read the object, not only the author who publishes the final interpretation.

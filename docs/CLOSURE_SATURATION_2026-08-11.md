# Closure saturation — 11 August 2026

This pass marks the point at which the project has enough closed and near-closed object/document systems to stop broad discovery work. Additional public searching now has sharply diminishing evidential return. The remaining open edges are mostly exact-source requests, item-level crosswalks, or accession/register pages.

The strongest result of the last two days is not a larger survey. It is a differentiated set of historical mechanisms. Cole shows publication-address persistence: a numbered cabinet can preserve the identity of a material publication even when individual preparations are later replaced or misplaced. The Quekett Microscopical Club shows cabinet circulation and reference authority: donation, exchange, naming and lending made the cabinet useful, while participants themselves recognized that the finished slide did not automatically transmit the preparer's knowledge. The Hunterian Quekett material shows institutional purchase, teaching, printed catalogue and surviving alphanumeric addresses. Elcock shows workshop-to-market continuity from raw material and preparation operations to standardized type slides, prices and dealer networks. Oxford RMS shows society objects translated into a modern container/drawer/slide/specimen database. Cajal shows successive institutional address translation. Grunow shows one sample number crossing raw material, slide variants, microscope coordinates, drawings, annotated books and instructions for remaking preparations. Pacini shows preparation identity surviving remounting onto new glass. Van Heurck shows an integrated scientific estate repeatedly split and re-addressed by disciplinary institutions. Grayson shows optical reference values and custody inscriptions preserving different dimensions of object identity. Tokyo and Coimbra show two different ways institutions made microscopy teaching administratively legible. La Plata shows distributed reference-series numbers remaining scientifically operative after institutional absorption. Gibson shows a mixed cabinet retaining commercial, local-society and expedition histories. Tiegs shows formal register addresses arriving after objects were already in use.

This is enough to support a first-order historical claim without requiring a new global census: microscope preparations became durable knowledge objects through systems of addresses. Numbers, slots, labels, catalogues, cabinets, registers, publication references and later museum identifiers organized how preparations were found, compared, circulated, repaired and re-used. Material continuity was only one possible basis of continuity. A preparation could be replaced inside a stable publication slot, remounted on new glass, dispersed into a new institution, or assigned successive administrative numbers while remaining historically legible. Conversely, an intact glass object could lose much of its operational meaning when its address system disappeared.

The active research mode should therefore change from discovery to synthesis. Public-web searching is closed unless a search begins from a newly bounded identifier. The residual queue is request-only: exact Elcock letters at St Andrews; the current NHM Challenger binary; ZEISS dispatch/accessory evidence for the Balfour objective engravings; the complete Norman item/export layer; selected Oxford/Hunterian catalogue-to-object checks; Naples dealer/account pages; Tokyo microscope-register pages; La Plata acquisition records; and the Bourgogne accession file. None of these residuals is required before the existing cases can be analysed and written. They can improve provenance or close an item-level bridge if they arrive.

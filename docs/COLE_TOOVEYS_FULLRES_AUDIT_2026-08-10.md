## Cabinet construction from the two case views

The two remaining high-resolution photographs materially strengthen the cabinet reading. Toovey's catalogued the outer case as mahogany. The photographs show a plain reddish-brown rectangular outer case with a hinged top lid and a separate front panel hinged along its lower edge so that it drops forward. With both open, the top indexed tray is exposed from above while the edges of a close-fitting stack of shallow removable trays are accessible from the front. The pale tray wood contrasts with the darker outer case; no wood species is assigned to the trays from image alone.

The upper tray is specially made around the publication. Positions **1–4** flank a central rectangular recess in which the printed **INDEX** card sits as a fitted component. Slot numbers are written directly on the wooden tray rails, and the same hand-applied numbering practice continues through the photographed sequence to **52**. This matters because the address system is physically built into the cabinet furniture. The case does not merely hold a set of slides: wood, numbered slots, and index together materialize the publication's retrieval architecture.

The exterior is deliberately plain. The closed view shows two pivoting hook/turn-type fasteners on the front face, paired hinge hardware, surface wear and scratches, but **no visible title plate, cabinet-maker plaque, publisher mark, retailer label, subscriber name, monogram, or institutional accession mark**. The open view likewise shows no visible mark on the inner lid, front panel, or exposed tray faces. The underside, back, tray reverses, and reverse of the index card remain unseen.

Toovey's catalogue says **twelve removable trays**. The case photographs confirm a stacked removable-tray construction, but they do not independently permit a secure count of twelve because the whole stack is not disassembled. Retain twelve as auction-catalogue evidence rather than a new visual count.

### Cabinet confidence after the case photographs

- **S-level:** the cabinet is customized specifically for the **Studies Volume I 1–52 architecture**. The fitted index recess plus slot numbering makes this an object-level identification, not a generic-case resemblance.
- **A+/strongly compatible:** a nineteenth-century or contemporary cabinet. Construction, hardware, wear, and integration with the slides fit that reading, while photographs alone cannot securely date manufacture.
- **A++ compatible but still unclosed:** the actual **publisher-supplied subscriber cabinet** promised in the 1882 prospectus. The functional match is now very strong, but no publisher/cabinet-maker/subscriber mark or independently documented comparison cabinet has yet been found.

The useful formulation is now:

> The cabinet did not merely contain the publication; it materialized its address system in wood, slots, and an index.

That sits directly beside the repair finding: fixed addresses survived even where preparations were replaced, reattributed, or misassigned.

## Correction to the low-resolution v4 reading

The earlier v4 analysis over-read the opening tray and treated slots 5–7 as exact matches. The full-resolution photographs correct this. The cabinet should therefore be described as a **complete 1–52 publication/index architecture with a substantially surviving numbered issue set and documented replacements/substitutions**, rather than a pristine 52/52 original-preparation survival.

## Analytical consequence

The stronger mechanism is now maintenance through fixed address. The cabinet preserved the publication by preserving numbered positions even when individual preparations, subjects, and in one case maker identity changed. A compact formulation for writing is:

> Paper was bound as a volume; glass was bound by an index whose fixed addresses could outlive individual preparations.

Or more sharply:

> The cabinet preserved the publication by preserving addresses, even when it no longer preserved every original preparation.

This strengthens the bibliography/object-history argument. The material volume was not only dual-container and cross-referenced; it was also locally repairable. The index could survive object substitution and keep the serial sequence legible.

# Nevil Story-Maskelyne and the Working Men's College network

**Date:** 2026-09-08  
**Branch:** `research/working-mens-microscopy-19c`  
**Status:** deep-network note v1; separates verified lecture/governance relations from unsourced classroom inference.

## 1. Why this changes the Thomas Davies case

Thomas Davies (1837–1892), British Museum mineralogical assistant, is already a closed case of dual formation:

```text
Working Men's College evening classes
 -> French + German / general education

British Museum Department of Mineralogy
 -> Nevil Story-Maskelyne
 -> meteorite / rock thin-section microscopy
 -> practical petrology
```

A new source pass shows that these were **not socially disconnected institutions**. Davies's technical mentor and departmental head, Nevil Story-Maskelyne, had a sustained relation to the same adult-education movement and to the London Working Men's College itself.

This does **not** prove that Davies learned microscopy from Maskelyne at WMC, nor that Maskelyne recruited Davies into the College. It does establish an overlapping institutional network that makes those narrower relations archivally testable.

## 2. 1852–53: Maskelyne lectures in the immediate institutional prehistory of WMC

A modern reconstruction based on surviving handbills of the **Society for Promoting Working Men's Associations** identifies a lecture series at the Hall of Association, Castle Street East, beginning in late 1852 and continuing through 1853. The subjects included:

- F. D. Maurice — Shakespeare;
- Walter Cooper — Burns;
- John Hullah — vocal music;
- R. C. Trench — proverbs;
- William Johnson — rivers;
- F. C. Penrose — architecture in relation to the working classes;
- **Nevil S. Maskelyne — `Photography`;**
- Viscount Goderich — entomology;
- S. C. Hansard — popular astronomy.

Evening classes in grammar, English history, book-keeping, French and singing ran alongside the lectures.

The reconstruction explicitly treats these lectures/classes as part of the process that resulted in the London Working Men's College, formally founded on **31 October 1854**.

Source:
- Seth Koven, discussion reproduced in *The Condition of England* excerpt / handbill-based reconstruction: https://catalogimages.wiley.com/images/db/pdf/9781405188746.excerpt.pdf

Evidence level: **B_DIRECT institutional prehistory**.

The lecture title `Photography` is especially relevant because Maskelyne was already an experienced experimental photographer. His 1854 correspondence with Fox Talbot describes photography as a difficult **manipulative and chemical art**, requiring familiarity with instruments and materials rather than mere possession of a printed formula.

Primary correspondence:
- Nevil Story-Maskelyne to William Henry Fox Talbot, 29 Nov 1854, Talbot Correspondence Project: https://foxtalbot.dmu.ac.uk/letters/ftbh.php?docnum=07072

Do not infer the contents of the 1852–53 WMA photography lecture from the 1854 Talbot letter. The juxtaposition is analytical: the lecturer understood photography as embodied manipulation, but the actual lecture content requires its handbill/report or lecture notes.

## 3. 1866: Maskelyne is formally on the WMC Council

The surviving Working Men's College programme for its **Thirteenth Year, commencing 22 October 1866**, prints the full Council list and includes:

`N. S. MASKELYNE, Esq., M.A., Wadham Coll., Oxf.`

Primary source:
- *The Working Men's College, founded 1854, 45 Great Ormond Street ... Thirteenth Year, commencing October 22nd, 1866*: https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/7/75/The_Working_Men%27s_College%2C_founded_1854%2C_45_Great_Ormond_Street%2C_Bloomsbury%2C_W.C._Principal%2C_Rev._F.D._Maurice%2C_M.A._.._%28IA_workingmenscolle188work%29.pdf

Evidence level: **A_INSTITUTIONAL** for Council membership.

The same programme defines the College as a cooperative association between working students and unpaid university/professional teachers. Council membership therefore places Maskelyne inside governance, but does not by itself identify a class he taught.

## 4. Royal Society retrospective: frequent WMC lecturing

The Royal Society obituary notice for **Nevil Story-Maskelyne, 1823–1911** is indexed with the explicit statement that he **`frequently lectured at the Working Men's College`**.

Source:
- `Obituary Notices of Fellows Deceased`, *Proceedings of the Royal Society of London. Series A*; JSTOR stable 93288: https://www.jstor.org/stable/93288

Evidence level: **B_RETROSPECTIVE** pending page-level transcription from a fully accessible copy of the obituary.

This retrospective statement materially strengthens the 1866 Council evidence. It suggests that Maskelyne's WMC relation was active pedagogically, not merely honorary governance. The precise dates, subjects and frequency remain to be reconstructed from WMC records.

## 5. 1866 WMC science curriculum: what Maskelyne could have intersected with, not what he necessarily taught

The 1866 programme supplies a useful boundary around the College's scientific curriculum.

### Regular Physical Sciences classes in the Michaelmas programme

- **Botany** — Mr Grugeon;
- **Natural Philosophy** — Rev. A. Goalen;
- **Zoology** — T. Spencer Cobbold, M.D., F.R.S., F.L.S.

Maskelyne is **not** printed as the regular teacher of any of these classes in this term.

### Certificate / scholarship structure

The College's examination scheme explicitly recognizes:

- Botany;
- Physiology;
- Geology and Physical Geography;
- Mechanics;
- Hydrostatics;
- Optics;
- Chemistry;
- Natural Philosophy;
- Zoology;
- Astronomy among mathematical studies.

Thus by 1866 WMC had a curricular architecture broad enough to accommodate mineralogical/optical/chemical science, even though this programme does not assign a regular mineralogy class to Maskelyne.

This distinction is important:

```text
Maskelyne on Council + frequent lecturer
          ≠
Maskelyne regular mineralogy teacher in 1866
```

## 6. Ruskin's 1866 British Museum intervention creates a revealing institutional contrast

In an 1866 published letter defending the British Museum mineral collection and Maskelyne's work, John Ruskin insisted that the British Museum was **not a `working-men's college`**. He distinguished a national museum devoted to treasuring/selecting unique objects from institutions of elementary/public teaching, while arguing for separate educational museums accessible to all classes.

Source:
- John Ruskin, `The British Museum`, 1866, later collected in *Arrows of the Chace* / *Works*.

This is unusually useful because Ruskin knew the London WMC from inside and specifically named Maskelyne in the same argument.

The resulting institutional topology is sharper than a generic `science popularization` model:

```text
Working Men's College
 -> preliminary/general instruction; cooperative adult education

British Museum
 -> national custody; expert collection; specialist object work

Maskelyne
 -> actor moving between both institutional worlds

Davies
 -> worker combining education in one with apprenticeship/labour in the other
```

The institutions were ideologically distinguished even when the same actors crossed between them.

## 7. Museum demonstration and the Devonshire Commission

The Devonshire Commission reports that working-men parties did visit the British Museum with instructors for voluntary demonstrations by museum officers. It also records competing views about formal lectures. Story-Maskelyne opposed formal lecture courses inside the Museum but supported a lecturer bringing a class to the galleries to illustrate lectures already given elsewhere using specimens too large or valuable to take to the lecture room.

Source:
- *Reports of the Royal Commission on Scientific Instruction and the Advancement of Science* (Devonshire Commission), British Museum evidence: https://education-uk.org/documents/devonshire/devonshire.html

This position is almost a material articulation of the division seen in the Davies case:

```text
lecture elsewhere
 -> museum gallery demonstration
 -> unique / valuable specimen
```

It also gives a specific mechanism by which adult classes could connect to museum objects without turning the British Museum into a classroom.

No source in the present pass identifies a particular WMC class led by Maskelyne through the Mineralogical Department. That remains a target.

## 8. Photography adds a second manipulation regime to Maskelyne's WMC relation

Maskelyne's 1852–53 `Photography` lecture is not microscopy, but it matters to the slide project because photography and thin-section microscopy share a problem of **making an optical object through disciplined manipulation of glass/materials/instruments**.

His Talbot correspondence explicitly stresses:

- manual familiarity;
- chemical manipulation;
- instruments and materials;
- failure/repetition;
- glass or non-glass support choices;
- wet photographic processes.

Later, in the British Museum, Maskelyne trained Davies in microscopic mineral determination using prepared thin sections.

Do not assert a direct technical genealogy from photography to meteorite thin sections without evidence. The defensible point is that the same actor carried multiple regimes of optical/material manipulation into adult lectures, university science and museum mineralogy.

## 9. Revised Thomas Davies network

Before this pass the strongest model was:

```text
WMC ---------- Davies ---------- British Museum / Maskelyne
 general ed.                         technical skill
```

It can now be redrawn:

```text
Society for Promoting Working Men's Associations
       |  Maskelyne lecture: Photography, 1852–53
       v
London Working Men's College, 1854–
       |                ^
       |                | Maskelyne: Council by 1866;
       |                | retrospective evidence of frequent lectures
       |
       +--> Davies evening classes: French/German

British Museum Mineralogy
       ^
       | Maskelyne = Keeper / technical mentor
       |
       +--> Davies = attendant/assistant
             -> meteorite lists
             -> thin-section microscopic determination
             -> practical petrology
```

The key new result is **institutional overlap**, not a proven causal recruitment path.

## 11. Current claim hierarchy

### Closed

- Maskelyne lectured on **Photography** in the Society for Promoting Working Men's Associations lecture programme immediately preceding WMC formation.
- Maskelyne is printed as a **Council member** of London WMC in 1866.
- Royal Society retrospective evidence says he **frequently lectured** at WMC.
- Davies attended WMC evening classes for French/German.
- Maskelyne trained Davies in microscopic determination of minerals in meteorite/rock thin sections at the British Museum.

### Open

- exact dates/subjects of Maskelyne's post-1854 WMC lectures;
- whether one was mineralogy, crystallography, chemistry, optics or photography;
- whether Davies attended a Maskelyne WMC lecture;
- whether Maskelyne encouraged or sponsored Davies's WMC enrolment;
- whether WMC students visited Maskelyne's British Museum department as a class;
- whether slides/thin sections moved physically between British Museum and WMC.

## 12. Historical consequence

The Davies case should now be read as **overlapping educational and occupational institutions**, not merely two parallel settings. WMC and British Museum performed different functions, yet Maskelyne crossed the institutional boundary while Davies combined participation in both.

This gives a stronger working proposition for the project:

> Adult education did not have to reproduce the laboratory or museum inside the college. It could function as one component of an urban ecology in which the same teachers, workers and objects moved among institutions with deliberately differentiated roles.

For microscope-slide history, the next decisive evidence is therefore a transaction: a class visit, borrowed specimen, thin section, demonstration, student record or correspondence that closes movement across this boundary.

# Thomas Davies and the Hannay glass slide — B.M.87756

**Date:** 2026-09-08  
**Branch:** `research/working-mens-microscopy-19c`  
**Status:** object-level late-career anchor; Davies handwriting relation secure, mounting attribution open.

## 1. Why this object matters

The Davies–Maskelyne case now has a specific glass-mounted mineralogical object tied directly to Thomas Davies's hand.

F. A. Bannister and Kathleen Lonsdale's 1943 re-examination of J. B. Hannay's purported artificial diamonds states that the remaining material from Story-Maskelyne's 1880 investigation consisted of **twelve minute particles mounted in Canada balsam on a glass slide**. The slide was accompanied by a card label reading `Hannay's artificial diamonds` **in the handwriting of Thomas Davies**.

The object was later formally catalogued and registered as **B.M.87756** in 1901 by L. J. Spencer.

This is the strongest presently recovered physical/object-documentary anchor for Davies's material work because it gives, in one chain:

```text
named mineralogical problem
 -> microscopic particles
 -> Canada-balsam glass mount
 -> Davies handwriting / custodial label
 -> museum relocation
 -> delayed formal registration
 -> later polarizing-microscope re-examination
 -> X-ray reactivation
```

It does **not** prove that Davies prepared or mounted the slide.

## 2. 1880: the material arrives as an identification problem

On 19 February 1880 Story-Maskelyne wrote to *The Times* that Hannay had sent him minute crystallized particles said to be artificially produced diamond. Maskelyne reported several tests:

- diamond-like lustre and cleavage appearance;
- strong refractive behaviour;
- hardness sufficient to score sapphire;
- cleavage-angle measurement by goniometer;
- near-inertness in polarized light;
- combustion on platinum.

The material was therefore handled as a **multi-method identification object**, not merely looked at through a microscope.

A closely preceding false claim from MacTear shows the same departmental style: microscopic crystalline particles were examined optically, some were mounted on a glass microscope slide for polarized-light work, and chemical tests were then used to reject the claim. The Hannay episode belongs to this existing practice of putting minute mineral/crystal particles onto glass so they could be tested across optical and chemical regimes.

## 3. The 1943 object description

Bannister and Lonsdale report that twelve particles remaining from Story-Maskelyne's 1880 work were found:

- **mounted in Canada balsam**;
- **on a glass slide**;
- irregular in shape;
- about **0.4 × 0.2 × 0.1 mm** on average.

They state that no record of Story-Maskelyne's original identification could then be found in the Mineral Department records and that his correspondence with Hannay had not been traced.

The material documentary bridge was instead the accompanying card:

> `Hannay's artificial diamonds`

which Bannister and Lonsdale identify as being in **Thomas Davies's handwriting**.

This must be encoded as:

`labelled/documented by Davies`

not:

`mounted/prepared by Davies`.

## 4. The glass slide survives an institutional move before formal registration

The timing is unusually informative. Story-Maskelyne's first London examination occurred only a few months before the Mineral collections moved from Bloomsbury to South Kensington in **June 1880**.

Bannister and Lonsdale explicitly remark on the good fortune that fragments **mounted on glass survived the removal**.

The sequence is therefore:

```text
Feb 1880 experimental/diagnostic object
 -> glass-mounted fragments + Davies card label
 -> June 1880 mass museum relocation
 -> object survives without immediate formal registration
 -> 1901 Spencer catalogues/registers it as B.M.87756
```

This gives the slide a custodial function beyond optical viewing. The glass carrier plus Davies's card maintained object identity through physical relocation and through a long interval before formal registration.

## 5. Davies's 1878–80 diary creates a direct crosswalk target

Davies's official daily-duty diary `DF/MIN/20/2/3` covers **April 1878–October 1880**. Unlike the formation-period problem, this diary actually overlaps the Hannay episode.

Read the diary at minimum for:

- **Feb 1880**, especially 16–29 Feb;
- dates around the 19 Feb *Times* letter;
- **May–June 1880**, during packing/removal of the Mineral collection;
- any post-move entries through Oct 1880.

Search/transcribe terms and operations such as:

`Hannay | diamond | Glasgow | Maskelyne | particles | slide | glass | balsam | mounted | microscope | polar | goniometer | labelled | card | packed | removed | South Kensington | Bloomsbury`.

The ideal closure would be:

```text
B.M.87756 physical object
 + Davies handwriting label
 + Davies dated diary entry
 = named object handled/documented by Davies in real time
```

Even a diary entry only on packing or labelling would materially strengthen the chain.

## 6. Registration lag is historically consequential

The object was not formally registered as `B.M.87756` until **1901**, when L. J. Spencer catalogued and labelled it.

This creates at least two layers of identity:

1. **Davies-era working identity** — handwritten card preserving what the object was understood to be while still part of active departmental work;
2. **Spencer-era formal collection identity** — accession/registration number and institutional catalogue entry.

The twenty-one-year gap is analytically useful. It shows that a scientific object can remain institutionally legible through an informal/local documentary device before being stabilized in a formal catalogue.

Working formulation:

> Davies's card label functioned as a pre-accession address system for a fragile glass-mounted object.

That proposition should be tested against the original card, diary and 1901 register rather than generalized from this single case.

## 7. 1943: the old preparation is reactivated under new instruments

Bannister and Lonsdale re-examined the particles first under the **polarizing microscope** and then with X-ray methods. Eleven of the twelve proved to be diamond; one was a birefringent non-diamond particle.

Thus B.M.87756 has a long evidentiary life:

```text
1880 optical/goniometric/physical-chemical identification
 -> preserved glass-mounted fragments
 -> 1943 polarized-light re-examination
 -> X-ray crystallographic test
```

As in Sorby's surviving metallurgical preparations, the durable object outlived its first epistemic regime and could be activated under later instrumentation.

## 8. Current custody/survival signal

A modern Gemmological Association of Australia history illustrates one of Hannay's particles and credits the image to **The Trustees of the Natural History Museum, London**, giving **collection number BM.87756**.

This is good evidence that material from B.M.87756 remains under NHM custody and that the historical collection number remains current enough for image attribution.

It does **not**, by itself, prove that:

- all twelve particles remain;
- they are still on the original 1880 glass slide;
- the Davies card label remains physically paired with the specimen today;
- the slide has not been altered by 1943 or subsequent analytical work.

Those are object-curator questions.

## 10. Evidence boundary

### Secure

- Hannay's particles were investigated by Story-Maskelyne in 1880.
- twelve remaining particles were found in 1943 mounted in Canada balsam on a glass slide.
- the slide was accompanied by a card labelled `Hannay's artificial diamonds` in Thomas Davies's handwriting.
- the glass-mounted fragments survived the 1880 transfer from Bloomsbury to South Kensington.
- the object was formally registered as B.M.87756 in 1901 by L. J. Spencer.
- Bannister and Lonsdale used polarized microscopy and X-ray methods on the surviving particles in 1943.
- a recent published history credits a photograph of a Hannay particle to NHM under collection number BM.87756.

### Not secure / not claimed

- Davies mounted the glass slide.
- Davies personally performed Maskelyne's 1880 tests.
- the original glass mount remains intact today.
- all twelve particles remain today.
- BM.87756 derives from Working Men's College material or teaching.

## 11. Significance for the WMC project

The WMC relevance is biographical/infrastructural rather than provenance-based.

Davies is independently documented as a WMC evening student who acquired French and German while his technical mineralogical education came through British Museum work under Maskelyne. B.M.87756 now supplies a physical object showing that his later departmental role included the documentary handling of a glass-mounted microscopic object.

This makes the composite-career argument materially testable:

```text
adult general education
 + museum apprenticeship
 + repeated object documentation/handling
 -> durable scientific material competence
```

The slide is not a WMC slide. It is evidence for what one documented WMC student could become able to do inside a specialist museum workplace.

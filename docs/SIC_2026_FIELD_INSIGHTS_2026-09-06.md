# SIC 2026 — scientific instrument studies field insights

Date: 2026-09-06

Status: **external historiographical control note; source-locked to the user-supplied conference Book of Abstracts.**

Source: *XLV Scientific Instrument Symposium, Across Centres and Peripheries: Scientific Instruments in New Perspectives*, La Chaux-de-Fonds and Neuchâtel, 7–11 September 2026, *Book of Abstracts* (150 pp.).

Evidence level: **secure/body for claims explicitly stated in the abstracts; analytical synthesis for the field-level grouping below.** This note does not promote the conference programme into evidence about the nineteenth-century microscope-slide or optical-glass primary-source corpora. It is a historiographical positioning document only.

## 1. The field is not abandoning hard instrument scholarship

The 2026 programme still contains technically exact work on construction, operation, precision, calibration, classification, and reconstruction. Examples include:

- Afra Akyol on whether planispheric astrolabes can be operated beyond the latitudes represented by their plates (p. 79).
- Hamid Bohloul and Richard L. Kremer on reconstructing Ibn al-Shatir's reported astrolabe-clock from a difficult textual witness where no instrument or treatise survives (p. 81).
- Beyza Topcuoglu on reconstructing a thirteenth-century cylindrical sundial from written construction instructions and testing its transfer from Cairo latitude to Istanbul latitude (p. 86).
- Roland Wittje on reconstructing Oslo Observatory magnetometers from publications, correspondence, notebooks, calculations, and the historical observatory space (p. 117).

The important point is therefore not a replacement of technical instrument history by material-culture language. The older technical core remains active.

## 2. The strongest expansion is from object to trajectory

A recurrent unit of analysis is the instrument moving between operational settings rather than the instrument treated as a stable finished object.

- The CERN ELISA proton accelerator is followed from accelerator infrastructure into applied research, medical and art-conservation uses, and finally a working public exhibition. The abstract asks how meaning and status shift as the device crosses laboratory, disciplinary, and exhibition boundaries (p. 71).
- ETH geobotanical instruments are explicitly treated as **relational objects** moving between the Zurich institute and Alpine or international field sites; they make peripheral environments measurable and later undergo an identity change from working tools to stored collection objects (p. 146).
- Sabina Luz follows a printing chronograph that failed to take root at the Paris Observatory but acquired new uses and meanings in Rio de Janeiro, stressing circulation, local adaptation, transformation, and resignification (p. 112).

Analytical synthesis: the active question is increasingly not simply "what is this instrument?" but "what happens to its operation, meaning, and identity as it crosses environments?"

## 3. Materials are being used to recover distributed labour and operational possibility

Material analysis is not functioning merely as a conservation supplement.

- The Simplon 1905 paper connects Jaderin's measuring wires, Guillaume's invar alloy, Carpentier's workshop, tunnel conditions, and international metrological/geodetic institutions. The material properties of invar altered what field measurement could practically do (p. 67).
- Ava Spurr's diptych-sundial paper breaks the apparently single-maker object into ivory, brass hinges, magnetised needle, pigments, engraving, and illustration. The maker's signature is treated as insufficient because the object condenses geographically and occupationally distributed labour (p. 87).

Analytical synthesis: maker attribution is becoming one layer inside a larger reconstruction of materials, supply chains, subcontracted skills, and hidden labour.

## 4. Functional heritage and reactivation have become research problems

Several papers treat continued or reconstructed operation as an epistemic issue rather than a display choice.

- DRYLU tests solid lubricants for scientific and technical heritage under static and dynamic conditions using accelerated ageing, tribology, acoustic emission, imaging, and spectroscopy, while explicitly asking whether the intervention is compatible with conservation ethics (p. 73).
- The LUBEST work asks whether lubricants in working heritage should be preserved, removed, or replaced and studies corrosion, wear, ageing, and maintenance regimes experimentally (p. 76).
- The mechanical ballistic calculator case asks how lubrication should work for an object that is neither fully static nor continuously operated, but intermittently reactivated for research and education (p. 74).
- Wolfgang Engels's cobbler's-ball reenactment is explicitly framed as a **heuristic experiment** to identify plausible historical uses rather than to assert a definitive reconstruction (p. 137).

Analytical synthesis: an instrument's historical function is itself becoming part of what may need to be preserved, tested, or reconstructed. This creates a productive tension between material survival and operational knowledge.

## 5. Provenance, markets, forgery, and ethical stewardship form a major cluster

Session O, "Circulation & Networks: Scholars, Traders, Collectors, Archives & Databases," makes this especially visible.

- David Coffeen describes the international trade in early instruments through dealers, collectors, curators, restorers, auctioneers, thieves, and fakers, including the practical production and corruption of provenance (p. 104).
- Joshua Nall's "The Lloyd Evan Williams Forgeries: Connoisseurship on Trial" follows composite forgeries circulating between trade, collectors, scholars, and museums. The case exposes changing standards of expertise as instruments came to be treated not only as fine craftsmanship but as artefacts of past scientific practice (p. 105).
- Harvard CHSI describes ongoing provenance research on questioned objects and the conversion of archival collection files into structured metadata as part of ethical stewardship and public accountability (p. 106).
- Nina Kubowitsch reconstructs East-West German trade in technical cultural assets through the GDR's KuA, intermediaries, expropriated private property, and museum collections (p. 108).
- Sumner Braund studies Ottoman Jewish diaspora dealers in Paris and Algiers and the circulation of Islamic astrolabes and globes, asking how meanings changed across communities and networks (p. 115).

Analytical synthesis: provenance in scientific-instrument studies is now joined to art-market history, dispossession, diaspora, museum accountability, restoration, and the history of expertise.

## 6. "Centre" and "periphery" are being decomposed into different functions

The conference theme does not produce a single diffusion model.

- Dana Freiburger's Hori Takeo paper argues that Kyoto, Copenhagen, Manchukuo, and Sapporo could each operate as a scientific centre for a specific spectroscopy programme, while British Hilger instruments complicate the geography of production and research (p. 144).
- Jane Desborough, by contrast, shows a materially and politically real centre-periphery relation in nineteenth-century weights-and-measures inspection: standards, objects, expertise, and state power moved from London to county councils (p. 145).
- Luz's France-Brazil chronograph case shows that an instrument abandoned in one recognised centre could gain a new epistemic life elsewhere (p. 112).

Analytical synthesis: production centre, metrological centre, administrative centre, research centre, and collection centre should not be assumed to coincide.

## 7. Instrument and documentation are increasingly treated as one evidentiary system

A strong paper often combines object evidence with manuals, catalogues, patents, correspondence, notebooks, images, registers, and oral history rather than treating text and object as separate source classes.

- The neurosurgical-instrument study combines close material analysis with catalogues, patent records, surgeon-manufacturer correspondence, and oral histories to show iterative design shaped by practitioner feedback, prototypes, and abandoned designs; it explicitly de-centres the manufacturer as sole origin of design (p. 148).
- Wittje's magnetometer reconstruction likewise depends on correspondence, notebooks, calculations, and spatial knowledge because the original bifilar instrument does not survive (p. 117).

Analytical synthesis: the instrument is increasingly reconstructed as an operational/documentary assemblage whose parts survive unevenly across archives and collections.

## 8. Digital methods appear mainly as infrastructure, not as the field's conceptual centre

The volume contains structured metadata, digitisation, CT, XRF, 3D models, animation, augmented reality, and digital archives, but these tools are typically subordinate to historical questions.

- The Arithmaurel project uses CT, XRF, 3D modelling, animation, and restoration to understand mechanism, invention, collection history, and circulation (p. 110).
- Coimbra's augmented-reality soundscape attempts to recover a silent timekeeping operating chain and its social relation to railways and postal services (p. 135).

Analytical synthesis: the digital layer is functioning chiefly as an evidentiary and interpretive infrastructure for operation, provenance, circulation, reconstruction, and public history.

## 9. Historiographical field map distilled from the volume

**Persistent core:** construction; operation; precision; calibration; classification; maker attribution; technical reconstruction.

**Rapidly expanded core:** circulation; adaptation; provenance; collecting; conservation; reactivation; heritage status.

**High-yield intersections:**

- materials + labour;
- instrument + documentation;
- local use + transregional network;
- function + heritage;
- maker + practitioner feedback;
- object history + political economy;
- archival survival + reconstruction.

**Narrative forms repeatedly being challenged:**

- invention -> improvement -> diffusion;
- fixed centre -> passive periphery;
- one named maker -> finished instrument;
- museum accession as the end of an object's history;
- object and document as separable evidentiary worlds.

## 10. Project-facing inference — keep separate from source claims

For the microscope-slide and glass-history projects, the conference is useful primarily as a **historiographical comparator**, not as primary evidence. The strongest convergence lies in questions already built around movement, changing addresses or identities, material transformation, repair, reuse, distributed labour, operational context, and archival/documentary reconstruction.

The important distinction is that these projects should not import the conference's vocabulary merely because it is current. Where the primary archives already show preparation, packing, breakage, repair, remounting, recataloguing, workshop labour, changing use, or movement between institutions, the archive should continue to carry the prose. SIC 2026 is most useful for identifying where those source-led reconstructions sit relative to current instrument studies.

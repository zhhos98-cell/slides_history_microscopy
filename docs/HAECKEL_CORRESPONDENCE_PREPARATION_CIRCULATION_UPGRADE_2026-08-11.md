## 1. Amateur preparation-making as social repetition, 1852

Christian Ernst Weiss wrote Haeckel from Merseburg on 24 May 1852 (EHA Jena, A 16617). He describes a potato preparation used to see starch grains, stained with iodine tincture. More importantly, he had taken the microscope to an evening gathering at his brother's house, where mostly teachers and other friends showed such interest that he was asked to bring the microscope again at Pentecost and **make new preparations**. He asks Haeckel for interesting material that would be easy to obtain and mentions preservable infusoria.

This is an unusually clean preparation-use loop: observation → social demonstration → demand for repeat performance → need for new preparations/material.

## 2. Challenger Office box → Haeckel → report/accounting, 1880

John Murray wrote from the Challenger Office, Edinburgh, on 15 October 1880 (EHA Jena, A 41561). He says he had sent Haeckel a **box** from the recent cruise north of Scotland. Contents included mostly surface material, two mud samples, a large Lucernaria and a deep-water Actinia; the bottled surface material contained abundant acanthometrae. Murray asks Haeckel to identify the genera of Radiolaria because Wyville Thomson needs them for a preliminary report. He also reminds Haeckel that Actinia from the Challenger had been sent earlier and suggests delegating them to a pupil if Haeckel has no time.

The same letter then switches to transport accounting: Murray asks Haeckel to obtain the railway bill or a copy, or rewrite a note in the required form, because the Treasury requires accounts/payment documentation.

This is a very strong event because **shipment, expert identification, report production, labour delegation and reimbursement paperwork occupy the same material route**.

## 3. Professional preparator: inspection, price, purchase, return, 1885

Emil Wiese, preparator at the natural-history museum in Strasbourg, wrote Haeckel on 17 October 1885 (EHA Jena, A 46372). He recalls having sent skeletons about four years earlier which Haeckel accepted, then sends further preparations `zur Ansicht resp. Ankauf` — for inspection and possible purchase. He prices individual preparations in marks and states that skeletons which do not please the buyer may be returned.

This is S-level evidence for an **approval-sale preparation market** inside Haeckel's network. Guard: these are mainly anatomical/skeletal preparations, so this is a preparator-market comparator rather than direct microscope-slide commerce.

## 4. Haeckel outward gift: Radiolaria on glass slides, 1896

Marie Eugenie delle Grazie thanked Haeckel on 7 November 1896 (EHA Jena, A 11) for a package that included a Radiolaria collection. She calls it a great gift and writes of the moment when she will see the `Glastäfelchen` under the microscope.

This gives a direct outward route: **Haeckel → Radiolaria collection → glass slides → gift recipient → future microscopic viewing**. The letter proves delivery and the intended mode of use. It does not identify the slide maker, source specimens or present survival.

## Mechanism

The Haeckel material is now more useful as a **transaction grammar** than as one linear provenance chain. The same broad category `preparation` moves through amateur making, expedition logistics, report production, professional sale, returnability, gift exchange and microscopic viewing.

Cabinets such as Cole and Gibson preserve relations spatially through slots/trays. The correspondence preserves relations as verbs: **make, send, inspect, buy, return, identify, report, reimburse, give, view**.

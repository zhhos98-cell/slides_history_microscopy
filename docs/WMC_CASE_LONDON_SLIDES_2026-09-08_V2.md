# London case v2 — WMC museum, Cooke, Davies, and the slide economy

**Date:** 2026-09-08  
**Branch:** `research/working-mens-microscopy-19c`  
**Supersedes for current analysis:** `WMC_CASE_LONDON_SLIDES_2026-09-08.md`.

## 1. Revised claim

London is stronger than a simple `WMC -> Cooke -> Quekett` story. The Working Men's College had a **material natural-history culture from 1856**, before Cooke's 1861 microscope class; named WMC participants later moved into museum and microscopic work; and Quekett subsequently turned mutual amateur microscopy into a large slide-borrowing/exchange infrastructure.

The current London model is therefore:

```text
WMC liberal education
 + WMC museum / specimen culture
 + working adult students
        |
        +-> Cooke microscope pedagogy -> former pupils -> amateur society -> Quekett slide economy
        |
        +-> museum / workplace scientific careers (Seeley; Davies)
        |
        +-> late WMC natural-history club with microscopic observations
```

## 2. Material culture inside the WMC from 1856

Marcella Pellegrino Sutcliffe's study of WMC science records that the **College Museum was founded in 1856 through contributions from students and teachers**. It assembled fossils, minerals, shells and antiquities; scientific objects and papers were presented; country walks supplemented Natural History classes.

Source:
- repository text: https://api.repository.cam.ac.uk/server/api/core/bitstreams/34150dc6-eef1-4aee-8727-ea138159b57c/content

This establishes that the WMC itself was already a place where working students could encounter, contribute and help organize natural-history objects. It is not yet evidence for microscope slides.

## 3. Cooke: microscope pedagogy enters the College

Nathan E. C. Smith documents Mordecai Cubitt Cooke's 1861 evening class in **Physiological Botany**, with the microscope explicitly part of the programme. Cooke remained connected to former WMC pupils, including Alfred Grugeon.

Source:
- https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/00033790.2023.2181398

A WMC programme for 1866 lists **Grugeon teaching Botany under Physical Sciences**, alongside Natural Philosophy and Zoology. This shows continuity of natural-science teaching through a Cooke-linked pupil/teacher network.

Primary programme:
- https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/7/75/The_Working_Men%27s_College%2C_founded_1854%2C_45_Great_Ormond_Street%2C_Bloomsbury%2C_W.C._Principal%2C_Rev._F.D._Maurice%2C_M.A._.._%28IA_workingmenscolle188work%29.pdf

## 4. Cooke / former pupils -> Society of Amateur Botanists -> Quekett

Smith reconstructs the Society of Amateur Botanists as a Cooke-led association involving former WMC pupils. Excursions, specimen interchange, library, herbarium and museum converted teacher-pupil relations into member-run material infrastructure.

Quekett's own history records that this association supplied the organizational model for the Quekett Microscopical Club in 1865; Cooke, Thomas Ketteringham and Witham Bywater were already meeting weekly to use microscopes and examine objects.

Sources:
- https://www.quekett.org/about/who/history
- https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/british-journal-for-the-history-of-science/article/illustrating-natural-history-images-periodicals-and-the-making-of-nineteenthcentury-scientific-communities/8B1DA7E7CA3CE9FBDA3311F5BF5F514F

By 1873 Quekett's general collection exceeded **2,000 preparations** and supported member borrowing and slide exchange.

Source:
- https://academic.oup.com/jhc/article/37/3/507/8653474

Thus the strongest institutional slide chain remains:

```text
WMC microscope pedagogy
 -> pupil network
 -> mutual specimen society
 -> Quekett
 -> preparation collection
 -> borrowing / exchange
```

The contributor-level relation `named WMC pupil -> named Quekett slide` still requires accession work.

## 5. Thomas Davies: the named WMC attendee who becomes a thin-section microscopist

L. Fletcher's obituary of **Thomas Davies (1837-1892)** supplies a different and exceptionally strong route.

Davies had brief elementary schooling and entered the British Museum in **1858** as a third-class attendant under Nevil Story-Maskelyne. In the early years of Davies's Museum work, Maskelyne was studying **thin sections of meteorites** and initiated Davies into the microscopic characters of rock-forming minerals. Davies became highly skilled in microscopic determination in rock sections.

The same source states that Davies improved his general education by attending **evening classes at the Working Men's College in Great Ormond Street**, acquiring French and German.

Source:
- L. Fletcher, `Thomas Davies, F.G.S.`, *Mineralogical Magazine* 10 (1893), 161-164: https://rruff.info/doclib/MinMag/Volume_10/10-46-161.pdf

This is a closed named-person relation:

```text
WMC evening education ----\
                           -> Thomas Davies -> scientific assistant / petrologist
Maskelyne museum training -/              -> thin-section microscopy
```

Evidence boundary: the WMC supplied general/language education in the obituary; **Maskelyne**, not the WMC, supplied the microscopic mineral training.

Historically this is highly significant. It shows how adult liberal education could coexist with workplace apprenticeship to move an attendant toward expert scientific labour. The relevant history of working-men microscopy is therefore not confined to classes explicitly titled `microscopy`.

## 6. Harry Govier Seeley: WMC museum competence leaves London

The Science Museum Group records that Harry Govier Seeley studied English and mathematics at the WMC in the late 1850s and became **secretary to the College museum**. In 1859 he entered Sidney Sussex College, Cambridge and was employed by Adam Sedgwick as an assistant in the Woodwardian Museum, lecturing and cataloguing fossils.

Source:
- https://collection.sciencemuseumgroup.org.uk/people/ap28054/seeley-harry-govier

This gives a direct WMC material-skill route into Cambridge, even while microscopy at the Cambridge WMC itself remains unclosed.

## 7. Microscopy returns inside WMC social life: Lubbock Field Club

In 1893 the WMC's Lubbock Field Club was established to collect and record facts in natural science, organize excursions, discuss papers and questions, and conduct **microscopic observations and exhibitions of specimens**.

Source:
- Sutcliffe repository text: https://api.repository.cam.ac.uk/server/api/core/bitstreams/34150dc6-eef1-4aee-8727-ea138159b57c/content

This demonstrates direct late-century microscopy inside a WMC club environment. Physical glass-slide holdings remain to be identified.

## 8. Current London evidence ladder

### Closed material relations

- 1856 WMC museum: fossils/minerals/shells/antiquities contributed by members.
- 1861 Cooke: microscope explicitly in physiological-botany pedagogy.
- Davies: WMC evening student + Maskelyne-trained thin-section microscopist.
- Seeley: WMC museum secretary -> Woodwardian Museum assistant.
- 1865-73 Quekett: large preparation collection, borrowing and exchange downstream from WMC-linked amateur network.
- 1893 Lubbock Field Club: microscopic observations and specimen exhibitions at WMC.

### Still open

- physical slides used in Cooke's WMC class;
- WMC museum microscope/preparation cabinet;
- named WMC pupil -> named Quekett slide accession/donation/exchange;
- physical slides used by Lubbock Field Club.

## 9. Revised analytical formulation

London now supports two intertwined mechanisms rather than one:

1. **institutional reproduction** — WMC pedagogy becomes self-organized amateur society and eventually a slide-library/exchange economy;
2. **occupational mobility** — WMC general and museum education accompanies working or lower-status museum personnel into curatorial and microscopic expertise.

This second mechanism, made visible by Davies and Seeley, connects the WMC question directly to histories of museum labour and technical skill.

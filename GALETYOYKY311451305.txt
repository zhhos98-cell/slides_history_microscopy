The Students are, for the most part, working men, and the Teachers are, in general, members of the Universities and of the different professions, or those w&#39;ho have themselves been Students in the College. Its purpose is to unite these classes together by associating them in the common work of teaching and learning. It provides in&not; struction at the smallest possible cost (the teaching being almost wholly unpaid) in the subjects with which it most concerns English citizens to be acquainted, and thus tries to place a liberal education within the reach of working men.
FOURTEENTH REPORT,OF THE &lt;span class=&quot;hitHighlite&quot;&gt;WORKING&lt;/span&gt; &lt;span class=&quot;hitHighlite&quot;&gt;MEN&rsquo;S&lt;/span&gt; &lt;span class=&quot;hitHighlite&quot;&gt;COLLEGE&lt;/span&gt;.,A comparatively short time has passed since the issue of the last Report in July, 1889, but it has been thought advisable that in the future some brief statement of the position of the College should be placed on record every year. The efforts of the Advertising Sub-Committee have been con&not; tinued with success. It maybe remembered that in the October term of 1888, the number of class entries was 800; but in the same term of 1889, the class entries amounted to 1,110, and the in&not; dividual students to 875&mdash;numbers which were in excess of any attained since the foundation of the College, and which on some nights in the week rendered it no easy task to provide class&not; rooms for the various teachers. During the year ending 2nd August, 1890, as many as 1,099 individual students entered for one or more of the classes, and so passed to some extent under the influence of the College. In the present term the numbers have not yet been received in full, but up to the 2nd week in November the class entries have been 989, and the individual students 769. The annexed tables show, at various dates, the dis&not; tribution of the students amongst the Preparatory, Lower and Higher Divisions, and the relative attractions of the several sub&not; jects taught. They also give the proportions of class entries to individuals, and, with regard to new students, an analysis of the numbers according to their respective ages and employments. It may be observed that the percentage of class entries compared with individuals has rather increased, and that the percentage of clerks, collectors, reporters and other students pursuing similar vocations, as distinguished from those employed in actual handi&not; crafts or other manual work, shows some tendency to diminish. The financial position of the College is, comparatively speak&not; ing, satisfactory. Whilst the amounts received from students&rsquo; Les are rising, it has been found possible to provide lor the increased numbers with no great increase ol expenditure under any head, except that of class teachers. 1 he total of donations and subscriptions in 18891 is unusually high in consequence of a special appeal which was circulated in that year, and which from some friends of the College met with a most generous response. The Annual Grant of /400 which the Charity Commissioners have allotted, out of the City of London Parochial Funds, to this College, and the College for Men and Women in Queen Square,
2,though not sufficient to permit any large development or exten&not; sion of the College work that is not self-supporting, will enable that work to be continued on at least its present footing without the old trouble of frequently recurring deficits. It is not yet certain when the first payment will be received, as the Scheme of the Charity Commissioners has to go through many formalities before its final ratification. The grant was allotted on the condition that some kind of union should be established between the two Colleges, and accordingly a working alliance was arranged, which took effect from October, 1889. A complete amalgamation!was subsequently proposed and discussed, but it was found difficult to arrive at a settlement of all the points that arose, and it was ultimately decided to continue the alliance already arranged. This arrange&not; ment is now in operation, and its terms have been embodied, with some few changes and additions, in the Scheme prepared by the Charity Commissioners. Under its provisions, many of the classes in each College are open to the students of the other; the two Colleges jointly arrange the Saturday nights&rsquo; lectures, and, with respect to men students, reciprocally throw open their Libraries and Common Rooms. A Joint Committee is appointed to carry out the provisions of the Alliance. The free popular lectures on Saturday nights have been as a rule very well attended. MURE, Vice-Principal. &lt;span class=&quot;hitHighlite&quot;&gt;Working&lt;/span&gt; &lt;span class=&quot;hitHighlite&quot;&gt;Men&rsquo;s&lt;/span&gt; &lt;span class=&quot;hitHighlite&quot;&gt;College&lt;/span&gt;, November , 1890.,Mr. TanslEY, Director of the Preparatory Division, reports as follows:&mdash; Although we have not quite so many members this (October) term as last year, we are holding them better, as the ratio of attendances to entries is larger. The effect of the lower scale of fees, introduced in 1888, is still noticeable. We are now attracting men with less leisure for home work than those who formerly came to us ; and we consequently have to keep them in the Preparatory Division longer. The students used often to pass into the Lower Division in one term, but we now have to keep the majority of them two or even three terms. Still we pass up a large, if not a larger proportion of our men, and many of them take a good place in the Lower Division. It seems that in the Preparatory Division we touch a stratum not touched by any other institution for adult education, except the recently established Board School Evening Classes, which do not aim at creating and fostering College life and spirit as we do in all our Divisions. If it be added that our Preparatory Division is most certainly training its members to rise from one stage of the College curriculum to the other, it would appear that this Division is fully justifying its existence. As arithmetic teacher in the Lower Division Mr. TaNSLEY reports that, as last year the classes were too large for efficient management, it has been requisite this year to have three instead of two. The students having little time for work at home the courses have been made longer, and half-course certificates have been instituted. The practice classes have proved very useful, and are much appre&not; ciated by the students. The number of the Grammar classes has also been increased to three, in which the subject is carefully studied from beginning to end. Much homework is done, in the preparation of which the students get valuable individual help from the teachers of the practice class. Mr. Dodds reports that in the Higher Division the work continues satisfactory. The class of English for Foreigners has been temporarily discontinued, but will be resumed in January, 1891. The Science classes under Mr. Scott have in&not; creased in numbers and vigour, the subjects of Physiology and Biology having been again taken up after an interval. The number of Shorthand students has cut the^Vecord, as Mr. Pocock&rsquo;s special report shews. The French class is most flourishing, seven separate sections receive instruction from M. Petr&eacute; personally, and his assistants conduct practice classes. The German class has also grown since 1889, while new classes have been started in Italian and Spanish Under Mr. Cook&rsquo;s efficient supervision, the Art class which has always been a feature in the College curriculum, has been well maintained. Mr. L. PoCOCK reports that the Shorthand classes for the year ending August 1890, show a large increase in the number of students as well as an increase of classes held per week, as compared with the preceding year. In the October term of 1889 the seven weekly classes comprised 137 students, who entered the four different sections into which the classes are divided in the following pro&not; portions_Beginners 69, of whom 20 presented themselves for examination in December, and 15 passed into the Intermediate; Intermediate30, of whom 10 candidates presented themselves and 3 passed ; Advanced 28, of whom 18 were examined and 16 passed ; and Reporting 10, of whom 3 were examined and all passed. These classes are with one exception conducted by present or past students of the College. Extension classes in connection with the College are also being held at the Alexandra Institute, Walworth, and at the Presbyterian Church Institute, Somers Town. At the first named centre, those in FTench and Shorthand have now been going on with a very considerable amount of success for two years. Those at Somers Town have only recently sprung into existence.

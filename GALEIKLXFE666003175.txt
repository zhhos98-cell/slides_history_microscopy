SIXTEENTH REPORT,OF THE &lt;span class=&quot;hitHighlite&quot;&gt;WORKING&lt;/span&gt; &lt;span class=&quot;hitHighlite&quot;&gt;MEN&rsquo;S&lt;/span&gt; &lt;span class=&quot;hitHighlite&quot;&gt;COLLEGE&lt;/span&gt;. November , 1891, to 31&iacute;/ December , 1892. - ж - It has been thought expedient that the Report, now annually presented to the Council by the Executive Committee, should henceforward form part of a Calendar which should bring together in a more compact form than heretofore the official record and statistics of the College work, and, having in view our possible future connexion with a London University, should supply such information for the guidance of the students as may be from time to time desirable. It is proposed that the Calendar should appear in the January term, and that the Report should deal with each preceding year ending on the 31st of December. The period between the issue of the last Report in November, 1891, and the end of 1892 offers a retrospect that is in most points satisfactory. The numbers that were given approximately for the October term 1891, viz : class entries q66, and individual students 755, ultimately reached the respective figures of 1015 and 785. In the October term of 1892 there was a slight increase, the figures being 1030 and 789 respectively. If a comparison is made between the totals of class entries year by year (beginning in October, but omitting the short Midsummer terms), it will be found that there has been a steady rise. In 1888-89 the total was 2089 ; in 1889-90, a year comprising the fullest October term in our annals, it was 2310; in 1890-91, 2328; in 1891-92,2370;&mdash;figures which show that notwithstanding fluctuations in the number of individual students, the amount of work done in the classes has some regular tendency to expand. The percentage of new students has become somewhat smaller, so that on the average the College retains its students for a longer period and thereby gains for its leaching and training a fuller opportunity ot producing a durable effect. I he Appendix gives statistics and other particulars as to the numbers, ages, employments, &amp;amp;c., of the students, and their distribution among the Divisions and Classes. A list of the honours and certifi&not; cates granted will be found at p. 42. The educational work of the College appears to be on the whole a matter for congratulation. 152 certificates have been granted
since January. 1892, as compared with 100 in 1891, and the range of subjects for which they were granted has been appreciably higher. The Scheme of College studies and examinations was last year referred to a sub-committee who, after enquiry among the teachers and a careful consideration of the subject, presented a report which was with slight modifications adopted by the Committee. Most of the changes recommended appear in the General Prospectus and the Term programme, where a re-arrangement of the Divisions and Classes has been made. Some extracts from the report itself are printed in the Calendar (p. 36). It will be seen that First stage as well as Advanced stage examinations are prescribed in most of the subjects taught ; and that it is in contemplation to maintain per&not; manently all such classes as are requisite for the Matriculation ex&not; aminations of the University of London. Some suggestions regard&not; ing courses leading to these Examinations&#39;will be found at p. 38. With regard to these classes the sub-committee, whilst fully recognizing the great value of our volunteer teaching, recommend that where volunteer teachers cannot be obtained, paid teachers shall be provided so far as practicable, rather than that the class should be allowed to drop out of the programme. For detailed accounts of the work in various classes, the appended reports furnished by Directors and Teachers may be consulted. Under the Charity Commissioners&rsquo; Scheme of 9th May, 1891, there are now 8 classes in the College for Men and Women, and 25 classes in this College, which are open to the students of either college. The proposed establishment of a Teaching University for London, either by making large and fundamental changes in the present University of London, or by creating a second university side by side with the old one, has been and still remains a question of much moment to the College, and a short recapitulation of the steps that have been taken with regard to it may not be considered out of place. A draft of a Charter creating a new university in London, tobe composed of King&rsquo;s College, University College, and the medical schools of the ten chief Metropolitan hospitals, received the sanction of the Privy Council in July, 1891. A few months later the scheme was modified by the inclusion of Gresham College as a constituent body, and by the alteration of the name cf the intended University from the &ldquo;Albert&rdquo; to the &ldquo;Gresham.&rdquo; I he Charter however had been framed on too narrow a basis and was received with disapprobation in many quarters and on various grounds. As it could not become valid without being laid before both Houses of Parliament for a certain period, advantage was taken of the delay to organize an opposition to the scheme. In December, 1891, a meeting was held at the Birkbeek of representatives of that Institute, the City of London College, and our own College, with the result that a Committee, which subsequently included representatives of Bedford College and of the London Society for the Extension of University Teaching, was formed for the purposes of resisting, on
educational grounds, the confirmation of the Charter, and of endeavouring to obtain for the institutions represented on the Committee adequate recognition by any Teaching University which might ultimately be established for the Metropolis. With these objects in view, a public meeting was held in the London Chamber of Commerce under the presidency of Sir John Lubbock in February, 1892. A Counter-Memorial, in reply to a Memorial published by some of the supporters of the Gresham Charter, was drawn up and sent round to Members of Parliament and the leading newspapers. The Committee also took part in a deputa&not; tion formed by delegates from the University of London, the Victoria University, and the educational bodies, which was received on March 3rd by Lord Salisbury, and at which Prebendary Whittington, Principal of the City of London College (in the unavoidable absence of Sir J. Lubbock) submitted the claims of the Evening Institutions. Petitions to the House of Commons, were sent in by each of the five bodies represented on the Committee.* The question came before the House on March 10th, and Mr. Balfour announced that the draft Charter, as well as the general question of establishing a Teaching University in London, would be referred to a new Royal Commission. This decision involved the rejection of the draft Charter as it stood ; and the new Royal Commission, which was shortly after&not; wards appointed, is still engaged in conducting, under the presidency of Lord Cowper, a prolonged enquiry into the matters referred to it The three Evening Institutions, having been thus far successful, continued to work in concert, and presented to the Commissioners a joint Memorialt setting forth their claims. Each Institution also sent in an Addendum I to the joint Memorial, relating to its own special circumstances, and gave oral evidence through its represen&not; tatives. Evidence for this College was given last October by the Principal, Vice-Principal, and Secretary. One indirect result of these efforts in common has been that a mutual arrangement is now on foot between the Birkbeck and this College providing that Students from the Birkbeck may attend classes here on payment ot the class fee only, without the term fee, whilst our students may attend classes there on the reduced scale, without paying the Membership fee ; and also providing that a representative of the Birkbeck shall sit on our Council, and a representative of this College on the Birkbeck educational com&not; mittee. The reports and prospectuses of the two institutions are to be interchanged. With regard to finance, it will be seen from the annexed accounts that, as was anticipated in the Report for 1891, the ordinary work of the College has been carried on without pecuniary difficulties. It is still, however, far beyond our means to take into use the house next door, which belongs to the College but is in the occupation of a tenant. APPENDIX TO REPORT.,SHORT REPORTS FROM DIRECTORS OF STUDIES AND TEACHERS.,PREPARATORY DIVISION. Mr. Tansley, Director, reports that the numbers for the October term this year show a fair increase, being 125 for 1892, against 112 for 1891. There has been a marked improvement in the quality of the work done, as tested by the December examination. Of 30 men attending the examination, 3 obtained over 90 per cent, of full marks, two gaining scholarships, by which they are enabled to attend two classes in the Lower Division free. The system of giving home work has this year been extended to all the Classes, and although many of the students have great difficulty in finding time to w&#39;ork out of Class, a fair proportion give in weekly papers. A Practice Class has been instituted, under the direction of Mr. Cole, at which help can be obtained with the more difficult of the home-work questions by those who require it. Mr. Tansley adds, &ldquo; The College owes its best thanks to the teachers of the Division, who one and all give the most careful attention to the w&rsquo;ork, and I have great pleasure in recording that the Committee have appointed Mr. Cole to be Assistant Director.
